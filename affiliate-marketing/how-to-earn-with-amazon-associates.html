How to Earn with Amazon Associates in 2026

Amazon Associates is the easiest affiliate programme to join, and one of the hardest to earn well from. That gap is the problem this guide solves. Joining takes minutes, but earning takes a clear method: a niche that pays, content that answers a buying question, links that follow the rules, and a plan to get three real sales in your first 180 days. Without that plan, many new associates lose the account before they earn anything. With it, you can build a small, honest income stream that grows slowly over time.

What Amazon Associates is, in plain words

Amazon Associates is Amazon's affiliate programme, which is free to join. An affiliate programme is a scheme where a retailer pays you a commission when a reader clicks your special link and buys a product. A commission is a small share of the sale price, paid to you for sending the buyer. You do not sell the product, hold stock, or handle delivery. Amazon does that. Your job is to help a reader decide, and to link them to the product page.

When you join, Amazon gives you a tracking ID, which is a short code that identifies you as the referrer. It also gives you a Store ID, which is the name for your Associates account. You add the tracking ID to your links, so Amazon can tell which sales came from you. When a reader clicks your link and buys a qualifying product, Amazon records the sale, and pays you a commission after the product ships and is paid for, and after the payment timing in your account terms.

Amazon Associates is not a job, and it is not a salary. It is a commission scheme, and income varies by niche, traffic, product price, and how well your content matches a buying question. Some associates earn a small sum each month, and many earn nothing for the first months. That is normal, and this guide is honest about it.

How it actually works

The chain has five steps, and each step has a rule you must know. First, a reader clicks your Special Link, which is Amazon's name for a link that carries your tracking ID. Second, a session starts, which is the period after the click during which a sale still counts as yours. Third, the reader buys a qualifying product, which is a product that earns commission under the programme terms. Fourth, the product ships and is paid for. Fifth, Amazon pays you, about 60 days after the month in which the sale was earned, as Amazon's own Associates Program Policies explain, as updated on 14 April 2026.

The session rule is the most important, and the most misunderstood. Amazon's policies say a qualifying purchase needs a click through a Special Link, and a session that ends at 24 hours, an order, or another associate's link. That 24-hour rule is called the cookie window, and it means a reader who buys within 24 hours of clicking your link counts as your sale. If the reader adds a product to their cart within that 24 hours, and orders it within 89 days of the click, that cart item can still count, under the 89-day cart rule. That is the figure Amazon states in its policies, so this guide uses 89 days, not 90 days, even though some guides say 90 days.

The whole-cart rule is the second point to understand. When a reader clicks your link and buys, you can earn commission on the whole cart, not only on the product you linked to, if the other items are qualifying purchases and the session rules are met. That is why a link to a low price product can still earn a useful commission, if the reader buys other items in the same session. It is also why a review of one product can earn from a different product in the same cart, and why the rate alone does not tell the full story.

A purchase is disqualified if the product is returned, if you bought it through your own link, if the link was redirected or cloaked to hide the destination, or if the sale came from prohibited paid search or another violation. The product must also be shipped and paid for within 180 days of the click, or the sale does not count. Those rules are in Amazon's policies, and they are the rules your account is judged by, not a summary in a blog post.

What it pays

Commission rates differ by product category, and they change. That is the honest starting point, and it is why this guide does not present a rate table as current fact. The three guides that rank for this topic disagree with each other. One says rates run from 1 percent to 10 percent. Another says the top rate is 20 percent, with Amazon Games at 20 percent, and Luxury Beauty and Furniture at 10 percent. A third says rates run from 1 percent to 10 percent, with a similar range. Those tables cannot all be right at the same time, and a rate you read in a blog post may be old, or may apply to a different country.

Treat any rate in this guide as a snapshot dated November 2026, and check the live rate in your own account. The pattern is consistent across sources, even if the exact figures differ: some categories pay a low rate, often around 1 percent to 3 percent, while others pay a higher rate, often around 4 percent to 10 percent, with a small number of categories higher still. Electronics and groceries are usually at the low end. Home, beauty, and some other categories are usually higher. That pattern is useful for choosing a niche, but the exact rate for a product today is only in your Associates dashboard, in the Commission Income Statement, which is Amazon's own report of what each category pays.

The rate is not the whole story. Three other factors matter as much as the rate. First, order value, which is the price of the product. A 3 percent rate on a 500 dollar product earns more than a 10 percent rate on a 10 dollar product. Second, whole-cart buying, which means a reader may buy more than the product you linked to. Third, bounty events, which are fixed payments for a specific action, such as a reader signing up for a trial or a service through your link, where the programme offers one. Bounty rates also change, so check them in your own account.

Example only: these numbers are not typical results, and the rates below are a November 2026 snapshot, not a promise. Imagine a reader clicks your link to a 100 dollar product in a category that pays 4 percent, and buys that product plus a 50 dollar item in the same session. The commission on the first product is 4 dollars, and the commission on the second product depends on its own category rate. If the second product also pays 4 percent, the commission is 2 dollars, and the total is 6 dollars. That example shows why order value and whole-cart buying matter, and why a single rate figure can mislead you.

Who can join and what you need first

Anyone who meets Amazon's requirements can apply, but you need content first. Amazon asks you to list the website, blog, channel, app, or social account where you will place links, and it checks that the place has recent, original content. A new site with one thin post, or a social profile with no posts, is likely to be turned down. Build the content first, then apply.

You need a website, blog, channel, app, or social account with recent original content that matches the products you plan to promote. You need an about page and a privacy page on a site, if you apply with a site, because Amazon checks that the site is real and that it explains who runs it. You need a clear disclosure plan, because Amazon requires you to disclose that you earn from qualifying purchases, in the words Amazon gives, which this guide explains below. And you need to be in a country where the programme operates, because each Amazon country is a separate programme, as explained in the country section below.

You do not need a large audience to apply. You do need a real audience, or a real chance of one, shown by content that answers a question. A blog with ten useful guides, a channel with ten useful videos, or a social profile with regular, helpful posts is a stronger application than a site with 100 thin pages copied from product descriptions. Amazon's own participation requirements also ask for original content, not copied product descriptions, which is the same advice Google gives in its guide on creating helpful, reliable, people-first content.

How to sign up, step by step

Sign up on the Associates site for your country, not on a general Amazon shopping page. The steps below are the standard flow, but the screens change, so follow the prompts in your own account if they differ.

Step 1: open the Amazon Associates site for your country, and sign in with your Amazon account, or create one. Step 2: enter your account details, including your name, address, and contact details. Step 3: list your sites, apps, or social profiles, with their real addresses, and describe what each one is about. Step 4: choose your Store ID, which is the name for your Associates account, and set your tracking ID. Step 5: explain how you will drive traffic, in plain words, such as "a blog with buyer guides" or "a YouTube channel with product reviews". Step 6: enter your tax details and your payment details, as Amazon asks, and check them twice, because a wrong detail can delay payment. Step 7: read the Operating Agreement and the Program Policies, and accept them. That last step matters, because those documents are the rules your account is judged by.

You do not pay to join. If a site asks you to pay to join Amazon Associates, it is not Amazon. Join only through the official Associates site for your country.

The 180-day rule

The 180-day rule is the key beginner trap, and it is the rule that closes most new accounts. After you are approved, you have 180 days to make 3 qualifying sales, which are sales that meet the programme rules, through your Special Links. If you do not make 3 qualifying sales in 180 days, Amazon can close the account, and you will need to apply again. Your own orders do not count. Orders from friends or family, made to help you, do not count as qualifying sales if they break the rules, and you must not ask people to buy through your link to help you reach the target.

Plan for the rule before you apply. Do not apply on the day you publish your first post. Publish five to ten useful guides first, with links in place, and check that the links work and that the content answers a buying question. Then apply, so the 180 days start when you already have content that can bring clicks. The first-90-days plan at the end of this guide gives you a week-by-week order for that work.

If your account is closed for missing the target, you can apply again when you have more content and more traffic. A rejection is not a ban for life, but a second application with the same thin content is likely to fail again. Fix the content first, then reapply.

Building links

Amazon gives you three main ways to build a link, and each one has a place. SiteStripe is a toolbar that appears at the top of an Amazon page when you are signed in as an associate on a desktop browser. It lets you create a link for the product you are viewing, with your tracking ID already in it. GetLink is the mobile version, for building a link from your phone. Both are simple, and they are the best way to build a link for a single product in a post or a video description.

Use a tracking ID per site or campaign, where Amazon allows it, so you can tell which site or which post earned. A tracking ID is a short code, such as a store name plus a suffix, that you add to the link. For example, one ID for your blog, one for your channel, and one for a specific campaign, if you run one. Note each ID in your own sheet, so you know what each one means. For the tracking system in detail, read how to track affiliate earnings.

The Product Advertising API, which is Amazon's tool for pulling product data into a site automatically, is only available after qualifying sales, and only to associates who meet Amazon's requirements for it. Do not plan your site around the API at the start. Build links by hand with SiteStripe or GetLink, and add the API later, if you qualify and if you need it.

Do not cloak an Amazon link. Cloaking means showing a short link that hides the real destination, and Amazon's policies do not allow it where it hides the destination. Use the full link Amazon gives you, or a link shortener that shows the destination clearly, if the programme allows it. Check the policies in your own account before you use any link tool.

How people actually earn

People earn with Amazon Associates by answering a buying question, not by listing products. Four content formats work best, and each one suits a different question. A buyer guide answers "what should I buy for this need", with a shortlist, a winner for each type of user, and a clear price. A single product review answers "is this product worth it", with real use, drawbacks, and a verdict. A comparison answers "which one should I choose", with the same criteria for each option, and a winner per user type. An informational article answers a question, such as "how do I do this task", and links to the tool that does the job, where it fits naturally.

Each format needs the same foundation: a real question, a clear verdict, a price checked today, and a disclosure. A buyer guide that lists ten products with no verdict is a catalogue, not a guide. A review that repeats the product page is not a review. For the review method in detail, read how to write affiliate product reviews.

Traffic is the other half of earning. A guide with no readers earns nothing, however well it is written. Search traffic from Google, video traffic from YouTube, and social traffic from Pinterest can all bring readers, but each one takes time to build. Do not expect a new guide to rank in its first week. A new post may take weeks to appear in search, and months to reach page one, if it reaches page one at all. That is normal, and it is why the first-90-days plan below starts with content, not with links.

Choosing a niche that pays

A niche is a focused topic, such as home office gear, beginner photography, or budget cooking tools. Choose a niche on four factors, not on the rate alone. First, category rate, because a low rate on a low price product earns very little per sale. Second, order value, because a higher price can earn more, even at a low rate. Third, return rate, because a product that is often returned loses its commission when the return is processed. Fourth, honest interest, because you will write about this topic for months, and a niche you do not care about will show in your writing.

Avoid a niche where every product is low price and low rate, unless you can bring a large volume of buyers, which a new site cannot. Avoid a niche where returns are high, such as fashion or electronics with complex setup, unless you understand the return risk and warn the reader about it. And avoid a niche where you cannot test the product, or study it honestly, because a review without evidence will not earn trust.

A good test is to list five products in the niche, check the price of each, note the category rate from your dashboard, and work out the commission per sale for each one. If the best product in the niche earns only a few cents per sale, the niche will need a very large volume to pay. If the best product earns a few dollars per sale, and the products are ones people buy with other items in the same cart, the niche has a better chance. For the niche method in detail, read how to choose an affiliate niche.

The country problem

Each Amazon country is a separate programme, with its own sign-up, its own rates, and its own rules. An account for Amazon.com in the United States does not cover Amazon.co.uk in the United Kingdom, or Amazon.de in Germany, or Amazon.in in India. If your audience is in another country, you need to join that country's programme separately, or use a link-localisation tool, which is a tool that sends each reader to the Amazon store for their own country.

That matters because a reader who clicks a .com link from the United Kingdom may see a product they cannot buy, or a price in a currency they do not use, and they will leave. A reader in your own country, who clicks a link for their own store, is more likely to buy. Check where your audience lives, in your analytics or in your channel data, and join the programme for the country where most of them live first. Then add other countries when you have proof that readers come from there.

Payment methods and thresholds also differ by country. Amazon pays by direct deposit, by gift card, or by check, depending on the country and the account, and the minimum payment threshold differs by method and by country. Some guides quote 10 dollars for direct deposit or gift card, and 100 dollars for a check, but those figures come from other guides, not from the portion of Amazon's policies page checked for this guide, so check the threshold in your own dashboard before you state it to a reader. Do not quote a threshold from a blog post as a fact.

The rules that get accounts closed

Amazon closes accounts for breaking its rules, and the rules are not hidden. They are in the Operating Agreement and the Program Policies, which you accepted when you joined. The most common reasons are easy to avoid, if you know them. Do not hide the disclosure, or put it only at the bottom of a page. Do not cloak a link to hide the destination. Do not use stale prices, or copy Amazon reviews or images, and present them as your own. Do not put links in PDFs, in offline material, or in cold email, which is email to people who did not ask to hear from you. Do not offer an incentive for a click or a purchase, such as a discount or a gift for buying through your link. Do not buy through your own links. Do not use prohibited paid search, which is bidding on Amazon trademarks or other restricted terms in adverts, where the policies forbid it.

Stale prices deserve a special note. A price you saw last month may be out of date today, and a reader who clicks through to a different price will lose trust in your guide, and in Amazon. Check the price on the product page on the day you publish, and write the date beside it. If your site shows a price automatically, check that the price updates, and fix it if it does not. A guide with a stale price is a guide that misleads readers, even if every other sentence is true.

If Amazon warns you about a rule, act at once. Read the warning, fix the page it names, and check your other pages for the same problem. A warning you ignore can become a closed account. Keep a note of each warning, and what you fixed, so you can show that you acted on it.

Costs, time, and realistic expectations

Joining Amazon Associates is free. The costs are the same as for any content site: hosting, a domain, an email tool, and your time. A single buyer guide can take four to eight hours to research, test, write, and publish. A review can take a similar time, if you test the product properly. Those hours are the real cost, and they add up over a month.

Expect months before steady income, and expect nothing at first. Most new sites earn nothing for the first months, while search traffic builds and while the first guides find readers. A first sale in the first 90 days is a realistic goal, not a promise, and it depends on the niche, the traffic, and the quality of the content. Results vary by experience, location, demand, time, competition, expenses, and platform fees, and no honest guide can promise what you will earn.

Do not repeat a salary figure as affiliate income. One guide that ranks for this topic presents a jobs site salary figure as affiliate earnings, and that figure is a job salary, not typical affiliate income. It must not be repeated as what an associate earns. Your own income will depend on your own traffic and your own content, not on a salary survey.

Common mistakes

  • Building only on low rate, low price products, so each sale earns a few cents, and volume never makes up for it.
  • Ignoring the 24-hour window, and expecting a sale days later to count, when the session has already ended.
  • Skipping the disclosure, or hiding it at the bottom of a page, where readers will not see it before they click.
  • Forgetting returns, and counting a sale as earned before the product ships, is paid for, and stays bought.
  • Trusting a blog rate table, instead of checking the live rate in your own Associates dashboard.
  • Applying before you have content, so the 180 days start with nothing that can bring clicks.
  • Copying Amazon reviews or images, instead of writing original content with your own evidence.
  • Putting links in PDFs, cold email, or offline material, where Amazon's policies forbid them.
  • Buying through your own links, which is disqualified, and can put your account at risk.

A first-90-days plan

Use the first 90 days to build content, apply, publish buyer guides, get three real sales, and review the dashboard. Do not try to earn in week one. Follow this order.

Days 1 to 30: build content first. Choose a niche, publish five useful guides, and check that each one answers a buying question, with a clear verdict and a price checked that day. Add an about page, a privacy page, and a disclosure page. Do not apply yet.

Days 31 to 45: apply to Amazon Associates, with your site or channel as the traffic source. While you wait, publish two more guides, and build your link map, with a tracking ID for each site or channel.

Days 46 to 75: publish five more buyer guides, reviews, or comparisons, with links built by SiteStripe or GetLink. Check each link from a phone, and note the date you checked the price. Share each guide once, on the channel where your readers already are, and do not spam it in many places.

Days 76 to 90: review the dashboard. Note which guides brought clicks, which links brought sales, and which products were returned. Fix any broken link, update any stale price, and make one decision: which guide deserves a follow-up, and which product deserves a deeper review. If you have not yet made three qualifying sales, do not panic. Keep publishing, and keep the content useful, because the 180 days give you more time than the first 90 days alone.

Frequently asked questions

There is no typical figure, because income depends on the niche, the product price, the category rate, the traffic, and how well the content matches a buying question. Conclusion: build content first, then apply, then earn slowly

Amazon Associates rewards a simple order: build useful content first, apply with a real site or channel, publish buyer guides that answer a real question, check the price on the day you publish, disclose clearly, and follow the rules in your own account. The 24-hour session, the 89-day cart rule, the 180-day target of three qualifying sales, and payment about 60 days after the month earned are the four numbers to remember, and the live rate in your dashboard is the only rate to trust.
