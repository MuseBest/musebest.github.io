How to Earn with Online Course Affiliate Programs in 2026

Learning how to earn with online course affiliate programs is a natural fit if you already teach, blog, or make videos about a skill. You do not need to create a course yourself. You recommend courses and learning platforms made by other people, and you earn a commission when someone buys through your link. It suits anyone who already answers questions such as "where can I learn data analysis" or "which design course is worth it", because the recommendation appears at the exact moment someone needs to learn something.

This guide is for beginners who blog, teach, podcast, or run a newsletter about a skill, a career change, or a hobby. You will learn how course affiliate programs work, which platforms pay what, how to choose programs that fit your audience, and what content actually converts.

How online course affiliate programs work

An online course affiliate program, also called an e-learning affiliate program, is a partnership. You join the program, you get a unique tracking link, and you share it in content that helps people choose a course. When a reader clicks your link and buys a course, a certificate, or a learning subscription, you earn a commission. A commission is the payment you earn for a sale you referred.

Most large programs run through affiliate networks such as Impact, Awin, CJ or PartnerStack, which handle tracking, reporting and payments. You apply through the network, and the network pays you, usually monthly, once your balance passes a minimum threshold. Coursera, Skillshare and edX run their programs on Impact. Udemy runs its program on Impact too, and you apply through its own affiliates page.

Tracking works through a small file called a cookie, which is stored in the visitor's browser when they click your link. A cookie window is the number of days after the click during which a purchase still counts as yours. Course buyers often research for weeks before they enrol, especially for expensive certificates, so a longer cookie helps. edX offers a 60 day cookie, and course platforms such as Thinkific and Teachable offer 90 days. Udemy's cookie is only 7 days, which is one of the shortest in the niche, so it suits readers who are ready to buy now.

Commission models vary more than most people expect, and the model often matters more than the headline percentage. A percentage per sale pays a share of each course sold. A first payment only model pays on the first month or first year of a subscription, then stops. A recurring model pays a share of every payment while the customer stays subscribed. A tiered model pays higher rates once you pass a sales threshold. A flat fee model pays a fixed amount per sale or signup. Low course prices can mean small payouts even at a high percentage, so always work out the dollars per sale, not just the rate.

Course marketplaces vs course platforms

Course affiliate programs fall into two groups, and they suit different audiences. Do not mix them up, because promoting the wrong group to your audience wastes your traffic.

Group What you promote Who it suits Example programs
Course marketplaces Courses and certificates for learners Bloggers, teachers and creators whose audience wants to learn a skill Coursera, Udemy, Skillshare, edX, DataCamp, MasterClass
Course platforms Software for people who want to create and sell their own courses Bloggers and creators whose audience wants to build a course business Thinkific, Kajabi, Teachable, Podia

Marketplaces pay per course sale or per new membership. Platforms pay a recurring share of the creator's software subscription, often 30 percent, sometimes for the customer's lifetime. Platform commissions can be larger per referral, because a software subscription costs more than one course, but they only convert if your audience wants to create courses. If your readers want to learn a skill, promote marketplaces. If your readers want to teach a skill, promote platforms. For the wider method, read how to choose a profitable affiliate niche.

Step 1: Pick the learner audience first

The best program is the one your audience already wants. A 45 percent commission on a course nobody in your audience needs earns nothing, while 10 percent on a course they are already searching for adds up.

Start with who reads, watches or listens to you. Professionals building careers suit Coursera, edX, and exam prep platforms, because recognised certificates and high stakes outcomes matter to them. Tech and data learners suit DataCamp, Pluralsight and Codecademy, because those brands have structured learning paths. Creatives and hobbyists suit Skillshare, Domestika and CreativeLive, because the classes are project based and cheap to start. A broad general audience suits Udemy and MasterClass, because the catalogues cover almost everything. Language learners suit Rocket Languages, Babbel and Rosetta Stone. Workplace and compliance buyers suit 360training and GoSkills, because the training is required for a licence or a job.

Write down your audience in one sentence before you apply anywhere. For example: "My readers are career changers who want a data analysis certificate they can show an employer." That sentence decides which programs fit and which courses to feature. If you cannot write that sentence yet, build the audience first.

Step 2: Shortlist two or three programs

Compare programs on commission type, cookie length, payout terms, and how much buyers already trust the brand. Fit matters more than the headline rate. A note on Udemy, because the guides disagree. Several affiliate guides report a 10 percent base commission, with higher rates for top performers. But Udemy itself does not publish a commission rate. Its official affiliates page says only that it offers competitive rates, and the rate is set inside your Impact publisher account after approval. The Udemy affiliates program page confirms the program runs on Impact. Do not quote 10 percent as an official figure. Treat it as reported by guides, and check your own account for the real rate.

Also note the price problem. Udemy courses are frequently discounted to 10 to 20 US dollars, which helps conversions but keeps payouts per sale low. At a reported 10 percent, a 15 dollar course earns about 1.50 US dollars. Coursera certificates cost far more, so the same 15 percent can earn much more per sale. Always work out the dollars, not just the percentage.

Check the Impact network page if you are new to affiliate networks, since most course programs run through it.

Step 3: Apply and meet the traffic bar

Most course programs review your site or channel before approving you. Some prefer established creators with engaged audiences. Others, such as Skillshare, are open to anyone with an aligned audience. A focused site with useful content improves your chances everywhere.

Udemy sets the bar in writing: you need at least 500 unique monthly visitors over the last 3 months, or at least 500 followers on a social media page, plus an audience that fits its courses. Applications are reviewed in 3 to 4 business days. You also need a tax document approved on Impact before your first payout, and you need a 10 US dollar balance to withdraw.

Before you apply anywhere, have these in place: a live website or channel with real published content about learning or your skill, a visible affiliate disclosure, and a contact page. Do not apply with a site that only talks about one course platform, and do not apply with paid advertising as your only traffic source, because some programs reject those applications. Step 4: Publish reviews, comparisons and tutorials

Affiliate links in a sidebar rarely earn much. Courses sell when a recommendation appears at the moment someone needs to learn something, from a person they trust. Five formats work best.

Honest reviews work well. Share what you liked, what you did not like, and who the course suits. Take the course yourself when you can, or at least review the syllabus, the instructor, and student reviews. Some programs offer partners free trials or temporary access, such as Rocket Languages, which gives partners 30 days of full course access.

Comparison posts work well. Compare two or three platforms on price, quality, certificate value, and fit for one audience. High intent searches such as "Coursera vs edX" or "best Python course for beginners" attract readers who are close to enrolling.

How-to tutorials work well. Teach part of a skill, then recommend a course to go further. A reader who just learned the basics from you is ready to hear which course continues the journey.

Email sequences work well for expensive courses. Recommend a course after several emails of useful advice, not in the first email. Expensive courses need nurturing, because learners research for weeks before they pay.

YouTube and podcasts work well for visual or audio audiences. Walk through a course, interview the instructor, or show your own learning project. For video basics, read how to start a YouTube channel.

Publish five to ten pieces in your skill area before you judge the results. Link them to each other so readers can move from a tutorial to a comparison to a review. For the writing craft, read how to write affiliate product reviews.

Step 5: Track and keep terms current

Use the affiliate dashboard to track clicks, conversion rate, and earnings per course. Note which courses convert and which ones send clicks but no sales. A course with a high rate but a low conversion rate can earn less than a modest course that converts well.

Course terms change often. Prices are discounted without notice, certificates are added or removed, and programs change their commission rates. Check each program's own page every few months, and update your comparison tables the same week a term changes. Add a note with the date you last checked the figures, so readers know how fresh the information is.

Watch out for one more trap: promoting a course that competes directly with your own course or coaching. Affiliate income works best as a complement to what you already do. If you teach a skill yourself, recommend courses that fill a gap before or after your own offer, not courses that replace it.

Worked example: what a small learning blog could earn

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. They show how the maths works across cheap and expensive courses.

Imagine a small blog about career change that gets 6,000 visitors a month. About 20 percent of them, or 1,200 people, read a course article. Of those, about 4 percent click an affiliate link, which is 48 clicks.

Now split those clicks. Twenty four clicks go to Udemy courses priced at 15 US dollars, and 5 percent of clicks convert, which is about 1 sale. At a reported 10 percent, that sale earns about 1.50 US dollars. Twenty four clicks go to Coursera certificates priced at 49 US dollars, and 5 percent convert, which is about 1 sale. At 15 percent, that sale earns about 7.35 US dollars. The two sales together earn about 8.85 US dollars that month.

Now imagine the same blog a year later, with 30,000 visitors a month and 15 course articles instead of 3. The same maths gives 6,000 course readers, 240 clicks, about 12 sales, and roughly 53 US dollars a month. Add one high ticket certificate sale, such as a professional certificate at 300 US dollars earning 45 percent, and one sale adds 135 US dollars. The lesson is simple: cheap courses need volume, while expensive certificates need far fewer sales to earn the same money.

Recheck: 6,000 times 20 percent is 1,200. 1,200 times 4 percent is 48. 24 times 5 percent is 1.2, rounded to 1 sale each. 10 percent of 15 is 1.50. 15 percent of 49 is 7.35. 1.50 plus 7.35 is 8.85. 45 percent of 300 is 135. The maths checks out as an illustration, not a promise. Your real result depends on traffic, prices, discounting, conversion rates, and refund rates.

What it costs and what to expect

Starting costs are low. A domain costs about 10 to 15 US dollars a year, and hosting costs about 35 to 60 US dollars in the first year. Course affiliate programs are free to join. You may want to buy one or two courses to review them properly, which costs 15 to 300 US dollars depending on the course, though many platforms offer free trials or free audit options. Your main cost is time: expect 5 to 10 hours per useful article or video, plus the time to take a course before you review it.

Expect a slow start. Course buyers research for weeks, and your first commission often arrives a month after the sale because of payout holds and minimum thresholds. Most beginners see their first payout after three to six months of steady publishing. Meaningful income takes a year or more of accumulated content, because cheap courses pay only a few dollars per sale and need volume. Results vary with traffic, content quality, program terms, and how well the courses fit your audience. Nothing here is a promise of income.

Common mistakes

  • Promoting courses your audience does not need, just because the commission looks high.
  • Quoting a commission rate that the program itself does not publish, such as Udemy's rate, as if it were official.
  • Ignoring the cookie window, then wondering why a 7 day cookie misses slow buyers.
  • Linking to a course homepage instead of the specific course you reviewed, which converts worse.
  • Promoting a course you have not checked, with no idea whether the syllabus or instructor is any good.
  • Writing only best course lists, with no tutorials or reviews that help readers decide.
  • Forgetting to disclose the affiliate relationship near the link.
  • Promoting a course that competes directly with your own course or coaching.

Risks and scams to watch for

Never pay to join a course affiliate program. Genuine programs are free, and any program that asks for a joining fee is a warning sign.

Watch out for low quality courses sold with big promises. Some courses promise a job, a certificate that employers do not recognise, or income after one course. Check the syllabus, the instructor's background, student reviews, and whether the certificate comes from a recognised institution before you recommend it. If you cannot verify the quality, say so rather than repeating the marketing claims.

Be careful with fake course review sites that copy each other's commission figures. Rates go out of date fast, and several guides list different figures for the same program. Check the program's own page and note the date you checked it. For disclosure rules, the FTC endorsement guides explain when and how to disclose a paid recommendation.

Finally, protect your readers from overspending. A learner does not need five courses on one skill. Recommend one good course for their level, explain who it suits, and say when a free option is enough. For the basics of honest promotion, read how to promote affiliate products.

Frequently asked questions

Which online course affiliate program pays the most?

Tiered and recurring programs usually pay the most over time. Rocket Languages pays up to 60 percent for top partners, Master of Project Academy rises to 40 percent above 5,000 US dollars in monthly sales, and course platforms such as Thinkific and Kajabi pay 30 percent recurring. What you earn also depends on course prices and conversion rates, so a high percentage on a cheap course can pay less than a lower percentage on an expensive certificate.

Do I need to buy a course to promote it as an affiliate?

No, but firsthand experience makes your recommendation more credible and usually improves conversions. At minimum, review the syllabus, the instructor, and student reviews. Some programs offer partners free trials or temporary access.

Do e-learning affiliate programs require approval?

Most do. Programs on networks such as Impact, Awin and CJ review your site or channel before approving you, and some prefer established creators with engaged audiences. Others, such as Skillshare, are open to anyone with an aligned audience. A focused site with useful content improves your chances.

Can I use affiliate links in YouTube videos and email newsletters?

Yes, most programs allow affiliate links in videos, podcasts and newsletters, though some restrict coupon sites, paid search, or brand name ads. Always read each program's terms, and disclose that you earn a commission clearly and close to the link.

What is the difference between recurring and one-time commissions?

One-time commissions pay once per sale, or on the first subscription payment only. Recurring commissions keep paying for as long as the customer stays subscribed, sometimes for life, as with Thinkific and Kajabi. Recurring programs earn less upfront but can build steady income from referrals who stay.

How do I choose the best e-learning affiliate program for my niche?

Start with the topics your audience already asks about, then shortlist reputable platforms with strong courses in those areas. Compare commission type, cookie length and payout terms, check course quality yourself, and favour programs whose brand your audience already trusts. Fit matters more than the headline rate.

Conclusion

To earn with online course affiliate programs, pick the learner audience first, shortlist two or three programs that fit them, meet the traffic bar before you apply, then publish reviews, comparisons and tutorials that help learners choose. Remember that cheap courses need volume while expensive certificates pay more per sale, and that some rates, such as Udemy's, are reported by guides rather than published by the program. Check the current terms, disclose every link, and keep your tables current.
