How to Earn with Web Hosting Affiliate Programs in 2026

Learning how to earn with web hosting affiliate programs is a sensible first step into affiliate marketing, because every new website needs hosting. Bloggers, small businesses, online stores and freelancers all pay for hosting, and hosting companies pay affiliates for sending them those customers. This guide shows you how the programs work, which ones pay what, and how to promote them honestly, without promising income you cannot guarantee.

This guide is for beginners who already write, teach or make videos about blogging, WordPress or starting a website. You will learn how hosting affiliate programs work, how to compare them, how to join, what content earns clicks, and what a realistic first year looks like.

How web hosting affiliate programs work

A web hosting affiliate program is a partnership. You join the program, you get a unique tracking link, and you place that link in content that helps people choose hosting. When a reader clicks your link and buys an eligible hosting plan, the hosting company pays you a commission. A commission is the payment you earn for a sale you referred.

The process has five parts. First, you choose a hosting company with an affiliate program. Second, you apply and get approved. Third, you receive your tracking link and marketing materials. Fourth, you publish useful content that attracts people who need hosting. Fifth, you earn a commission on each qualifying purchase that is tracked back to your link.

Tracking works through a small file called a cookie, which is stored in the visitor's browser when they click your link. A cookie window, also called a cookie duration, is the number of days after the click during which a purchase still counts as yours. Bluehost uses a 30 day cookie, and SiteGround uses a 60 day cookie, according to their official affiliate pages checked in November 2026. A longer window helps, because people often research hosting for days before they buy.

Most hosting programs pay in one of three ways. A flat commission pays a fixed amount per sale, such as 65 US dollars. A percentage commission pays a share of the sale price, such as 40 percent. A recurring commission pays you a share of each payment while the customer stays subscribed. Some programs mix these, for example a flat payment plus a small recurring share. Check the current terms on each program's own page before you quote a figure, because rates change.

Step 1: Pick the audience first

The biggest mistake new hosting affiliates make is picking the program with the biggest payout, then looking for an audience. Do it the other way round. Ask who already reads your content, and what kind of hosting they need.

A blog for new bloggers suits budget shared hosting, because the readers want a cheap first website. A blog for small businesses suits reliable WordPress hosting with good support. A channel for developers suits cloud or VPS hosting, which is hosting with more power and control for bigger projects. An agency audience suits reseller hosting, which lets an agency sell hosting to its own clients under its own brand.

Write down your audience in one sentence before you apply anywhere. For example: "My readers are new bloggers who want to start a WordPress blog for under 60 US dollars a year." That sentence decides which programs fit and which content to write. If you cannot write that sentence yet, build the audience first and come back to hosting affiliates later. For the wider method, read how to start affiliate marketing.

Step 2: Compare programs on six checks

Do not compare programs on the headline commission alone. A 200 dollar payout means nothing if the product converts badly, if buyers cancel during the refund period, or if the program rejects your application. Compare on six checks instead.

Step 3: Apply and set up tracking and payouts

Joining a hosting affiliate program is free. A genuine program never charges you to join, and any program that asks for a joining fee is a warning sign. Read how to spot a programme that charges to join in our beginner programs guide before you apply anywhere.

The application usually asks for your name, email, website or channel address, and how you plan to promote the product. Some programs approve anyone with a live website. Others review your site and traffic first. Approval usually takes one to three days, though some programs take longer.

Before you apply, make sure your site is ready. You need a finished site on a domain you own, a few published articles, a visible affiliate disclosure, and a contact page. Programs reject unfinished sites, sites with no content, and applications from social profiles alone. For the full setup, read how to start a blog.

Once approved, do three things on day one. First, add your payout details in the dashboard, because missing payout details are the most common reason a first payment fails. Second, check the minimum payout threshold, so you know how much you must earn before money arrives. Third, read the program rules on paid ads, coupon sites and email promotion. Most programs ban bidding on their brand name in paid search, and some ban coupon sites outright. Breaking a rule can cancel your commissions.

Step 4: Publish the three content types that convert

Hosting affiliate income comes from content that helps someone who is about to buy hosting. Three content types do most of the work.

First, step by step tutorials. A post such as "How to start a WordPress blog" naturally needs a hosting step, and your affiliate link sits where the reader needs it. Tutorials attract beginners who are ready to buy today. Write the tutorial so it works without the link, then add the link where it helps the reader complete the step.

Second, honest comparison posts. A post such as "Hostinger vs Bluehost" or "Best hosting for a small business website" attracts readers who are deciding between two providers. Compare price, renewal price, speed, support, and who each host suits. Name a winner only if the comparison supports it, and say who should pick the other option. Readers trust a comparison that admits trade-offs. For the writing craft, read how to write affiliate product reviews.

Third, detailed reviews of one host. A good review covers features, real pricing including the renewal price, limits, support, and who the host does not suit. Do not copy the host's own marketing claims. If you have not used the host yourself, say so, and base the review on the official documentation and pricing page.

Publish five to ten useful posts before you judge the results. One post rarely ranks or earns. A small cluster of tutorials, comparisons and reviews, all linked to each other, gives Google and your readers a reason to trust your site. For keyword help, read keyword research for a blog.

Step 5: Track, update and grow

Use the affiliate dashboard to track clicks, conversions and earnings per post. The dashboard shows which articles send clicks and which ones turn clicks into sales. Double down on the content that converts, and rewrite or remove the content that sends clicks but no sales.

Update your posts every few months. Hosting prices, plans and affiliate terms change often. A post that quotes last year's price or an old commission rate loses trust and can mislead readers. Add a note with the date you last checked the figures, and recheck the official affiliate page before each update.

Once two or three posts earn steadily, add a second program for comparison content, and try one more channel, such as YouTube tutorials or an email newsletter. Do not join ten programs at once. Spreading yourself across ten programs teaches you nothing about any of them.

Worked example: what a small hosting blog could earn

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. They show how the maths works, so you can test your own figures.

Imagine a small blog that gets 5,000 visitors a month. About 10 percent of them, or 500 people, read a hosting article. Of those 500, about 5 percent click an affiliate link, which is 25 clicks. If 4 percent of those clicks turn into a qualifying sale, that is 1 sale a month. At 65 US dollars per sale, that is 65 US dollars that month, paid 45 to 70 days later after the refund window.

Now imagine the same blog a year later, with 20,000 visitors a month and 10 hosting articles instead of 3. The same maths gives 2,000 hosting readers, 100 clicks, 4 sales, and 260 US dollars a month. The growth comes from more useful content and more traffic, not from a better commission rate. If the click rate is lower, or if buyers cancel during the refund window, the result is lower. If the blog targets high intent keywords such as "best hosting for WordPress beginners", the conversion rate can be higher, because those readers are close to buying.

Recheck: 5,000 times 10 percent is 500. 500 times 5 percent is 25. 25 times 4 percent is 1. 1 times 65 is 65. 20,000 times 10 percent is 2,000. 2,000 times 5 percent is 100. 100 times 4 percent is 4. 4 times 65 is 260. The maths checks out.

What it costs and what to expect

Starting costs are low. A domain costs about 10 to 15 US dollars a year, and shared hosting costs about 35 to 60 US dollars in the first year. You can start with a free WordPress theme and free plugins. Your main cost is time: expect 5 to 10 hours per useful article, plus time to update older posts.

Expect a slow start. A new blog usually earns nothing from hosting affiliates for three to six months, because it takes time for posts to rank in Google and for readers to trust a new site. Your first sale often comes from a tutorial that ranks for a specific question, not from your homepage. Payouts arrive 30 to 70 days after the sale, because of refund holds and payment processing, so even after your first sale you wait weeks for the money.

Results vary with traffic, content quality, keyword choice, program terms, and how well your audience matches the product. No one can promise you a monthly figure. Treat the first year as building an asset: a library of useful hosting content that can earn for years if you keep it current.

Common mistakes

  • Promoting the highest paying host without checking whether the product suits your readers.
  • Hiding the renewal price, which is often two or three times the first year price.
  • Claiming you tested a host when you only read its marketing page.
  • Publishing a list of ten hosts with no real comparison, which helps no one decide.
  • Stuffing affiliate links into every paragraph instead of placing them where they help.
  • Forgetting to disclose the affiliate relationship near the link.
  • Ignoring the program rules, then losing commissions for banned promotion methods.
  • Quoting old commission figures from copied lists instead of checking the official page.

Risks and scams to watch for

Never pay to join an affiliate program. Genuine hosting affiliate programs are free. If a program asks for a joining fee, a starter kit, or a deposit, walk away.

Watch out for fake hosting review sites that rank hosts by commission rather than quality. If every host on a list is described as the best, the list is advertising, not advice. Compare any recommendation against the host's own pricing page and against independent reviews.

Be careful with paid ads. Most hosting programs ban bidding on their brand name, and some ban paid search entirely. Check the terms before you spend money on ads, or you may lose both the ad spend and the commissions.

Finally, protect your readers. Only recommend hosting you would use yourself, or that you have checked carefully against the official documentation. A reader who buys bad hosting on your advice will not trust your next recommendation. For the basics of honest promotion, read how to promote affiliate products.

Frequently asked questions

How much can you earn from hosting affiliate programs?

It depends on traffic, conversion rate, commission per sale, and the program terms. A small blog might earn nothing for months, then a few sales a month once its tutorials rank. A larger blog with high intent traffic can earn far more. No figure is guaranteed, and refunds can cancel commissions, so treat any earnings claim without a source with care.

Do you need a website to join a hosting affiliate program?

Not always. Some programs accept YouTube channels or social media accounts with a relevant audience. Most programs prefer a website, and some require one. Check the eligibility rules on each program's official page before you apply.

Which hosting affiliate program is best for beginners?

Hostinger and Bluehost are the usual starting points for beginner blogging content, because their products suit new website owners and their terms are clear. SiteGround is a good choice if your readers care about performance and support, and its weekly payouts help small affiliates. The best program is the one that matches your audience, not the one with the biggest headline payout.

Can I promote more than one hosting program?

Yes. Most affiliates promote two or three programs, which lets them compare products honestly and give readers a choice. Follow each program's rules, and do not recommend a host you would not use yourself just because it pays more.

How long does it take to get paid?

Most programs hold commissions for 30 to 60 days while buyers can ask for a refund. Bluehost pays 45 to 70 days after the sale. SiteGround pays weekly once a sale is approved, with no minimum threshold. Check the payout terms in each program dashboard, because they change.

Is web hosting affiliate marketing profitable?

It can be, for publishers who attract readers that are about to buy hosting and who publish honest, useful content. Competition is strong, rankings take months, and earnings are never guaranteed. Treat it as a long term project, not quick income.

Conclusion

To earn with web hosting affiliate programs, pick an audience first, compare programs on commission, cookie, payout terms, refunds, product quality and rules, then publish tutorials, comparisons and honest reviews that help readers choose. Start with two or three programs that fit your readers, disclose every affiliate link, and keep your prices and terms current. Your first sale may take months, but a small library of useful hosting content can earn for years.
