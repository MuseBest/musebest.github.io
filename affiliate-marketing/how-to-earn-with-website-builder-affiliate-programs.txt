<h2 id="how-website-builder-affiliate-programs-work">How website builder affiliate programs work</h2>
<p>A website builder affiliate program pays you a share of the subscription when a reader buys a paid plan through your link. Most programs pay a revenue share, which is a percentage of what the customer pays.</p>
<p>Three terms decide what you actually earn. The commission rate is the percentage or flat amount, and it is usually quoted as "up to" a figure, which means the top rate, not the average. The earning period is how long you keep earning from one customer, and it matters more than the rate. The cookie is the tracking window, which is the number of days after the click during which a purchase still counts as yours.</p>
<p>The network is the platform that tracks sales and pays you. Most website builder programs run on Impact, PartnerStack, Awin or ShareASale. The payout threshold is the minimum balance you must reach before the network pays you.</p>
<p>Attribution also matters. Some programs use first click, which means the first affiliate link the customer clicked gets the sale. Webflow uses first-touch attribution, which means the first link counts, and its official affiliate page says so. Check the attribution rule before you choose, because it changes which of your links earns.</p>

<h2 id="how-to-choose-the-right-program-for-your-audience">How to choose the right program for your audience</h2>
<p>Audience fit comes first, and the rate comes second. A reader who is a photographer needs Pixpa or Format. A reader who is a designer needs Webflow or Framer.</p>
<p>Then compare the earning period, not just the rate. A 50 percent share for 12 months is worth far more than a 50 percent share for one month. Then check the cookie, because a 90-day cookie beats a 30-day cookie when readers take weeks to decide.</p>

<h2 id="affiliate-program-or-partner-program-which-fits-client-work">Affiliate program or partner program: which fits client work</h2>
<p>Website builders run two different kinds of program, and mixing them up is a common mistake. An affiliate program pays you for promoting the builder to a wide audience, through content, videos, or social media. A partner program pays you for referring your own clients, one by one.</p>
<p>Webflow makes the distinction clear on its own affiliate page. Its affiliate program is for content creators and influencers, and it does not pay for one-to-one client referrals.</p>
<p>If you build websites for clients, apply to the partner program, not the affiliate program, because the affiliate program will not pay you for your own clients. If you publish content for a wide audience, apply to the affiliate program.</p>

<h2 id="how-to-apply-step-by-step">How to apply, step by step</h2>
<p>Step 1: Build at least five useful pages before you apply. A builder comparison, a guide to choosing a builder, and a review of one builder are a good start.</p>
<p>Step 2: Join the network that runs the program you want. Webflow runs through PartnerStack. Shopify runs through Impact. Many builders run their own direct programs. The <a href="https://impact.com/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Impact</a> site explains how publisher accounts work.</p>
<p>Step 3: Read the program terms before you apply. Look for the commission, the earning period, the cookie, the payout threshold, and the banned claims. Most builders ban bidding on their brand name in paid search ads.</p>
<p>Step 4: Apply with honest information about your audience, your traffic, and your content. Do not inflate your numbers, because networks check.</p>
<p>Step 5: If you are accepted, add your disclosure near the top of each page that carries affiliate links, before the first affiliate link.</p>
<p>Step 6: If you are rejected, improve the site and reapply in one to three months. You can also start with a program that accepts smaller sites, such as SITE123 or Dorik, and apply to the bigger names later.</p>
<h2 id="example-only-what-the-maths-looks-like">Example only: what the maths looks like</h2>
<p>This example uses made up numbers. It shows the maths, not what you will earn. Example only.</p>
<p>Suppose a design blog gets 2,000 visits a month on a Webflow review. Suppose 2 percent of visitors click the affiliate link. That is 2,000 times 0.02, which is 40 clicks. Suppose 5 percent of those clickers buy a plan. That is 40 times 0.05, which is 2 sales. Suppose the plan costs 150 dollars a year and the program pays 50 percent for 12 months, which is 75 dollars per sale. That is 2 times 75, which is 150 dollars in the first year.</p>
<p>Now suppose both customers renew in the second year at the base tier. That is 2 times 75 again, which is another 150 dollars, with no new traffic needed. The recurring model pays less at first and more over time, but only if customers stay subscribed.</p>

<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Do not quote an "up to" rate as if it were the average, because the top rate is not what most sales pay.</li>
<li>Do not ignore the earning period, because 50 percent for one month is worth far less than 50 percent for 12 months.</li>
<li>Do not mix up affiliate and partner programs, because the affiliate program will not pay you for your own clients.</li>
<li>Do not promote hosting as if it were a website builder, because readers who want a builder will leave.</li>
<li>Do not hide your disclosure at the bottom of the page, because readers must see it before they click.</li>
<li>Do not forget the payout minimum, because a 300 dollar minimum delays your first payment for months on a small site.</li>

<h2 id="risks-and-scams-to-watch-for">Risks and scams to watch for</h2>
<p>Website builder affiliate marketing has real rules, and breaking them can end your account. The US Federal Trade Commission requires a clear disclosure when you earn a commission from a recommendation, and the disclosure must be easy to notice. The <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking" rel="noopener" target="_blank">FTC Endorsement Guides</a> explain what counts as a clear disclosure. Put it near the top of the page, in plain words, before the first affiliate link.</p>
<p>Most builders ban bidding on their brand name in paid search ads, and many ban claims such as "free website" when the plan costs money. Read the program terms before you publish, and read them again when you update a page.</p>
<p>Watch for scams that target new affiliates. No real program charges you a fee to join, and no real program asks you to pay for a starter kit or a list of high paying programs before you can apply. Be careful with any site that promises a fixed monthly income from builder links, because no one can promise that. If a program is not listed on a known network such as Impact, PartnerStack, Awin or ShareASale, and you cannot find its official terms, do not promote it.</p>
<p>Affiliate income is income, and you must report it under the tax rules of your country. Keep records of every payout, and check your local tax authority or a qualified accountant if you are unsure.</p>

<p>Build a site with several useful pages about building websites, add a clear disclosure, then apply through the network or direct program that runs it.</p>
<h3 id="what-is-the-difference-between-an-affiliate-program-and-a-partner-program">What is the difference between an affiliate program and a partner program?</h3>
<p>An affiliate program pays you for promoting the builder to a wide audience through content. A partner program pays you for referring your own clients, one by one. If you build sites for clients, apply to the partner program.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Website builder affiliate programs pay a share of the subscription, and the earning period matters more than the headline rate. Webflow pays 50 percent for up to 12 months with a 90-day cookie, and that recurring model beats a one-time bounty over two years. Keep your disclosures clear, and never quote an "up to" rate as if it were the average.</p>
