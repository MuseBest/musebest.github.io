How to Do Affiliate Marketing Without a Website in 2026

Every guide seems to say the same thing: build a website first. If you do not have a site, that advice can feel like a closed door. The direct answer is yes, you can do affiliate marketing without a website, with rules you must follow. You can promote products on video, on Pinterest, by email, in groups, and in long-form answers, and you can earn commissions from those channels. But you do not own those channels, and each one has its own rules, so the method matters as much as the channel.

What affiliate marketing without a website means

Affiliate marketing without a website means you promote products through a channel you do not own as a site, such as a video channel, a social profile, an email list, or a long-form platform. You still need a place where people can find your content, a link they can click, and a programme that pays you when they buy. What you do not need is a domain, hosting, or a blog.

What it does not mean is that you can skip trust, or skip the rules. A reader still needs a reason to click your link, and a programme still needs a reason to accept you. It also does not mean the work is free. You pay with time instead of money, and time is a real cost. The first month is for building proof, not income, and results vary by niche, channel, and effort.

The main risk is rented land. A platform can change its rules, limit your reach, or close your account, and you lose the audience you built there. That is why this guide ends with an email list, which is the one asset you can own without a website, and why it ends with a plan to build a site later, when you are ready.

The two rules before you start

Two rules apply on every channel, before you post a single link. First, disclose clearly next to the link. A disclosure is a short, plain statement that you may earn a commission if the reader buys through your link. It must sit beside the link, not hidden in a profile bio that the reader may never open. In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission requires this in its Endorsement Guides FAQ, which says material connections must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously, and that disclosures must travel with the endorsement, including in video. Other countries have their own advertising rules, so check the rules that apply where you live and where your audience lives.

Second, read the programme terms as well as the platform rules. A programme is the affiliate scheme that pays you, such as a retailer or a network. A platform is the place where you post, such as YouTube, Pinterest, or Facebook. Each has its own rules, and they do not always match. A platform may allow affiliate links, while a programme may forbid links in a certain place, or in a certain type of message. If the two rules conflict, follow the stricter one. If you break a programme rule, you can lose commissions or lose the account. If you break a platform rule, you can lose the account itself.

Method 1: YouTube and short video

YouTube is the strongest no-website channel for most beginners, because it is a search engine as well as a video platform. A tutorial, a review, or a comparison can keep bringing viewers for months, long after you publish it. You do not need subscribers to use ordinary description links, and you do not need to join the YouTube Partner Programme to place an affiliate link in a video description. You do need to disclose the link, in the video and in writing near the link.

Make videos that answer a question with a natural product decision. A tutorial that shows how to do a task, a review that tests a product, and a comparison that helps a viewer choose between two options all convert better than a video that only talks about a product. Put the link in the first two lines of the description, so a viewer on a phone can see it without opening more text. Add a pinned comment with the same link, and label it clearly. For the full method, read how to use YouTube for affiliate marketing.

Short video on its own is harder, because links in Shorts descriptions and comments are not clickable. Use Shorts to point viewers to a longer video, or to a link in your bio, and say so in the video. Do not expect a Short to earn on its own. Its job is to bring viewers to a place where they can click.

Method 2: Pinterest

Pinterest works like a visual search engine, and pins can keep bringing clicks for months. That makes it a good fit for products people search for with a picture in mind, such as home, craft, fashion, food, and planning tools. Create a pin with a clear image, a short title that names the benefit, and a description that says what the reader will find. Add your disclosure on the pin, where a reader will see it before they click, not only on the page the pin links to.

Link each pin to a useful destination, such as a video, a long-form post, or a landing page for your email list. Do not link a pin straight to a product page with no context, because that reads like an advert, and Pinterest may limit it. Make several pins for one piece of content, with different images and titles, and watch which one brings clicks. For a wider guide to the platform, read how to make money on Pinterest.

Be careful with programme rules here too. Some programmes forbid Pinterest links, or forbid links that hide the destination. Check the terms before you pin, and follow the stricter rule.

Method 3: Email list on a hosted opt-in page

An email list is the asset you own, even without a website. An opt-in page is a single page, hosted by your email tool, where a reader enters an email address in exchange for a free item, such as a checklist or a short guide. That free item is called a lead magnet. You can share the opt-in page link on video, on Pinterest, in groups, and in your bio, without owning a site.

Start with one lead magnet that solves one small problem fast. A one page checklist beats a long ebook, because the reader can use it at once. Write a short welcome email that delivers the magnet, says what you will send, and how often. Then send one useful email a week, with one product you genuinely recommend, and a clear disclosure beside the link.

Email law matters here. In the United States, the CAN-SPAM Act sets rules for marketing email, including honest headers, identifying the message as an advert, a valid postal address, a clear opt-out explanation, and honouring opt-outs within 10 business days. The Federal Trade Commission explains these rules in its CAN-SPAM compliance guide, which also notes that penalties can reach up to 53,088 dollars per email in violation, as of November 2026. Other countries have their own email laws, so check the rules that apply where you live. Never buy an email list, and never add people without clear permission.

Method 4: Facebook groups and profiles

Facebook groups can bring fast clicks, because members already share an interest, and a helpful answer in a group can reach people who trust the group. That speed comes with the highest ban risk of any method in this guide. Groups have their own rules, and many groups forbid affiliate links outright. A post that breaks a group rule can be removed, and repeat posts can get you removed from the group, or reported to Facebook.

Use groups to help first, and link second. Answer a question in full, in the group, without asking the reader to click. If a product genuinely helps, and the group rules allow a link, add it with a clear disclosure beside it, and say why you recommend it. Never drop a bare link with no context. Never post the same link in many groups on the same day. That pattern looks like spam, and it is the fastest way to lose access.

A Facebook profile is not a shop. A post on your own profile can include a link, if your friends and followers expect that kind of post from you, but a profile full of links will lose trust fast. Keep the ratio high on help, and low on links.

Method 5: Long-form and answers

Long-form platforms, such as Medium, LinkedIn, Quora, and Reddit, let you write a full guide or answer without owning a site. They work best when you answer first and link second. A reader who finds a full, honest answer is more likely to trust the link at the end than a reader who finds a short post that only says "click here".

On Medium and LinkedIn, write a complete guide on one problem, with steps, examples, and a clear verdict. Put the affiliate link near the end, with a disclosure beside it, and say what the link is. On Quora and Reddit, answer the question in the thread, in full, and only add a link if it genuinely helps the reader and the community rules allow it. Many Reddit communities forbid affiliate links, and a link that breaks the rules can get your account banned from that community. Read the rules of each community before you post, and follow them.

One warning about sources: the Medium guide that ranks for this topic says its author used AI to help research and draft the story, and some of its linked policy pages would not load when checked. Treat policy quotes from a single article as leads, not as verified facts, and check the official page yourself before you repeat a rule. That habit will save you from repeating an old fine figure or a rule that has changed.

Method 6: Short-form video

Short-form video on TikTok and Instagram Reels can bring fast views, but it is a weak channel for direct affiliate sales, because captions do not have clickable links. The link lives in your bio, or in a link-in-bio page, which is a single page with a short list of links. A viewer must leave the video, open your profile, tap the bio link, and then tap the product link. Each step loses viewers, so only a small share will reach the product.

Use short video to build an audience and to point viewers to a place where they can click. Make a short tip, a quick demo, or a before and after, and end with a clear next step, such as "the full guide is in the link in my bio". Keep the bio link list short, with three to five links at most, and label each one clearly, so the viewer knows what they will find. For the Instagram method in detail, read how to make money on Instagram with a small account.

Do not buy followers or views to grow faster. Bought followers do not buy, and platforms can limit accounts that use them. Growth on short video is slow at first, and that is normal.

How to apply to programmes without a website

Applying without a website needs a different answer to one question: where is your traffic? A programme application usually asks for a website, a channel, or a social profile. List the channel you will actually use, with its real address, such as your YouTube channel, your Pinterest profile, or your email opt-in page. Describe your audience in one sentence, and name the products you plan to promote, and why they fit that audience. A clear, honest application beats a vague one.

Some programmes require a website, and will turn you down without one. That is a real limit, and you should expect it. Amazon Associates, for example, asks you to list the sites, apps, or social profiles where you will place links, and its Associates Program Policies say links must only appear on registered sites, with email allowed only to people who opted in, and must not be cloaked or hidden, and must not appear in unsolicited email. Check the terms for each programme you join, because the rule that matters is the one in your own account, not a summary in a blog post.

Start with programmes that accept social profiles and channels. Many networks and retailers do, and the best programmes for beginners are listed in best affiliate programs for beginners. Apply to one or two at first, and wait for the answer before you apply to ten. A rejection is not the end. It usually means the programme wants more content, a clearer audience, or a site, and you can apply again when you have it.

A simple funnel without a website

A funnel is the path a reader follows from your content to a sale. Without a website, a simple funnel has five steps. First, content that answers a question, such as a video, a pin, or a long-form answer. Second, a trust point, such as a clear verdict, a real drawback, or a photo of the product in use. Third, a call to action, such as "check the current price here". Fourth, a link destination, such as the product page, or your email opt-in page. Fifth, a follow-up, such as a second video, a second pin, or a welcome email that answers the next question.

Keep the funnel short. Each extra step loses readers. A video that links straight to a product page has one step. A pin that links to a video that links to a product has two steps. A post that links to an email list that links to a product has three steps, but it gains an owned audience in return. Choose the shortest funnel that still builds trust, and test it with real clicks before you build a longer one.

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Imagine a YouTube tutorial that gets 500 views in its first month. If 50 viewers click the description link, the click rate is 10 percent. If 1 of those 50 buys, the conversion rate is 2 percent of clicks. If the commission is 8 dollars, the video earned 8 dollars in its first month. That is a small sum, and it is only an example, but it shows how views, clicks, and sales connect, and why a video with fewer views but a better fit can earn more than a video with many views and no fit.

What it costs and what to expect

Starting without a website can cost nothing in money. You can film on a phone, post on free platforms, and use a free email tool tier to start. The cost is time. A single video can take three to five hours to plan, film, and edit. A pin can take 30 minutes to make well. A welcome email can take two hours to write well. Those hours add up, and they are the real cost of the free route.

Expect a slow start. The first month is for building proof: a few videos, a few pins, a small list, and a clear record of what you tried. Do not expect a first commission in a set number of weeks, because no honest guide can promise that. Results vary by niche, channel, effort, and the products you choose. Some beginners earn a first small commission in the first months, and many earn nothing for longer. That is normal, and it is not a sign that the method does not work. It is a sign that trust takes time to build.

Paid adverts are not a beginner method. They cost money, they can lose money fast, and they need a tested funnel before they make sense. Treat them as an advanced, optional step, and only try them when you can afford to lose the test budget, and when you have a post or video that already earns without adverts.

Common mistakes

  • Dropping bare links in groups, comments, and threads, with no help and no context, which looks like spam.
  • Hiding the disclosure in a bio, or at the bottom of a page, where the reader will not see it before they click.
  • Promoting too many products at once, so no single recommendation feels honest or tested.
  • Ignoring group and community rules, and losing access to the audience you were trying to reach.
  • Making income claims, or promising a first commission in a set time, when results vary and no honest guide can promise that.
  • Sending affiliate links in unsolicited email, which breaks programme rules and email law in many countries.
  • Building only on rented platforms, with no email list, so a rule change can take your whole audience away.
  • Paying for adverts before you have a post or video that earns without them.

A simple 30-day starter plan

Use one channel, one free gift, and one product, for 30 days. Do not try all six methods at once. Pick the channel that fits your skills: video if you like to talk, Pinterest if you like images, writing if you like long-form answers. Then follow this plan.

Week 1: choose a niche and one product, read the programme terms and the platform rules, and set up your channel profile with a clear description and a disclosure line. Week 2: create your lead magnet and your opt-in page, and write your welcome email. Week 3: publish four pieces of content on your one channel, each answering a real question, each with a disclosure and a link where the rules allow it. Week 4: check your clicks, note which piece brought the most clicks, and make two more pieces like the best one. Track one number only at this stage: clicks per piece of content. Sales will follow later, or they will not, and the click data will tell you which topic deserves more work.

Frequently asked questions

Can I do affiliate marketing without a website?

Yes, you can do affiliate marketing without a website, by promoting products on YouTube, Pinterest, email, Facebook groups, and long-form platforms. You still need a channel where people can find your content, a link they can click, and a programme that accepts you without a site. You must disclose the link clearly, and follow both the programme terms and the platform rules.

Do I need followers to make affiliate sales?

No, you do not need a large following to make a first sale, but you do need trust and a good fit between the product and the audience. A small audience that trusts you can buy more than a large audience that does not. Do not trust claims that beginners earn first commissions with under a set follower count, because those claims are rarely sourced, and results vary.

How do I get affiliate links without a website?

Apply to a programme with your channel or social profile as your traffic source, instead of a domain. List the real address of your YouTube channel, Pinterest profile, or email opt-in page, describe your audience in one sentence, and name the products you plan to promote. Some programmes require a website and will turn you down, so start with programmes that accept social profiles and channels.

Is affiliate marketing without a website against the rules?

No, it is not against the rules in general, but each programme and each platform has its own rules, and some forbid links in certain places. Amazon Associates, for example, does not allow links in unsolicited email, and does not allow cloaked links. Read the terms for each programme you join, and the rules for each platform where you post, and follow the stricter rule when they differ.

Can I start affiliate marketing with no money?

Yes, you can start with no money, by filming on a phone, posting on free platforms, and using a free email tool tier. The cost is time, not money, and a single video can take hours to make well. Paid adverts are not a beginner method, because they cost money and can lose money fast.

Should I build a website eventually?

Yes, in most cases, you should build a website when you have proof that a topic works, such as steady clicks and a small email list. A site gives you a home for reviews and guides that search can find, a place where your links live under your control, and an easier programme application. Until then, the no-website methods in this guide are a fair way to learn and build proof.

Conclusion: pick one channel, follow the rules, and build an asset you own

Affiliate marketing without a website works when you pick one channel, answer real questions, disclose clearly next to every link, and follow both the programme terms and the platform rules. Start with YouTube, Pinterest, or email, build a small list you own, and track one number: clicks per piece of content. Then build a website when you have proof that a topic works, not before.

Your next step is to choose one channel this week, and publish four pieces of content on it in the next 30 days.
