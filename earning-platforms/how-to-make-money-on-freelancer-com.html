How to Make Money on Freelancer.com in 2026

Learning how to make money on Freelancer.com starts with one honest fact. Anyone can enter, because clients post projects and freelancers bid for them. That open door is also the problem. A new bidder with no reviews competes against hundreds of sellers, many with long histories and low prices. This guide shows you how to win bids anyway: how to set up a profile clients trust, pick the right projects, write bids that answer the exact job, use contests to earn your first reviews, and avoid the fees and scams that catch beginners.

How Freelancer.com works

Freelancer.com is a bidding marketplace. Clients post projects with a description and a budget range. Freelancers read the project, send a bid with a price and a short message, and the client picks a winner. There are three ways to earn: bid on projects, enter contests, and take hourly work.

In a fixed-price project, you agree a price before you start. The client funds a milestone payment, which is a portion of the total held safely until the work is done. In an hourly project, you log your hours with the desktop time tracker, and the client is billed weekly. In a contest, a client posts a brief and a prize, many freelancers submit entries, and the client picks one winner who receives the prize.

Payments run through SafePay, the platform's milestone payment system. The client deposits the money before you start, and Freelancer.com releases it when the work is approved. That system protects you, as long as you use it properly and never agree to work without a funded milestone. For the wider picture of the platform, read our Freelancer.com review.

What it costs: fees, bid limits, and the balance rule

Freelancer.com charges freelancers a fee on every project, and the fee is charged when you accept the project, not when you get paid. That timing matters, and it catches beginners out. The figures below come from the official fee page, checked in November 2026.

Item What happens Figure, November 2026
Fixed-price fee Charged when you accept a project 10 percent or 5 dollars, whichever is greater
Hourly fee Charged on hourly work 10 percent
Contest fee Charged when the prize is released after handover 10 percent or 5 dollars, whichever is greater
Preferred Freelancer Recruiter fee For Recruiter projects, charged when payment arrives 15 percent
Free bids Bids included with a free account 6 per month initially
Minimum balance Balance needed before you can place any bid 20 dollars

Read the full fee page, Freelancer Fees and Charges, before you bid, because fees and limits can change. Withdrawal methods, minimums, and costs vary by method and currency, so check the official fee page for the current figures rather than trusting a guide that quotes them from memory.

The fee-at-acceptance rule has a real effect on small projects. On a 10 dollar project, the 5 dollar minimum fee is half the value, so you keep 5 dollars before any withdrawal cost. On a 50 dollar project, the 10 percent fee is 5 dollars, so you keep 45 dollars. The lesson is simple: once you have a few reviews, aim for projects above 50 dollars, where the minimum fee stops eating your pay. In your first weeks, a small project can still be worth it for the review, but know what it costs you before you accept it.

Step 1: Set up your profile

Your profile decides whether a client reads your bid or skips it. Start by verifying your identity. Many clients filter out unverified freelancers, because verification is the simplest way to avoid bots and fake accounts. It usually takes a day or two, so start it before you bid on anything.

Write a tight headline that names your niche, not your job title. "Logo designer for food brands" beats "Graphic designer". Write a short overview that says what you do, who you help, and what result you deliver. Keep it plain and specific. Add the skills that match your niche, and fill in your education and languages honestly.

Build a portfolio before you bid. If you have no client work yet, make sample pieces for imaginary clients, and label them as samples. A designer can make three logos for made-up firms. A writer can write three short posts for a made-up brand. A developer can build a small demo site. Contest entries also make good portfolio pieces, which is one reason contests matter for beginners. For a fuller method, read how to create a freelance portfolio.

Take the skill tests that match your niche, if they are available. A passed test does not prove you are good, but it gives a new profile one more signal that a client can check. If you are new to freelancing itself, read how to start freelancing first.

Step 2: Learn the bidding system

Bidding is the core skill on Freelancer.com, and it works differently from Fiverr gigs or Upwork proposals. When a client posts a project, they see a list of bidders ranked by bid amount, reviews, Preferred Freelancer status, and how well each profile matches the project tags. New bidders start at the bottom of that list, so your bid message has to do more work than a top seller's bid.

Free members receive 6 bids a month initially, so every bid is precious. Paid memberships add more bids, but do not buy one until you know which projects are worth bidding on. Spend your first month learning the feed: read 20 projects a day, note which ones attract 100 bids and which attract 5, and watch what the winning bidders have in common. That study costs nothing and teaches you more than 6 wasted bids.

Check your account balance before you bid. Freelancer.com requires a minimum balance of 20 dollars before you place any bid, as of November 2026. Keep that balance topped up, and remember that the fee is charged when you accept a project, so a win also costs you money before you are paid.

Step 3: Pick the right projects

Most beginners lose money by bidding on the wrong projects. Use filters to find the ones worth your 6 bids. Look for projects posted in the last few hours, because fresh projects have fewer bidders and clients who are still reading bids. Look for the "Deposit Made" marker, which shows the client has funded a milestone. Look for clients with verified payment and a history of hiring, with good feedback from past freelancers.

Avoid the data entry trap. Projects that promise 500 dollars for simple data entry, with no clear brief and a client who joined yesterday, are almost always scams or bait. Avoid projects with 100 bids already, because your new profile will not stand out in that crowd. Avoid clients who ask you to move to Telegram or WhatsApp, ask for a security deposit, or ask you to pay for software before you start. Those are scams, and the scams section below explains them in full.

Pick projects that match your niche exactly, where you can point to a sample that proves you can do the job. A bid that says "I have done this exact job before, here is the sample" beats a bid that says "I can do anything". Bid on small projects first, in the 30 to 100 dollar range, where the fee is fair and the client is more willing to try a new freelancer.

Step 4: Write a bid that wins

Your bid has two parts: the price, and a short message. Both matter, and both should answer the exact project, not a generic template.

Open your message with the client's need, in your own words. If the client wants a logo for a coffee brand, your first sentence should say what you would make for a coffee brand, not that you are a designer with five years of experience. Ask one specific question about the project, such as which style they prefer or when they need it. A real question proves you read the brief. Then give one short piece of proof, such as a link to a similar sample. End with your price, your delivery time, and a clear next step.

Example only: this is a structure, not a template to copy. A strong bid has four parts. First, one sentence that restates the client's goal, which shows you read the project. Second, one question that shows you understand the job. Third, one sentence of proof, with a link to a relevant sample. Fourth, your price and delivery time, stated plainly. Keep the whole message under 150 words. Clients read dozens of bids, and long ones get skipped.

Price honestly. Do not bid the lowest price to win, because the lowest bidder often wins the worst clients. Bid a fair price for the work, and let your message and samples do the persuading. If your price is higher than the budget, say why in one sentence, and name what the client gets for it. For a fuller method, read how to write freelance proposals.

Step 5: Use contests to get your first reviews

Contests are the best route to your first reviews on Freelancer.com, because they do not cost bids. A client posts a brief and a prize, you submit an entry, and if the client picks yours, you win the prize and a review. Even if you do not win, your entry becomes a portfolio piece.

Enter contests in your niche, where you can do good work fast. Read the brief twice, and follow it exactly. Clients pick entries that match what they asked for, not entries that show off a different style. Submit your best work, not a quick draft, because the client compares entries side by side. Check the prize and the fee before you enter: the contest fee is 10 percent or 5 dollars, whichever is greater, charged when the prize is released after handover.

Do not enter every contest you see. Pick two or three a week where your skills fit well, and do them properly. Three strong entries beat ten rushed ones. When you win, deliver the handover files promptly and politely, and ask the client for a review. That first review is the hardest to earn, and a contest win is the cheapest way to get it.

Step 6: Deliver safely and get paid

Never start work before the milestone is funded. When a client awards you a project, check that the milestone payment is in place before you write a word or draw a line. If the client asks you to start first and fund later, decline politely. The milestone system is what protects you, and working without it leaves you with no leverage if the client vanishes.

Keep all communication on Freelancer.com. The platform's record of your messages is what decides a dispute, and messages on Telegram or email do not count. If the client asks for a change, agree it in the project chat, and ask for a new milestone if the change adds real work. Deliver on time, and tell the client what you have done at each step.

For hourly work, use the desktop time tracker. It records your hours and takes screenshots, and the client is billed weekly for the time you logged. Only log time you actually worked, and only with the tracker running. When a milestone is complete, ask the client to release it. If the client is slow to respond, send one polite reminder, then wait. Most clients release promptly once they see the work is done.

Scams to avoid

Freelancer.com has more scam posts than most marketplaces, and beginners are the target. The patterns are consistent, and spotting them keeps you safe.

Never pay to get work. If a client asks for a security deposit, a training fee, a software licence, or any payment before you start, it is a scam. Real clients pay you, not the other way round. Never move to Telegram, WhatsApp, or email to discuss payment. Scammers use those channels because the platform cannot see them. Never share bank details, passwords, or identity documents with a client. Freelancer.com verifies your identity itself, and a client never needs your bank details.

Watch for the data entry trap: a high-paying data entry job with a vague brief, a new client, and a request to contact them off the platform. Watch for fake cheques and payment proofs that ask you to send money first. Watch for clients who ask you to create multiple accounts, which can get all your accounts banned. If a project feels wrong, trust that feeling, do not bid, and report it. For a wider guide to spotting bad job posts, read how to spot online job scams.

Growing: Preferred Freelancer and repeat clients

The Preferred Freelancer Program is the platform's badge for proven sellers. Members get better visibility, access to Recruiter projects, and a badge that clients trust. The trade-off is the fee: Preferred Freelancer members pay 15 percent on Recruiter projects, charged when payment arrives. You cannot apply for the programme on day one. It follows a record of completed projects, good reviews, and on-time delivery. Treat it as a goal for your sixth month, not your first.

Repeat clients matter more than any badge. A client who hires you twice costs you no bids the second time, and the relationship lets you charge a fairer price. Ask happy clients whether they have more work, and keep in touch politely after a project ends. One steady client who sends regular work is worth more than ten one-off bids.

Raise your rates as reviews build. After three to five good reviews, raise your prices by 10 to 20 percent. After ten reviews, raise them again. For a deeper method, read how to set freelance rates.

What it costs to make money on Freelancer.com, and what to expect

Starting on Freelancer.com costs nothing to join, but it is not free to work. You need a 20 dollar minimum balance before you can bid, and every project you accept costs you a fee of 10 percent or 5 dollars, whichever is greater. Your first payout also waits for milestones to be released, so your first money arrives weeks after your first win.

Expect a slow first few months. The first month is usually spent trying to win one review, and many beginners earn nothing in that month. That is normal. Reviews are the bottleneck, and contests are the cheapest way through it. Do not trust any guide that promises a monthly income figure for your first months, because earnings depend on your skill, your niche, your prices, and how many bids you can afford. Anyone who quotes a timeline without knowing your niche is guessing.

Treat Freelancer.com as a small business. Bid carefully, deliver well, keep your messages on the platform, and let each review make the next bid easier. If you prefer a platform where clients post jobs and you bid with Connects, read our guide on how to make money on Upwork.

Common mistakes

  • Copying the same bid message to every project, which clients spot at once.
  • Bidding on projects with 100 bids already, where a new profile cannot stand out.
  • Ignoring the fee minimum, and accepting a 10 dollar project where the fee takes half the pay.
  • Working without a funded milestone, which leaves you unpaid if the client vanishes.
  • Moving the conversation to Telegram or WhatsApp, which removes your protection.
  • Paying a security deposit or training fee to get work, which is always a scam.
  • Bidding on data entry jobs with vague briefs and new clients, which are usually traps.
  • Buying a paid membership before you know which projects are worth bidding on.

Conclusion: bid less, bid better, and earn the first review

Making money on Freelancer.com comes down to a simple method. Set up a verified profile with real samples, spend your 6 monthly bids only on fresh projects with verified payment, write short bids that answer the exact job, and use contests to earn your first reviews without spending bids. Then deliver safely behind a funded milestone, keep your messages on the platform, and let each review make the next bid easier. Start today by verifying your profile and entering one contest in your niche.
