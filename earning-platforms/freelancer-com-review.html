Freelancer.com Review in 2026

Freelancer.com review: bidding, the 10% project fee, memberships, milestones and safety risks. Learn whether beginners can win work there in 2026.

Freelancer.com is a legitimate marketplace, and it is also the hardest of the three big freelance platforms for a beginner to break into. That blunt verdict is the core of this Freelancer.com review. Open bidding means you compete against many freelancers on nearly every project, prices get pushed down, and free members start with a small bid allowance that paid members can outspend. Specialists with a narrow skill and the patience to bid at volume can still win work here. Beginners in generic categories, hoping for quick wins at strong rates, will mostly find frustration.

What Freelancer.com is and how it works

Freelancer.com is a global marketplace where clients post projects and freelancers bid for them with a price, a timeline, and a short pitch. The client compares bids side by side and picks a winner. The platform also runs contests, where clients post a prize for a piece of work, freelancers submit entries, and only the winner gets paid. A third route, called Services, lets freelancers list fixed-price offers that clients can buy directly, similar to a Gig model, but bidding remains the main way work is won.

Signing up, creating a profile, selecting skills, uploading a portfolio, and browsing projects are free. Bidding itself is free in the sense that placing a bid costs no cash fee, but your bid allowance is limited. Free members start with a small monthly bid allowance, and the exact allowance is shown in your account. You can earn extra bids through activity, or buy more bidding capacity through a paid membership. Before you place any bid, your account must also hold a minimum balance, normally 20 dollars, which stays reserved while your bid is open. That balance rule surprises many beginners, so plan for it before you start bidding.

What Freelancer.com costs freelancers

The freelancer project fee is 10% of the project value or 5 dollars, whichever is greater, on fixed-price projects, and 10% on hourly payments. Contest prizes carry the same 10% or 5 dollar rule, charged only when you win and the handover completes. Freelancers in the Preferred Freelancer Program pay 15% on Recruiter projects, charged when payment arrives. These figures were confirmed on Freelancer.com's official fee page on 2026-11-03, in its United States dollar answers. The same page shows different figures in other currencies, such as Australian dollars, so always check the fee page version for your own country and currency before you bid.

The minimum fee matters far more than most beginners realise. On a 20 dollar project, the 5 dollar minimum is 25% of the project, not 10%. On any fixed-price project below 50 dollars, the minimum pushes your effective fee above 10%. The table below makes that clear.

| Project value | Fee charged | You receive | Effective fee | | 20 dollars | 5 dollars | 15 dollars | 25% | | 40 dollars | 5 dollars | 35 dollars | 12.5% | | 50 dollars | 5 dollars | 45 dollars | 10% | | 200 dollars | 20 dollars | 180 dollars | 10% | | 500 dollars | 50 dollars | 450 dollars | 10% |

Clients pay a separate fee on their side, so the platform collects from both sides of a project. The client fee is commonly around 3% on fixed-price projects, with its own small minimum, and it varies by currency and project type. Check the official Freelancer.com fee page for the current rates in your currency, because the Australian dollar figures on that page differ from the United States dollar figures quoted here.

Paid memberships add monthly bids and extra features, and they are billed monthly or annually until cancelled. Prices and bid allowances are shown in your account when you upgrade, and they change from time to time, so this review does not quote a membership price as a fixed fact. Treat membership as a business cost to test, not as a requirement on day one. If you bid rarely, a paid plan wastes money. If you bid daily in a competitive category, the extra bids may be the only way to stay visible, and that reality is itself a warning about the platform's economics.

Getting paid: milestones, withdrawal, and the minimum balance

Freelancer.com uses a milestone payment system. The client deposits funds into a milestone before work begins, and those funds stay held until the milestone is released, refunded, or dealt with through dispute resolution. For fixed-price work, agree milestones that match real stages of the job, and do not start a stage before its milestone is funded. For hourly work, use the platform's time tracking where available, and keep clear records of what you did. Unfunded work has weak protection, however good your relationship with the client feels.

Withdrawal fees depend on the method. According to the official fee page, PayPal, Skrill, Payoneer debit card, and Express Withdrawal each cost 1 dollar, while international wire transfer costs 25 dollars. Local bank deposit is free where available. The minimum withdrawal after fees is 50 dollars, which matters for beginners: small balances sit locked until your total earnings pass that threshold, after fees. Third-party providers may add their own conversion charges on top. Do not plan your monthly budget around withdrawing small amounts weekly, because the platform is not built for that.

Two more charges deserve attention. Accounts that have not logged in for six months can face a maintenance fee of up to 14 dollars per month until the account is reactivated or terminated, with a refund available on reactivation on request. Users who move communications and payments off the platform can face a penalty of 30% of the project budget under the platform's terms. Both rules push in the same direction: stay active, and keep all work and payment inside Freelancer.com.

Bidding: how to bid without burning your allowance

Bidding is a numbers game with a quality filter, and beginners lose when they ignore either half. Sending the same generic bid to every project wastes your allowance and teaches you nothing. Sending only two perfect bids a month in a crowded category may never win anything either. The workable middle is a tight weekly routine: pick a narrow set of projects that fit your skill, read each brief fully, and write a short bid that names the client's problem, your relevant proof, your price, and your timeline. Skip projects with vague briefs, unrealistic budgets, or clients with no hiring history. Each skipped bad project protects bids for a good one.

Price honestly. The race to the bottom is real on Freelancer.com, and very low bids attract clients who expect very low prices forever. A bid slightly above the cheapest, with a clear scope and a portfolio sample, often beats the cheapest bid, because clients who have been burned by poor work will pay a little more for reliability. Set a floor price below which you will not bid, and calculate it with the fee table above. A 30 dollar project that pays you 25 dollars after the minimum fee, and takes you five hours, pays 5 dollars per hour before other costs. That is not a bargain worth winning.

Contests: a portfolio path with a catch

Contests deserve a separate look, because they work differently from bidding. A client posts a prize, freelancers submit finished work, and only the winner is paid. Entering is free, which makes contests tempting for beginners who want portfolio pieces. The catch is obvious: you do the work upfront, and you are paid only if you win. For designers, that trade can still make sense, because a strong entry can be reused in a portfolio even when it loses, and a win brings both money and a public result. For most service work, contests are a poor deal, because custom work cannot be reused, and hours spent on losing entries earn nothing.

If you enter contests, set strict limits. Enter only contests in your strongest skill, where the prize justifies the hours, and where the brief is clear. Never enter a contest that asks for speculative work on a vague brief with a tiny prize. Count contest hours as portfolio investment, not as income, and stop entering if you have no wins after a set number of entries. A portfolio built from contest losses is still a portfolio, but it is an expensive way to build one.

Spotting bad clients and staying safe

Freelancer.com's open model attracts good clients and bad ones, so vetting matters as much as bidding. Watch for these warning signs: a client who demands full upfront payment outside the platform, a client who asks you to move to private messaging and pay by bank transfer, a client who refuses a video call or any identity check while asking for sensitive work, a client who sends files you did not expect, and a client who ghosts after delivery but before milestone release. Keep all communication and payment inside the platform, even when a client claims platform fees are the reason to move elsewhere. The 30% off-platform penalty and the loss of milestone protection make that shortcut far more expensive than the fee it avoids.

When a dispute arises, use the platform's dispute process early, and keep your evidence in the project chat: the agreed scope, the delivered files, and the milestone history. Paid arbitration exists for milestone disputes that cannot be settled between the parties, and it carries its own fee, so settling early is almost always cheaper. Who Freelancer.com does not suit

Freelancer.com does not suit beginners in generic categories such as general data entry, basic logo design, or general virtual assistance, because those categories carry the fiercest price competition and the lowest win rates for new accounts. It does not suit sellers who cannot bid at volume or pay for membership to compete, because the bid economy rewards both. It does not suit sellers who expect Upwork-level rates on similar work, because open bidding pushes prices down. It does not suit anyone unwilling to keep a 20 dollar minimum balance tied up while bids are open, or to wait until 50 dollars after fees before withdrawing. Frequently asked questions

Is Freelancer.com legit or a scam?

Freelancer.com is a legitimate, publicly listed marketplace with a milestone payment system, and it pays freelancers who complete funded work properly. Legitimate does not mean beginner-friendly, because open bidding and price competition make early wins hard.

How much does Freelancer.com charge freelancers?

Freelancers pay 10% or 5 dollars, whichever is greater, on fixed-price projects, 10% on hourly payments, and the same 10% or 5 dollar rule on contest prizes. The exact minimum varies by currency, so check the official fee page for your country.

How many bids do free members get?

Free members start with a small monthly bid allowance, shown in their account, and the official fee page notes that free members initially receive 6 bids per month. Extra bids can be earned through activity or added through a paid membership.

What is the minimum withdrawal on Freelancer.com?

The minimum withdrawal after fees is 50 dollars. Withdrawal fees are 1 dollar for PayPal, Skrill, Payoneer debit card, and Express Withdrawal, and 25 dollars for international wire transfer, with possible extra charges from payment providers.

Is Freelancer.com better than Upwork?

Upwork generally offers higher-paying clients and a less aggressive bidding system, while Freelancer.com offers more open volume and contests. Beginners who dislike open price competition usually find Upwork easier, while specialists comfortable with bidding volume may prefer Freelancer.com.

Conclusion: should you start on Freelancer.com?

Start on Freelancer.com only if you have a narrow specialism, you can bid consistently, you accept the 10% fee with its 5 dollar minimum, and you can wait until 50 dollars after fees before withdrawing. Set a weekly bid limit, a floor price, and a review point after one month of targeted bidding. If you have no wins and no useful replies by then, change your service, your bids, or your platform before you spend more.
