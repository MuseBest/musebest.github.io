2d:T58fb,<h2 id="how-to-make-money-on-peopleperhour-in-2026">How to Make Money on PeoplePerHour in 2026</h2>
<p>Learning how to make money on PeoplePerHour starts with one honest fact. It is a smaller marketplace than <a href="https://earnquill.com/earning-platforms/how-to-make-money-on-fiverr" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Fiverr</a> or <a href="https://earnquill.com/earning-platforms/how-to-make-money-on-upwork" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Upwork</a>, its application is stricter, and its clients are mostly in the United Kingdom. That smaller size is also its advantage. There is less competition, the clients tend to be serious businesses, and a good freelancer can build steady repeat work. This guide shows you how to pass the application, write Offers buyers actually buy, spend your 15 monthly proposal credits wisely, and price your work for the tiered fee without getting caught out.</p>

<h3 id="how-peopleperhour-works">How PeoplePerHour works</h3>
<p>PeoplePerHour is a freelance marketplace where businesses, called Buyers, hire freelancers for professional services such as writing, design, web development, marketing, and administration. There are three ways to find work.</p>
<p>First, you can sell an Offer. An Offer is a fixed-price packaged service you create, such as "I will write three blog posts for 150 pounds". Buyers browse Offers and buy the ones they need. Second, you can send Proposals. Buyers post projects, and you spend a proposal credit to apply. Third, buyers can contact you directly from your profile about custom work.</p>
<p>Every project runs through two systems. Escrow holds the client's money safely while you work, so you are paid when the work is approved. WorkStream is the workspace where you and the client talk, share files, and record every change. All communication, files, and agreements live in the WorkStream, and that record is what protects you if anything goes wrong. Keep everything there, and never move a project to email or chat apps.</p>
<h3 id="what-it-costs-the-fee-tiers-credits-and-clearing-period">What it costs: the fee tiers, credits, and clearing period</h3>

<p>The official page also says there is no registration or sign-up fee, no fee to search for projects, and no fee to post an Offer. The minimum payment using auto-pay or per hour contracts is 6 pounds excluding VAT, and the minimum service fee per invoice is 1 pound excluding VAT. Read the full page, <a href="https://support.peopleperhour.com/hc/en-us/articles/205218337-Freelancer-commission-fees" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Freelancer commission fees</a>, before you price your work, because fees can change.</p>
<p>There is a second scheme to know about. Third-party guides report a newer scheme for newly registered freelancers: 20 percent on the first 500 pounds billed in a calendar month, then 5 percent. This newer scheme was not verified on the official page in this run, so do not treat either scheme as the only current rule. Check the fee shown in your own account before you quote a price, because that is the fee you will actually pay.</p>
<p>Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Imagine you win a 200 pound job from a new Buyer under the per-client tiers. The fee is 20 percent, which is 40 pounds, so you keep 160 pounds. Now imagine you win a 1,000 pound job from the same Buyer. The first 250 pounds are charged at 20 percent, which is 50 pounds. The remaining 750 pounds are charged at 7.5 percent, which is 56.25 pounds. The total fee is 106.25 pounds, so you keep 893.75 pounds. Recheck this maths yourself, because the fee falls as you bill more to the same Buyer, which is why repeat clients are worth more here than anywhere else.</p>
<p>You also get 15 free proposal credits at the start of each month. One credit is normally used for each Proposal you send. That limit shapes your whole strategy, as Step 4 explains.</p>
<p>Getting paid takes time. After you finish a job and raise an invoice, your money enters a clearing period before you can withdraw it. Guides that checked PeoplePerHour's own withdrawal pages report a clearing period of about 14 working days for newer freelancers, with shorter periods as your earnings grow. Working days are not calendar days, so 14 working days is close to three calendar weeks. Check the clearing date shown in your own account before you plan a payout, because the exact period depends on your earnings history.</p>
<h3 id="step-1-pass-the-application">Step 1: Pass the application</h3>
<p>PeoplePerHour is stricter than Fiverr or Upwork. You cannot just sign up and start selling. You apply, and a review team checks your application before you can sell. Many beginners fail this step, so treat it as your first job on the platform.</p>
<p>Apply as a specialist, not a generalist. A profile that says "I do writing, design, video, and admin" tells the review team nothing, and it tells buyers nothing either. Pick one service, and build the whole application around it. Use a real photo of yourself, not a logo and not a stock image. Write a human bio in plain words: what you do, who you help, and what result you deliver. Avoid grand claims and copied phrases.</p>
<p>Your portfolio is mandatory, not optional. Include two to three pieces of real work, or samples made for imaginary clients and labelled as samples. A writer can include three short posts. A designer can include three logos. A developer can include a small demo site. If your portfolio is empty, your application will almost certainly be rejected.</p>
<p>If your application is rejected, do not apply again at once with the same profile. Read the rejection message, fix the weak point, and reapply when you have something better to show. Common reasons for rejection are a vague bio, no portfolio, a generalist profile, or a photo that is not a real photo of you.</p>
<h3 id="step-2-build-a-profile-uk-buyers-trust">Step 2: Build a profile UK buyers trust</h3>
<p>Most PeoplePerHour buyers are in the United Kingdom, so your profile should speak to them. Write in clear, correct English, and use British spelling. A profile with poor grammar tells a UK buyer that you will struggle with their work, even if your skill is strong. Read your bio out loud before you publish it, and fix every error.</p>
<p>Write a micro-niche headline, not a job title. "Email copywriter for UK software firms" beats "Freelance writer". List your languages, and say which time zone you work in. UK business hours are the morning and afternoon in the UK, so say when you are online in UK time, and reply during those hours where you can. Buyers notice fast replies, and a freelancer who answers in UK business hours wins more work.</p>
<p>Add your working hours, your location, and your portfolio. Keep your profile focused on one service, and do not list skills you cannot use. A buyer who hires you for a skill you faked will leave a review that follows you for months. If you are new to freelancing itself, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-start-freelancing" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to start freelancing</a> before you build the profile.</p>
<h3 id="step-3-create-your-first-offers">Step 3: Create your first Offers</h3>
<p>An Offer is a fixed-price package that a buyer can buy without talking to you first. It is the closest thing PeoplePerHour has to a Fiverr gig, and it is how most new sellers earn their first orders. Start with one Offer, not five.</p>
<p>Pick one clear service with a fixed price and defined deliverables. "I will write three 800-word blog posts for 150 pounds, delivered in five days" is an Offer. "I will do writing work" is not. Name the price in pounds, because most buyers pay in pounds and think in pounds. Say exactly what is included: how many pieces, how many words, how many revisions, and when you will deliver. Say what you need from the buyer to start.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/peopleperhour-offer-creation.jpg" alt="A laptop showing a simple fixed-price service offer layout, with a notebook and pen on the desk" /></p>
<p>Use a good image you own. Do not use stock photos in your Offer image, because buyers can tell, and it signals that the work may not be yours either. Take your own photo, or design a simple image that shows what you deliver. Write the Offer description in plain words, with the result first and the details after. For a fuller method, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-create-a-freelance-portfolio" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to create a freelance portfolio</a>.</p>
<p>Price your Offer to cover the fee. At 20 percent on a new Buyer, a 150 pound Offer leaves you 120 pounds before any withdrawal or conversion cost. Work that maths out before you publish, not after your first sale. For a deeper method, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-set-freelance-rates" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to set freelance rates</a>.</p>
<h3 id="step-4-spend-your-15-monthly-credits-wisely">Step 4: Spend your 15 monthly credits wisely</h3>
<p>You get 15 proposal credits a month, and one credit is normally used for each Proposal. That is a hard limit, and it decides your whole approach. Do not spend them all on day one. Spread them across the month, and spend each one as if it cost real money, because a wasted credit is a lost chance.</p>
<p>Filter for UK jobs with verified payment first. Read each job post fully before you spend a credit. Skip posts with no verified payment, a budget far below the work, or a brief so vague that you cannot tell what the client wants. Skip jobs with dozens of proposals already, because your new profile will not stand out. Spend your credits on fresh jobs, posted in the last day, from clients with a history of hiring and good feedback.</p>
<p>Follow up once if a client reads your proposal and does not reply. Send one short, polite message after a few days, and then move on. Do not send three messages, and do not lower your price to chase a client who has gone quiet. A client who does not reply was never going to hire you, and chasing them wastes time you could spend on the next proposal.</p>
<p>Keep a simple record of your credits. Note which jobs you applied for, when, and what happened. After a month, you will see which kinds of jobs reply and which never do.</p>
<h3 id="step-5-write-a-proposal-that-wins">Step 5: Write a proposal that wins</h3>
<p>Your proposal is a short letter to one client about one job. Open with the client's problem, in your own words. Then give one line of relevant proof, such as a similar project you finished and what it achieved. Then say what you will do first, and end with a clear next step. Keep it under 300 words.</p>
<p>Example only: this is a structure, not a template to copy. A strong proposal has four parts. First, one sentence that restates the client's goal, which shows you read the post. Second, one sentence of proof, with a link to a relevant sample. Third, two sentences on your plan, naming the first step you would take. Fourth, one question about the project and one clear next step. Do not copy a generic template and change the client's name. UK buyers can spot a copied proposal at once, and it tells them you did not read their job.</p>
<p>Attach one relevant sample, not five. A sample that matches the job beats a portfolio dump. Answer any screening questions in full sentences, because clients use them to filter out freelancers who did not read the post. Write in clear British English, and check your spelling before you send. For a fuller method, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-write-freelance-proposals" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to write freelance proposals</a>.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/peopleperhour-workstream.jpg" alt="A freelancer checking messages and project updates on a laptop, with a planner and phone on the desk" /></p>
<h3 id="step-6-deliver-safely-in-the-workstream">Step 6: Deliver safely in the WorkStream</h3>
<p>Every project on PeoplePerHour happens inside a WorkStream. It is not only a chat box. It is the legal and financial record of your project, and it is what protects you if anything goes wrong.</p>
<p>Wait for the client to fund the Escrow before you start. The client deposits the money into Escrow when they accept your proposal, and that deposit is your safety net. If the client disappears after you deliver, PeoplePerHour can release the funds because the proof is in the WorkStream. If you start before the Escrow is funded, you have no such protection.</p>
<p>Keep every message, file, and agreement in the WorkStream. If the client asks for a change, agree it there, in writing. If the change adds real work, ask for a new milestone or a revised price before you do it. Upload your finished files to the WorkStream, not by email. When the work is done, raise an invoice through the invoice button. The client pays the invoice, and the money goes into your PeoplePerHour wallet, minus the service fee.</p>
<p>You must reply to client messages within 24 hours, and the client must reply to you within 24 hours. That rule runs both ways, and it is one reason UK buyers trust the platform. Keep your replies fast, polite, and clear, especially in your first months.</p>
<h3 id="getting-paid">Getting paid</h3>
<p>After you raise an invoice and the client pays it, the money sits in your PeoplePerHour wallet. It then enters the clearing period described above, which guides report as about 14 working days for newer freelancers. Once the money clears, you can withdraw it to PayPal, Payoneer, or a bank account. Withdrawal fees and timing vary by method and country, and PeoplePerHour shows the fee when you request the withdrawal, so check it there rather than trusting a guide.</p>
<p>Remember that the service fee excludes VAT where VAT applies, so UK and EU freelancers may owe VAT on the fee itself. Keep records of every invoice, fee, and withdrawal from day one, because tax rules differ by country and you will need the records at tax time. Talk to a qualified accountant if you are unsure how freelance income is taxed where you live.</p>
<p>Some third-party guides report an annual subscription and an inactivity fee on PeoplePerHour, but neither was verified on an official page in this run, so check the current Terms before you rely on them. Do not budget for a fee you have not seen on the platform itself.</p>
<h3 id="growing-repeat-clients-and-raising-rates">Growing: repeat clients and raising rates</h3>
<p>Repeat clients are where PeoplePerHour pays off. The fee falls as you bill more to the same Buyer: from 20 percent below 250 pounds, to 7.5 percent up to 5,000 pounds, to 3.5 percent above that. A client who hires you five times costs you less in fees than five new clients, and costs you no proposal credits at all. Ask happy clients whether they have more work, and offer a retainer for regular tasks.</p>
<p>As your reviews build, you will also start receiving invitations to jobs, which means you spend fewer credits chasing work. That is the turning point on this platform: when buyers come to you, your 15 credits stop being a limit and become a bonus. Raise your rates as reviews build, by 10 to 20 percent after three to five good reviews, and again after ten.</p>
<h3 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Pricing at the bottom to win work, which attracts cheap clients and leaves you nothing after the 20 percent fee.</li>
<li>Applying as a generalist, which fails the application and tells buyers nothing.</li>
<li>Sending generic proposals that do not answer the exact project.</li>
<li>Spending all 15 credits in the first week, then having none left when a good job appears.</li>
<li>Using stock images in your Offers, which signals that the work may not be yours.</li>
<li>Working outside the WorkStream, which removes your payment protection.</li>
<li>Starting work before the client funds the Escrow.</li>
<li>Writing proposals with poor grammar for a UK audience, which costs you the job before your skill is judged.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="how-to-make-money-on-peopleperhour-what-it-costs-and-what-to-expect">How to make money on PeoplePerHour: what it costs and what to expect</h3>
<p>Starting on PeoplePerHour costs nothing. There is no registration fee, no fee to search for projects, and no fee to post an Offer. Your costs are your time, the 15 credits you spend each month, the service fee on each job, and the wait for your money to clear. The fee is 20 percent on a new Buyer under the per-client tiers, and it falls as you bill more to the same Buyer.</p>
<p>Expect a slow first 90 days. Your first weeks are for passing the application, building your profile, and writing your first Offers. Your first months are for earning your first reviews, and many beginners earn little or nothing in that time. That is normal on a smaller marketplace. PeoplePerHour is not a volume platform. You will not get 20 orders a day. You may get two good clients a month who stay with you for a year, and that is a better result.</p>

<p>You get approved by applying as a specialist with a real photo, a human bio, and a portfolio of two to three pieces of work. The review team checks your application before you can sell, and generalist profiles with no portfolio are usually rejected. If you are rejected, fix the weak point and reapply when you have something better to show, rather than applying again at once with the same profile.</p>
<h4>How much does PeoplePerHour charge?</h4>
<p>PeoplePerHour charges a service fee based on your lifetime billing with each Buyer, excluding VAT. The official Help Center page shows 20 percent below 250 pounds, 7.5 percent from 250 to 5,000 pounds, and 3.5 percent above 5,000 pounds, as of November 2026. Third-party guides report a newer monthly scheme, so check the fee shown in your own account, because that is the fee you will actually pay.</p>
<h4>What is a PeoplePerHour Offer?</h4>
<p>An Offer is a fixed-price packaged service you create, such as writing three blog posts for a set price. Buyers browse Offers and buy the ones they need, without posting a project first. It is the closest thing PeoplePerHour has to a Fiverr gig, and it is how most new sellers earn their first orders. There is no fee to post an Offer.</p>
<h4>How many proposals can I send on PeoplePerHour?</h4>
<p>You get 15 free proposal credits at the start of each month, and one credit is normally used for each Proposal you send. Spend them carefully, on fresh jobs from clients with verified payment and a history of hiring. Do not spend them all on day one, and keep a record of which jobs reply, so you learn where your credits work best.</p>
<h4>Can I work on PeoplePerHour without experience?</h4>
<p>You need a skill you can deliver, but you do not need platform experience. Beginners win first work with a clear niche, a small portfolio of sample work labelled honestly, and proposals that show they read the job post. Sample work made for imaginary clients counts, as long as you label it as a sample. A narrow niche beats a broad profile with no focus.</p>
<h4>Is PeoplePerHour worth it in 2026?</h4>
<p>PeoplePerHour is worth trying if you have a real skill, you write clear English, and you want serious UK clients with less competition than Fiverr or Upwork. It is less suited to people who want instant income, high volume, or a quick application. The stricter application and the 15-credit limit slow you down at first, but they also keep the marketplace smaller and more serious.</p>

Apply as a specialist with a real portfolio, build a profile UK buyers trust, create one clear Offer with a fair price, and spend your 15 monthly credits only on jobs you can do well.
