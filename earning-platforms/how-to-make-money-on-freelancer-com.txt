<p>Learning how to make money on Freelancer.com starts with one honest fact. Anyone can enter, because clients post projects and freelancers bid for them. That open door is also the problem. <h3 id="how-freelancer-com-works">How Freelancer.com works</h3>
<p>Freelancer.com is a bidding marketplace. Clients post projects with a description and a budget range. Freelancers read the project, send a bid with a price and a short message, and the client picks a winner. There are three ways to earn: bid on projects, enter contests, and take hourly work.</p>
<p>In a fixed-price project, you agree a price before you start. The client funds a milestone payment, which is a portion of the total held safely until the work is done. In an hourly project, you log your hours with the desktop time tracker, and the client is billed weekly. In a contest, a client posts a brief and a prize, many freelancers submit entries, and the client picks one winner who receives the prize.</p>
<p>Payments run through SafePay, the platform's milestone payment system. The client deposits the money before you start, and Freelancer.com releases it when the work is approved. That system protects you, as long as you use it properly and never agree to work without a funded milestone. For the wider picture of the platform, read our <a href="https://earnquill.com/earning-platforms/freelancer-com-review" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Freelancer.com review</a>.</p>

In your first weeks, a small project can still be worth it for the review, but know what it costs you before you accept it.</p>
<h3 id="step-1-set-up-your-profile">Step 1: Set up your profile</h3>
<p>Your profile decides whether a client reads your bid or skips it. Start by verifying your identity. Many clients filter out unverified freelancers, because verification is the simplest way to avoid bots and fake accounts. It usually takes a day or two, so start it before you bid on anything.</p>
<p>Write a tight headline that names your niche, not your job title. "Logo designer for food brands" beats "Graphic designer". Write a short overview that says what you do, who you help, and what result you deliver. Keep it plain and specific. Add the skills that match your niche, and fill in your education and languages honestly.</p>
<p>Build a portfolio before you bid. If you have no client work yet, make sample pieces for imaginary clients, and label them as samples. A designer can make three logos for made-up firms. A writer can write three short posts for a made-up brand. A developer can build a small demo site. Contest entries also make good portfolio pieces, which is one reason contests matter for beginners. For a fuller method, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-create-a-freelance-portfolio" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to create a freelance portfolio</a>.</p>
<p>Take the skill tests that match your niche, if they are available. A passed test does not prove you are good, but it gives a new profile one more signal that a client can check. If you are new to freelancing itself, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-start-freelancing" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to start freelancing</a> first.</p>
<h3 id="step-2-learn-the-bidding-system">Step 2: Learn the bidding system</h3>
<p>Bidding is the core skill on Freelancer.com, and it works differently from Fiverr gigs or Upwork proposals. When a client posts a project, they see a list of bidders ranked by bid amount, reviews, Preferred Freelancer status, and how well each profile matches the project tags. New bidders start at the bottom of that list, so your bid message has to do more work than a top seller's bid.</p>
<p>Free members receive 6 bids a month initially, so every bid is precious. Paid memberships add more bids, but do not buy one until you know which projects are worth bidding on. Spend your first month learning the feed: read 20 projects a day, note which ones attract 100 bids and which attract 5, and watch what the winning bidders have in common. That study costs nothing and teaches you more than 6 wasted bids.</p>
<p>Check your account balance before you bid. Freelancer.com requires a minimum balance of 20 dollars before you place any bid, as of November 2026. Keep that balance topped up, and remember that the fee is charged when you accept a project, so a win also costs you money before you are paid.</p>
<h3 id="step-3-pick-the-right-projects">Step 3: Pick the right projects</h3>
<p>Most beginners lose money by bidding on the wrong projects. Use filters to find the ones worth your 6 bids. Look for projects posted in the last few hours, because fresh projects have fewer bidders and clients who are still reading bids. Look for the "Deposit Made" marker, which shows the client has funded a milestone. Look for clients with verified payment and a history of hiring, with good feedback from past freelancers.</p>
<p>Avoid the data entry trap. Projects that promise 500 dollars for simple data entry, with no clear brief and a client who joined yesterday, are almost always scams or bait. Avoid projects with 100 bids already, because your new profile will not stand out in that crowd. Avoid clients who ask you to move to Telegram or WhatsApp, ask for a security deposit, or ask you to pay for software before you start. Those are scams, and the scams section below explains them in full.</p>
<p>Pick projects that match your niche exactly, where you can point to a sample that proves you can do the job. A bid that says "I have done this exact job before, here is the sample" beats a bid that says "I can do anything". Bid on small projects first, in the 30 to 100 dollar range, where the fee is fair and the client is more willing to try a new freelancer.</p>
<h3 id="step-4-write-a-bid-that-wins">Step 4: Write a bid that wins</h3>
<p>Your bid has two parts: the price, and a short message. Both matter, and both should answer the exact project, not a generic template.</p>
<p>Open your message with the client's need, in your own words. If the client wants a logo for a coffee brand, your first sentence should say what you would make for a coffee brand, not that you are a designer with five years of experience. Ask one specific question about the project, such as which style they prefer or when they need it. A real question proves you read the brief. Then give one short piece of proof, such as a link to a similar sample. End with your price, your delivery time, and a clear next step.</p>
<p>Example only: this is a structure, not a template to copy. A strong bid has four parts. First, one sentence that restates the client's goal, which shows you read the project. Second, one question that shows you understand the job. Third, one sentence of proof, with a link to a relevant sample. Fourth, your price and delivery time, stated plainly. Keep the whole message under 150 words. Clients read dozens of bids, and long ones get skipped.</p>
<p>Price honestly. Do not bid the lowest price to win, because the lowest bidder often wins the worst clients. Bid a fair price for the work, and let your message and samples do the persuading. If your price is higher than the budget, say why in one sentence, and name what the client gets for it. For a fuller method, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-write-freelance-proposals" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to write freelance proposals</a>.</p>

<h3 id="step-5-use-contests-to-get-your-first-reviews">Step 5: Use contests to get your first reviews</h3>
<p>Contests are the best route to your first reviews on Freelancer.com, because they do not cost bids. A client posts a brief and a prize, you submit an entry, and if the client picks yours, you win the prize and a review. Even if you do not win, your entry becomes a portfolio piece.</p>
<p>Enter contests in your niche, where you can do good work fast. Read the brief twice, and follow it exactly. Clients pick entries that match what they asked for, not entries that show off a different style. Submit your best work, not a quick draft, because the client compares entries side by side. Check the prize and the fee before you enter: the contest fee is 10 percent or 5 dollars, whichever is greater, charged when the prize is released after handover.</p>
<p>Do not enter every contest you see. Pick two or three a week where your skills fit well, and do them properly. Three strong entries beat ten rushed ones. When you win, deliver the handover files promptly and politely, and ask the client for a review. That first review is the hardest to earn, and a contest win is the cheapest way to get it.</p>
<h3 id="step-6-deliver-safely-and-get-paid">Step 6: Deliver safely and get paid</h3>
<p>Never start work before the milestone is funded. When a client awards you a project, check that the milestone payment is in place before you write a word or draw a line. If the client asks you to start first and fund later, decline politely. The milestone system is what protects you, and working without it leaves you with no leverage if the client vanishes.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/freelancer-milestone-payment.jpg" alt="A laptop showing a simple milestone payment progress layout, with a notebook and pen beside it" /></p>
<p>Keep all communication on Freelancer.com. The platform's record of your messages is what decides a dispute, and messages on Telegram or email do not count. If the client asks for a change, agree it in the project chat, and ask for a new milestone if the change adds real work. Deliver on time, and tell the client what you have done at each step.</p>
<p>For hourly work, use the desktop time tracker. It records your hours and takes screenshots, and the client is billed weekly for the time you logged. Only log time you actually worked, and only with the tracker running. When a milestone is complete, ask the client to release it. If the client is slow to respond, send one polite reminder, then wait. Most clients release promptly once they see the work is done.</p>
<h3 id="scams-to-avoid">Scams to avoid</h3>
<p>Freelancer.com has more scam posts than most marketplaces, and beginners are the target. The patterns are consistent, and spotting them keeps you safe.</p>
<p>Never pay to get work. If a client asks for a security deposit, a training fee, a software licence, or any payment before you start, it is a scam. Real clients pay you, not the other way round. Never move to Telegram, WhatsApp, or email to discuss payment. Scammers use those channels because the platform cannot see them. Never share bank details, passwords, or identity documents with a client. Freelancer.com verifies your identity itself, and a client never needs your bank details.</p>
<p>Watch for the data entry trap: a high-paying data entry job with a vague brief, a new client, and a request to contact them off the platform. Watch for fake cheques and payment proofs that ask you to send money first. Watch for clients who ask you to create multiple accounts, which can get all your accounts banned. If a project feels wrong, trust that feeling, do not bid, and report it. For a wider guide to spotting bad job posts, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-jobs/how-to-spot-online-job-scams" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to spot online job scams</a>.</p>
<h3 id="growing-preferred-freelancer-and-repeat-clients">Growing: Preferred Freelancer and repeat clients</h3>
<p>The Preferred Freelancer Program is the platform's badge for proven sellers. Members get better visibility, access to Recruiter projects, and a badge that clients trust. The trade-off is the fee: Preferred Freelancer members pay 15 percent on Recruiter projects, charged when payment arrives. You cannot apply for the programme on day one. It follows a record of completed projects, good reviews, and on-time delivery. Treat it as a goal for your sixth month, not your first.</p>
<p>Repeat clients matter more than any badge. A client who hires you twice costs you no bids the second time, and the relationship lets you charge a fairer price. Ask happy clients whether they have more work, and keep in touch politely after a project ends. One steady client who sends regular work is worth more than ten one-off bids.</p>
<p>Raise your rates as reviews build. After three to five good reviews, raise your prices by 10 to 20 percent. After ten reviews, raise them again. For a deeper method, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-set-freelance-rates" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to set freelance rates</a>.</p>
<h3 id="what-it-costs-to-make-money-on-freelancer-com-and-what-to-expect">What it costs to make money on Freelancer.com, and what to expect</h3>
<p>Starting on Freelancer.com costs nothing to join, but it is not free to work. You need a 20 dollar minimum balance before you can bid, and every project you accept costs you a fee of 10 percent or 5 dollars, whichever is greater. Your first payout also waits for milestones to be released, so your first money arrives weeks after your first win.</p>
<p>Expect a slow first few months. The first month is usually spent trying to win one review, and many beginners earn nothing in that month. That is normal. Reviews are the bottleneck, and contests are the cheapest way through it. Do not trust any guide that promises a monthly income figure for your first months, because earnings depend on your skill, your niche, your prices, and how many bids you can afford. Anyone who quotes a timeline without knowing your niche is guessing.</p>
<p>Treat Freelancer.com as a small business. Bid carefully, deliver well, keep your messages on the platform, and let each review make the next bid easier. If you prefer a platform where clients post jobs and you bid with Connects, read our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/earning-platforms/how-to-make-money-on-upwork" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to make money on Upwork</a>.</p>

Bid on fresh projects with verified payment and a funded milestone, and avoid projects with 100 bids already. Your first wins usually come from small projects and contests, not from large jobs.</p>
<h4>How much does Freelancer.com charge?</h4>
<p>Freelancer.com charges 10 percent or 5 dollars, whichever is greater, on fixed-price projects, and 10 percent on hourly projects, as of November 2026. The fee is charged when you accept the project, not when you get paid. Contest fees follow the same rule, and Preferred Freelancer members pay 15 percent on Recruiter projects. Free members receive 6 bids a month initially, and you need a 20 dollar minimum balance before you can bid.</p>
<h4>How do Freelancer.com contests work?</h4>
<p>A client posts a brief and a prize, freelancers submit entries, and the client picks one winner. The winner receives the prize and a review, after a handover of the files. Contests do not cost bids, which makes them the best route to your first reviews. Enter two or three a week in your niche, follow the brief exactly, and check the prize and fee before you enter.</p>
<h4>Is Freelancer.com safe for beginners?</h4>
<p>Freelancer.com is a real marketplace, but it has more scam posts than most platforms, so beginners need to be careful. Use the milestone system, keep all messages on the platform, never pay to get work, and never move to Telegram or WhatsApp. Check that clients have verified payment and a history of hiring before you spend one of your 6 monthly bids.</p>
<h4>How many free bids do I get on Freelancer.com?</h4>
<p>Free members receive 6 bids a month initially, as of November 2026. That figure comes from the official fee page. Some older guides say 8 bids, but that figure conflicts with the official page, so use 6 when you plan your month. Paid memberships add more bids, but wait until you know which projects are worth bidding on before you pay for one.</p>
<h4>How do I withdraw money from Freelancer.com?</h4>
<p>You withdraw through the methods shown in your account, such as PayPal, bank transfer, or other options for your country. Withdrawal minimums and costs vary by method and currency, so check the official fee page for the current figures before you plan a withdrawal. Always check the fee page itself, because third-party guides often quote old figures.</p>
