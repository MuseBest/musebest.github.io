How to Earn Money on Workana in 2026

If you speak Spanish or Portuguese, or you want to work with clients in Latin America, you may have more options than the big US platforms offer. Learning how to earn money on Workana is worth your time, because Workana is the largest freelance marketplace in Latin America, and it works in Spanish, Portuguese, and English.

Workana connects businesses with remote professionals for project work in design, programming, marketing, writing, admin support, and more. Clients fund projects through escrow before you start, which protects you from non-payment. The trade-off is a tiered commission that starts high and falls as you build repeat clients, plus limits on how many proposals you can send on the free plan.

This guide explains how Workana works, how to join, how to win projects, how the fees work, and who should use it. All facts were checked on 15 November 2026.

What is Workana and how does it work

Workana, at workana.com, is a freelance marketplace founded in 2012 in Buenos Aires. Independent reviews describe it as the largest freelance marketplace in Latin America, with more than 2 million registered freelancers, more than 600,000 companies served, and more than 20,000 projects completed each month. Its strongest markets are Brazil, Argentina, Colombia, and Mexico, and it has begun expanding toward Southeast Asia.

Workana has two products. The Marketplace is the self-service freelance platform, where clients post projects and freelancers send proposals. Certified Devs is a separate service where Workana matches pre-screened engineers with US companies for longer-term roles. This guide covers the Marketplace, which is the route most freelancers use.

The Marketplace works like this. A client posts a project with a budget and a deadline. Freelancers send proposals. The client picks one, chats to agree the scope, and funds an escrow account. The freelancer does the work, the client reviews it, and then the client releases the payment. The freelancer withdraws the money to a payment method of their choice.

Projects can be fixed-price or hourly. Categories include design and multimedia, programming and technology, digital marketing and sales, writing and translation, finance and business, and admin support. Some projects are short and simple. Others are ongoing roles that last for months.

Step 1: Create a profile that gets approved

Workana curators review profiles before they go live, so your profile must be clear and complete. You can sign in with Google or LinkedIn, then add your skills, bio, portfolio samples, and hourly rate.

Pick one service and one niche first. A freelancer who says "I do content writing" competes with thousands of people. A freelancer who says "I write product pages for Shopify supplement brands" competes with far fewer and can charge more. Workana rewards clarity, not breadth.

Build your portfolio before you apply to jobs. Even if you are new, create three to five sample projects that show your skill. Writers can write sample articles or landing pages. Designers can make mockups. Developers can build a small demo site. Clients hire proof, and a profile with no proof rarely wins.

Write your bio in the language your clients use. If you target Spanish-speaking clients, write in Spanish. If you target Brazilian clients, write in Portuguese. If you target US clients, write in clear English. A profile in the wrong language tells the client you did not read their market.

Step 2: Verify your identity

Workana requires identity verification before you can bid freely. You must upload a clear photo of a government-issued ID, such as a passport or national ID card, take a live selfie that matches the document, and provide proof of address dated within the last 90 days, such as a utility bill or bank statement.

One independent test in 2026 found verification took about 18 hours. That is faster than some platforms, but it still means you cannot bid on a project posted today if you have not verified yet. Verify your account as soon as you sign up, so you are ready when a good project appears.

Your account starts with no rating and no badges, which limits how many proposals you can send each week. That is normal for new accounts. Your limits grow as you complete work and earn good ratings.

Step 3: Find projects and write proposals that win

Browse projects in your niche and filter by verified clients only. Workana labels clients with a verified payment method, such as a verified credit card or bank account. Never waste your limited weekly proposals on unverified clients. One 2026 review found that filtering out unverified clients removed most low-quality listings at once.

Read each project twice before you apply. Check the budget, the deadline, the skills needed, and whether the client has hired before. A vague project with a tiny budget and no verified payment is a bad use of a proposal. A clear project from a verified client with a realistic budget is worth your time.

Write each proposal for that job alone. Most beginners fail here, because they send generic messages such as "Hi, I can do this job." That never wins in a competitive marketplace. A winning proposal usually has four parts: one sentence that proves you understand the job, a simple plan for how you will do it, a relevant sample, and one smart question that shows you are thinking about the client's problem.

Use your first three lines well. Clients see the first sentence of your proposal before they open it, so start with a direct point or a specific solution, not with a greeting. For example: "Your checkout flow loses buyers on mobile. Here is how I would fix it in your next build." That opening earns a click. A generic greeting does not.

Step 4: Agree the scope and fund the escrow

When a client picks you, agree the scope in writing before you start. Define what you will deliver, when you will deliver it, how many revisions are included, and how payment will be handled. Use milestones for larger projects, so you get paid as you finish each part.

Never start work until the client funds the milestone in Workana's escrow account. Workana promotes a guaranteed payment system, and escrow is its core protection. But escrow only protects you if the money is actually in the account. If a client asks you to start and promises to fund it "after you show a draft," walk away. That is a classic way to get free work.

Keep all communication on the platform. If a client asks you to move to a private chat app before the contract is funded, that is a warning sign. Workana's own help centre at help.workana.com explains how projects, payments, and disputes should work.

Step 5: Deliver, get approval, and withdraw your pay

Deliver the work on time and in the format you agreed. When the client approves it, the escrow funds are released to your Workana balance. Then you withdraw the money to your payment method.

Set up your withdrawal method early, and do not change it during a payout cycle, because Workana warns that changing payout settings during a payment cycle can delay your money. Some payout providers have minimums, which can delay withdrawals if your balance is small, so check the minimums before you choose a method.

Keep records of every project: the agreed scope, the messages, the files you sent, and the approval. If a dispute arises, those records are your best protection. Escrow protects organised freelancers, not careless ones.

How you get paid on Workana

Workana charges both sides. Clients pay a service fee, and freelancers pay a tiered commission. The table below sums up the money side, based on independent reviews read on 15 November 2026.

Item What we found
Sign-up Free
Client service fee 4.5% per contract, with a minimum of $2
Freelancer commission, first $300 with a client 20%
Freelancer commission, $301 to $3,000 with a client 10%
Freelancer commission, above $3,000 with a client 5%
Payment protection Escrow. Client funds the milestone before work starts
Payout methods PayPal, Payoneer, Mercado Pago, and local bank transfer. Fees and timing vary by method and country
Payout timing Funds clear after client approval, then follow the withdrawal cycle you choose

The tiered fee is the most important number on Workana. It is charged on your lifetime billing with each client, not on your total earnings. That means every new client starts at 20%, and the fee only falls when you bill more to the same client. Repeat clients are much cheaper than new ones.

One 2026 review reports a different, newer fee scale, starting at 10% and falling to 4.5% in five smaller steps. Because sources disagree, check the current fee page on Workana before you start, and do not plan your prices on a fee you have not confirmed.

Workana also limits proposals on the free plan. One 2026 review reports five weekly proposal credits on the free plan, with paid plans adding more proposals and extra features. Plan prices vary and change often, so check the current plans page in your account before you pay for anything. Do not pay for a plan until your profile, portfolio, and proposals are already strong, because paying more only means getting rejected faster if the basics are weak.

Total cash cost to start $0, plus commission on anything you earn

Expect a slow start. New accounts have no rating, limited proposals, and heavy competition. Many clients never reply, some choose the cheapest bidder, and some post vague jobs and change the scope later. That is normal marketplace economics, not a personal failure.

Your first win matters more than your first month's income. One completed project with a good rating raises your limits and your visibility. Two or three repeat clients can change the maths completely, because your fee falls and your proposals go further.

Example only: why repeat clients matter

This is a labelled example, not a typical result. It shows the maths only.

A freelancer wins a $500 fixed-price project with a new client. The fee is tiered: 20% on the first $300, which is $60, plus 10% on the remaining $200, which is $20. The total fee is $80, so the freelancer keeps $420.

Later, the same freelancer bills more than $3,000 to that same client over time. The next $500 project is charged at 5%, which is $25, so the freelancer keeps $475. The same work pays $55 more, simply because the client is a repeat client. That is why Workana rewards long relationships and punishes constant one-off work.

Pros and cons of Workana

Pros

  • Free to join, with no upfront fee to post or search.
  • Escrow protects you when the client funds the milestone first.
  • Strong in Spanish and Portuguese markets, with less competition than US platforms.
  • Tiered fees fall to 5% with repeat clients.
  • Wide range of categories, from design to programming to admin work.
  • US companies also hire on Workana for nearshore talent in similar time zones.

Cons

  • The 20% starting fee is high, and every new client restarts it.
  • Free plan limits weekly proposals, which slows new freelancers.
  • Many clients post low budgets, especially in local currencies.
  • Disputes can lock funds in escrow for up to 14 business days.
  • No structured vetting in the Marketplace, so quality varies on both sides.
  • Payout fees and currency conversion can eat small balances.

Who Workana suits and who it does not suit

Workana suits freelancers who can work comfortably in Spanish or Portuguese, or in English with Latin American clients. It suits designers, developers, marketers, writers, and admin professionals who are willing to treat proposals like sales, not like job applications. It suits people who aim for repeat clients, because the fee falls over time and the work becomes more profitable.

It also suits freelancers in Latin America who want clients in similar time zones. For US teams, Latin America offers zero to four hours of difference from US East Coast time, which makes live calls easy. That time zone overlap is one of Workana's real advantages.

Workana does not suit people who want fast money with no learning curve. It does not suit people who hate selling themselves, who do not want to compete for attention, or who expect the platform to provide guaranteed work. It does not suit people who only want US or European clients with large budgets, because many Workana budgets reflect local purchasing power. If that is your market, read how to make money on Upwork instead, and use how to get hired for remote jobs on LinkedIn to find direct clients.

Common mistakes

  • Sending generic proposals that say nothing about the client's job.
  • Spending limited proposals on unverified clients with vague budgets.
  • Starting work before the client funds the escrow.
  • Agreeing a vague scope with no milestones or revision limits.
  • Chasing every category instead of picking one service and one niche.
  • Paying for a membership plan before the profile and proposals are strong.
  • Changing payout settings during a payout cycle, then wondering why money is late.
  • Competing on price only, which attracts the worst clients.

Risks and scams to watch for

Workana is a legitimate marketplace, but freelancers face real risks on it.

The biggest risk is scope creep. A client agrees one task, then asks for more work without more pay. A vague scope invites this problem. Write down the deliverables, the file types, the number of pages or revisions, and the acceptance criteria before you start. If it is not written, it does not exist.

Disputes are the second risk. If a client refuses to approve work or changes requirements mid-project, escrow funds can stay locked while support investigates. One 2026 review reports funds locked for up to 14 business days during disputes. Keep your messages, files, and proof of delivery on the platform, because that record decides the outcome.

Watch for off-platform scams. If a client asks you to work outside Workana, share bank details early, or pay a fee to start, walk away. Real Workana work is funded through escrow on the platform. Never pay anyone to get a job on Workana.

Be careful with currency too. Many clients budget in Argentine pesos, Brazilian reals, or other local currencies. A rate that looks fair in one currency can be poor in another. Agree the currency in writing, check the conversion rate, and remember that payout providers charge their own fees on top.

Frequently asked questions

Is Workana legit?

Yes. Workana is a real freelance marketplace founded in 2012, and independent reviews describe it as the largest in Latin America. It uses escrow to protect payments, and it is free to join. That said, it is a marketplace, not a guarantee. You can do everything right and still face clients who never reply, low budgets, and disputes.

How much does Workana charge freelancers?

Workana charges a tiered commission based on your lifetime billing with each client: 20% on the first $300, 10% on $301 to $3,000, and 5% above $3,000, according to several independent reviews. One 2026 review reports a newer scale starting at 10% and falling to 4.5%. Because sources disagree, check the current fee page on Workana before you start. Clients also pay a 4.5% service fee per contract.

How do I get paid on Workana?

The client funds the milestone in escrow before you start. When you deliver and the client approves, the funds move to your Workana balance. You then withdraw through PayPal, Payoneer, Mercado Pago, or local bank transfer. Fees and timing vary by method and country, so set your payout method early and do not change it during a payout cycle.

Can beginners make money on Workana?

Beginners can join, but it is hard. New accounts have no rating, limited weekly proposals, and heavy competition. Beginners should build a portfolio of three to five sample projects first, pick one niche, and write specific proposals. Expect slow progress at first, and judge the platform after a month of consistent effort, not after a week.

Is Workana better than Upwork?

It depends on your market. Workana has less competition for Spanish and Portuguese projects, and its fees fall to 5% with repeat clients. Upwork has more clients, larger budgets, and a variable service fee of 0% to 15% per contract, but heavier competition. Many freelancers in Latin America use Workana for regional work and Upwork for US clients. Read our Upwork guide to compare them.

What documents do I need to verify my Workana account?

You need a government-issued photo ID, such as a passport or national ID card, a live selfie that matches the document, and proof of address dated within the last 90 days, such as a utility bill or bank statement. Verification can take about a day, so complete it as soon as you sign up.

Conclusion

Workana is a legitimate route to freelance income, especially if you work in Spanish or Portuguese markets or want nearshore clients in similar time zones. Escrow protects you when it is funded first, and the tiered fee rewards repeat clients. The trade-offs are a high starting fee, limited free proposals, low budgets from some clients, and slow dispute handling.

If that fits you, build a clear profile in one niche, verify your account at once, filter for verified clients only, write specific proposals, and never start work before escrow is funded. Win one client, then turn that client into a repeat client, because that is where Workana pays best.
