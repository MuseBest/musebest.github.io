</tr>
<tr>
<td>Threat hunter</td>
<td>Searches for hidden attackers inside a network</td>
<td>Senior</td>
<td>GIAC, CISSP</td>
</tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>A SOC analyst role, short for security operations centre analyst, is the most common entry point, because the work is structured and the team trains you. A GRC analyst role, where GRC stands for governance, risk, and compliance, suits people who write well and like detail. Penetration testing and threat hunting usually need several years of hands on work first, so treat them as later goals.</p>
<h2 id="skills-employers-ask-for">Skills employers ask for</h2>
<p>Employers ask for a mix of technical skill and clear communication. You do not need every skill on the list, just enough to do the job and to show you can learn the rest.</p>
<p>Technical skills matter most. Learn how networks work, including TCP/IP, DNS, and firewalls, and learn the basics of Windows and Linux. Learn to read logs, which are records of what a system did, and learn one scripting language, such as Python or Bash. Learn the main security tools: a SIEM platform, which collects and analyses security alerts, such as Splunk or Microsoft Sentinel; a vulnerability scanner; and an endpoint detection tool. Learn the <a href="https://attack.mitre.org/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">MITRE ATT&amp;CK framework</a>, a free knowledge base of attacker tactics.</p>
<p>Soft skills matter just as much in remote work. You must write clear incident notes, ask good questions instead of guessing, manage your own time, and stay calm when an alert turns out to be a real incident. Hiring managers test these skills in interviews, so give real examples from your lab work, your studies, or any job where you solved a problem under pressure.</p>
<h2 id="certifications-that-help-and-which-ones-to-skip-at-the-start">Certifications that help, and which ones to skip at the start</h2>
<p>Certifications help you get past screening, but they are not a substitute for skill. Start with one that matches the role you want.</p>
<p>CompTIA Security+ is the best first certification for most beginners. It covers networks, threats, risk, and basic defence, and many employers list it in entry level adverts. The <a href="https://www.comptia.org/certifications/security" rel="noopener" target="_blank">CompTIA Security+ page</a> describes it as a globally recognised certification that validates practical skills, and it notes that Security+ V8 is the current version. A CySA+ certification, which stands for Cybersecurity Analyst, is a sensible second step for analyst roles. CISSP is a respected senior certification, but it asks for five years of experience, so skip it until you have the experience to back it.</p>
<p>Do not collect certifications you do not need. Each exam costs money and study time, and a long list looks unfocused. Pick one, pass it, then use the skill in a lab project you can show to employers.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-get-experience-without-a-security-job-yet">How to get experience without a security job yet</h2>
<p>Employers hire people who can show their work, not people who only list courses. A home lab is the cheapest way to build that proof: a small test network you build on your own computer with virtual machines, which are software copies of computers that run inside your real one.</p>
<p>Start with TryHackMe or HackTheBox, which are guided practice platforms where you solve security tasks in a browser. TryHackMe suits beginners, while HackTheBox suits people who already know the basics. Pick one, finish a full beginner path, and keep notes. Then build a small lab: one Linux machine, one Windows machine, and a free SIEM tool such as Wazuh. Practise reading logs, spotting a failed login attack, and writing a short incident report.</p>
<p>Add two more kinds of proof. First, take part in a CTF event, which is a capture the flag competition where teams solve security puzzles. Second, write short posts about what you learned, on a blog or on LinkedIn, so a hiring manager can see how you think. Keep each post short and focused on one problem you solved.</p>

<h2 id="where-to-find-remote-cybersecurity-jobs">Where to find remote cybersecurity jobs</h2>
<p>Use a mix of boards and direct applications. General remote boards list the most roles, but security specific boards have less competition.</p>
<p>Start with the big remote boards: We Work Remotely, Remote.co, and FlexJobs, which screen listings for legitimacy. Add LinkedIn, with a saved search and a daily alert for your target role. Add security specific boards such as CyberSecJobs. Then go direct: pick 20 companies that hire remote security staff and check their careers pages every week. Many roles appear on a company site days before they reach a board.</p>
<p>Set up alerts so new roles reach you the same day, and apply within the first 48 hours when you can, because early applicants get read first. Keep a simple tracker of company, role, and date applied. Aim for 10 focused applications a week, not 50 rushed ones.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-read-a-remote-job-advert">How to read a remote job advert</h2>
<p>Not every advert that says remote is fully remote. Read the advert in full, and check four things.</p>
<p>First, check the location line. Genuine remote roles say remote, or they name a country or region. Hybrid roles need two or three days in an office. Location restricted roles say remote but add that you must live in one state or country. Second, check the time zone. A role that needs you online during United States business hours is hard to hold from a very different time zone. Third, check the clearance line. Defence and government roles may need a security clearance or a background check, which is a formal check of your history and identity. Fourth, check the pay line, and use the salary section of this guide if the advert hides it.</p>
<p>If the advert is vague, ask the recruiter in your first message. A clear answer is a good sign.</p>
<h2 id="building-a-cv-that-passes-screening">Building a CV that passes screening</h2>
<p>Most large employers use screening software that scans your CV for keywords before a human reads it. Your CV must pass that scan and impress the human.</p>
<p>Put your strongest proof at the top: your target role, your certification, and one line about your lab work. List skills in plain words that match the advert, such as SIEM, Splunk, incident response, and vulnerability management. Use numbers where you can, such as the alerts you triaged in a lab or the CTF tasks you solved. Keep the CV to two pages, and use the same words the advert uses, because the screening software matches exact words.</p>
<p>Add a short cover note for roles you really want. Say in three sentences why this role fits you, what proof you have, and that you can work the hours the role needs. Read our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-jobs/how-to-write-cv-for-remote-jobs" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to write a CV for remote jobs</a> for the full CV structure, and our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-jobs/how-to-get-hired-for-remote-jobs-on-linkedin" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to get hired for remote jobs on LinkedIn</a> to make your profile work harder.</p>
<h2 id="interview-preparation-for-remote-security-roles">Interview preparation for remote security roles</h2>
<p>Security interviews test how you think, not just what you know. Expect technical, scenario, and remote readiness questions.</p>
<p>Technical questions cover networks, logs, and tools. You may be asked to explain what happens when a user visits a website, or to read a short log sample and say what looks wrong. Scenario questions describe a problem, such as a user who clicked a phishing link, and ask what you would do first. Answer in order: contain the problem, gather the facts, tell the right people, then fix it and write it down. Remote readiness questions ask how you work from home and how you stay in touch with a team across time zones.</p>
<p>Prepare your setup before the call: test your camera, microphone, and internet, and sit somewhere quiet with a plain background. At the end, ask two good questions, such as what the on-call rota looks like and what the team expects in the first 90 days. Read our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-jobs/how-to-pass-remote-job-interview" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to pass a remote job interview</a> for more interview preparation.</p>

After 12 weeks, the same person would have one finished learning path, one small lab, one certification attempt, and about 20 applications sent. That is a realistic pace for someone with a day job.</p>

<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h2>

<h2 id="risks-and-scams-to-watch-for">Risks and scams to watch for</h2>
<p>Job scams target people who want remote work, and cybersecurity roles are no exception. A real employer never asks you to pay for a job, a training course, or equipment before you start. A real employer never asks for your bank details, your password, or a copy of your ID in the first message.</p>
<p>Watch for adverts that promise a high salary for no experience, that use a free email address, or that ask you to buy software or a starter kit. Watch for fake check scams, where a supposed employer sends you a check and asks you to send part of it back. The <a href="https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/job-scams" rel="noopener" target="_blank">US Federal Trade Commission guide to job scams</a> explains these tricks and says that honest employers never ask you to pay to get a job. If a listing feels wrong, search the company name with the word scam, and read our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-jobs/how-to-spot-online-job-scams" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to spot online job scams</a> before you reply.</p>
<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 id="do-i-need-a-degree-to-find-remote-cybersecurity-jobs">Do I need a degree to find remote cybersecurity jobs?</h3>
<p>No, most entry level adverts ask for skills and a certification, not a degree. A degree helps, and some government roles ask for one, but many SOC analyst and GRC roles hire people with a Security+ certificate and lab work. Focus on proof first.</p>
<h3 id="can-i-get-a-remote-cybersecurity-job-with-no-experience">Can I get a remote cybersecurity job with no experience?</h3>
<p>Yes, but you need proof instead of experience. Build a home lab, finish a beginner path on TryHackMe or HackTheBox, pass Security+, and write about what you learned. Apply for entry level SOC analyst and GRC roles, and for internships and apprenticeships.</p>
<h3 id="what-certifications-do-i-need-for-a-remote-cybersecurity-job">What certifications do I need for a remote cybersecurity job?</h3>
<p>Start with CompTIA Security+, which most entry level adverts list. Add CySA+ for analyst work, or a cloud security certificate for cloud roles.</p>
<h3 id="how-much-do-remote-cybersecurity-analysts-earn">How much do remote cybersecurity analysts earn?</h3>
<p>Reported 2026 estimates from DailyRemote put entry level analysts at 65,000 to 85,000 dollars a year, mid level analysts at 90,000 to 120,000 dollars, and senior analysts at 120,000 to 155,000 dollars. These are the guide own estimates with no official source, so check the advert and several sources before you negotiate.</p>
<h3 id="how-long-does-it-take-to-get-the-first-remote-cybersecurity-job">How long does it take to get the first remote cybersecurity job?</h3>
<p>Most beginners need three to six months of study and lab work, then one to three months of applications, at about 10 hours of study a week. If you study full time it can be faster, and if you study less it will be slower. Either pace is fine if you keep going.</p>
<h3 id="is-remote-cybersecurity-work-really-fully-remote">Is remote cybersecurity work really fully remote?</h3>
<p>Many roles are fully remote, but some are hybrid or restricted to one country or time zone. Defence and government roles may also need a security clearance. Always read the location and time zone lines in the advert, and ask the recruiter if it is vague.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>To find remote cybersecurity jobs, pick one entry role such as SOC analyst or GRC analyst, learn the core skills in a home lab, pass Security+, build a CV that matches each advert, and apply for 10 focused roles a week. Check the location and time zone lines before you apply, prepare for scenario interviews, and compare salary ranges from more than one source before you negotiate.</p>
