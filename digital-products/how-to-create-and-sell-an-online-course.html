How to Create and Sell an Online Course in 2026

Learn how to create and sell an online course in 2026: plan a focused curriculum, record with simple gear, price it well, and launch to your first students.

An online course is a digital product with a promise bigger than a template or a guide: by the end, the student can do something they could not do before. That promise is why courses can charge more, and also why they fail more visibly. A course that teaches everything about a topic but changes nothing for the student will not earn repeat sales or good reviews. A focused course that takes a beginner to one clear result will.

This guide covers courses only: choosing a result worth teaching, planning a curriculum, recording with simple equipment, choosing a platform, launching to your first students, and supporting them well. It does not cover templates or ebooks in depth, which have their own guides, and it only touches pricing briefly. Read the pricing guide before you set a final price.

Start with the result, not the topic

Most first courses fail at the planning stage because the creator starts with a topic instead of a result. "Social media marketing" is a topic, and a course on it could run for twenty hours and still leave a student unsure what to do. "Plan and publish your first month of content for a small business Instagram account" is a result. A student knows whether they achieved it, and a buyer knows whether they want it.

Define your result in one sentence that names the student, the starting point, and the finish line. For example: "A beginner freelancer who has never pitched will send five tailored pitches by the end of the course." Then list the skills that result requires, in the order the student needs them. That list becomes your curriculum. Anything that does not serve the result is cut, however interesting it is. A short course that delivers its promise beats a long course that wanders.

Plan a curriculum students can finish

Completion matters more than comprehensiveness. Students who finish a course recommend it, review it, and buy the next one. Students who stall at lesson three do none of those things. Design for finishing: short lessons, clear progress, and a small win in every module.

A practical structure for a first course is four to six modules, each with three to six lessons, each lesson five to twelve minutes long. Open with a module that gives a quick, visible win, such as setting up the tool or completing the first small task. Put the hardest material in the middle, when students are already invested, and close with a module where students apply everything to their own project. End every module with one action step, written plainly: "By the end of this module, you will have drafted your first three post ideas." Action steps turn watching into doing, and doing is what produces results.

Validate the course before you record it all

Recording a full course before anyone wants it is the most expensive mistake a first course creator can make. Validate first, using the same principle as any first product: check that strangers want the result before you build the whole thing.

A simple validation route is a presell or beta launch. Outline the course, write the sales page, and offer a small number of founding student places at a lower price, with the honest explanation that the course is being recorded now and students will get early access plus input on the content. If enough people join, you have both proof and your first students. If almost nobody joins after you shared it in relevant places, you have learned something valuable before spending weeks recording.

A beta group also improves the course itself. Your first students will ask the questions your outline missed, stumble where your instructions are unclear, and tell you which lessons felt too long. Record with their feedback in mind, and you will launch a stronger course than one recorded in isolation. Treat beta students well: answer their questions promptly, credit their help, and give them the finished course and future updates. Their testimonials, given honestly and with permission, become your best sales material.

Record with simple gear and a clean process

You do not need a studio to record a good course. You need clear audio, readable visuals, and a quiet room. Audio matters most: students forgive an ordinary picture, but they abandon a course they cannot hear comfortably. A decent USB microphone, a quiet room with soft furnishings to reduce echo, and a short test recording checked on headphones will beat an expensive camera with bad sound.

Editing should be light but real: remove long pauses, mistakes, and filler, add chapter markers or section titles, and check that every lesson's audio level matches. Export at a quality the platform recommends, and watch each finished lesson once from start to finish before publishing. Students judge a course by its rough edges, and a lesson with a frozen screen or missing audio will damage trust even if the content is good.

Choose a platform that fits how you sell

Course platforms fall into two broad groups. Hosted course platforms such as Teachable, Thinkific, and Podia handle video hosting, student accounts, checkout, and progress tracking, and they charge a subscription, a transaction fee, or both. General selling platforms such as Gumroad handle checkout and delivery but offer simpler course features. Marketplaces bring their own buyers but take a larger share and control pricing and promotion more tightly.

Choose based on how you will reach students. If you already have an audience and want control over price and branding, a hosted platform or your own site with a course plugin suits you. If you have no audience and want discovery, a marketplace can bring students, but expect less control and a smaller share per sale. If you are testing a first course, a simple platform with low fixed costs is wiser than an expensive plan you must grow into.

Fees and terms change, so check each platform's live pricing and fee page before you commit, and calculate what you keep per sale at your planned price. Also check practical details: can students download lessons, does the platform handle tax collection for you, can you offer coupons and payment plans, and can you export your student list if you move later. Your student list is an asset; a platform that locks it away is a risk to your future launches.

Launch to your first students

A course launch works best as a short, planned sequence, not a single announcement. Start with the people who validated the idea: your waitlist, your beta students, and the communities where you tested the concept. Tell them when enrolment opens, what the course includes, what result it delivers, and when the launch price ends. A launch window of one to two weeks gives people time to decide without letting the announcement fade.

During launch week, teach in public. Share a free lesson, a useful tip, or a worked example that shows your teaching style and gives real value even to people who do not buy. Answer questions openly, including hard ones about who the course is not for. Honest answers build more trust than hype, and a buyer who knows the course fits them is far less likely to refund.

Support students and protect your reputation

Student support is part of the product, not an extra. State your support route and response time on the course page, and keep it. A student who waits a week for an answer will assume the course is abandoned. Answer in batches if needed, but answer reliably. Keep a list of repeated questions and turn them into a course FAQ or an added lesson. Each addition makes the course better for the next group.

Set fair boundaries. You support the course material and the stated result, not each student's entire business or unlimited one to one coaching, unless coaching is explicitly included and priced. If a student needs personal help beyond the course, you may offer it as a separate service or point them to the relevant lesson. Clear boundaries, stated kindly, protect both the student and your time.

Refunds deserve a clear, fair policy stated before purchase, such as a set number of days with reasonable conditions. A fair refund policy increases buyer confidence and reduces disputes. Handle refund requests promptly and politely, even when you disagree. One graceful refund costs less than a public complaint from a frustrated buyer.

Risks and limits

Courses take longer to build than templates or short guides, and the return is uncertain until students enrol. Recording, editing, platform fees, and support all cost time or money, and a course on a fast changing tool may need regular updates. Sales can also be uneven: launches spike, then enrolment slows until the next push. Do not borrow money or quit stable work to fund a first course, and keep your fixed costs low until enrolment proves itself.

Results vary with your topic, your audience, your teaching quality, your price, and how consistently you promote the course. Avoid income claims you cannot support, and be cautious about claims from course sellers about their own earnings. A first course that helps twenty students reach a real result, earns honest reviews, and teaches you how to teach is a genuine success, whatever the revenue. Build on that foundation with a second course or an improved version, rather than chasing a large launch before you are ready.

Frequently asked questions

How long should my first online course be?

Long enough to deliver one clear result and no longer. For most first courses, that means two to four hours of video across four to six modules, plus worksheets. A shorter course that students finish beats a longer course that students abandon.

What equipment do I need to record a course?

A decent USB microphone, a quiet room, screen recording software, and a computer that can handle recording and editing. A phone or basic webcam is enough for talking head parts at first. Spend on audio before video, and test everything with a short sample lesson before recording the full course.

How do I get students without an audience?

Validate with a beta group, share useful teaching content where your buyers gather, and consider a marketplace that brings its own buyers. Expect slower early enrolment, and build an email list from every free lesson and launch so your second course starts with an audience your first course created.

Should I presell my course before recording it?

Preselling is a sensible test if you are honest about the timeline and deliver what you promised. It proves demand before you spend weeks recording, and it gives you founding students whose feedback improves the course. Do not presell a course you are not committed to finishing on schedule.

How much should I charge for my first course?

First courses often start in a low to mid double figure range, with the exact price depending on the result, the depth, and the support included. Those are starter ranges only. Read the full pricing guide on this site and check each platform's live fee page before you set a final price.

Your next step

Your next step is to write one sentence naming your student, their starting point, and the result your course will deliver. If you cannot write that sentence, your course idea is not ready to outline yet. Once you can, list the skills the result requires, validate the idea with a beta offer, and record one module at a time.
