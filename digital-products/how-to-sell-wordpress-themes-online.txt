<h2 id="is-selling-wordpress-themes-still-worth-it">Is selling WordPress themes still worth it?</h2>
<p>Selling WordPress themes can still work in 2026, but it is not the easy income some guides suggest. The market is crowded, and buyers can choose from thousands of free and paid themes. A generic business theme with no clear audience will struggle.</p>
<p>What still sells is a theme built for one clear type of buyer. A theme made for wedding photographers, small dental clinics, or local restaurants solves a real problem, and buyers in that niche will pay for it. A theme that tries to suit everyone suits no one well enough to buy.</p>
<p>You should also know the work does not end at launch. Buyers expect updates when WordPress changes, answers to support questions, and fixes when something breaks. If you want a product you build once and never touch again, a theme is the wrong choice.</p>
<h2 id="who-buys-wordpress-themes">Who buys WordPress themes</h2>
<p>The buyer is usually not a developer. It is a small business owner, a blogger, or a freelancer who needs a website and cannot build one from scratch. They want a design that looks good, installs easily, and works with the tools they already use.</p>
<p>That matters, because it changes what you build. A buyer does not care how clever your code is. They care that the demo looks like their business, that the setup takes an afternoon and not a week, and that help is available when they get stuck.</p>
<h2 id="step-1-pick-a-narrow-niche">Step 1: Pick a narrow niche</h2>
<p>A niche is a specific type of buyer plus a specific problem. "A business theme" is not a niche. "A fast booking theme for small dental clinics" is a niche, because the buyer is clear and the features are clear.</p>
<p>Pick a niche where you understand the buyer. If you have worked in hospitality, themes for guesthouses and cafes are a natural fit. If you photograph weddings, themes for photographers are. Your background helps you choose the right demo content and the right words on the listing page.</p>
<p>Check the gap before you build. Search the marketplaces for your niche and read the top themes. Note what they include, what buyers complain about in reviews, and what is missing. If five strong themes already serve your niche well, pick a narrower corner of it.</p>
<h2 id="step-2-build-a-theme-worth-paying-for">Step 2: Build a theme worth paying for</h2>
<p>A paid theme must be clearly better than the free options. That means a polished design, a working demo with realistic content, clean code, and documentation that a non-technical buyer can follow.</p>
<h3 id="classic-theme-or-block-theme">Classic theme or block theme</h3>
<p>WordPress now offers two ways to build a theme. A classic theme uses PHP templates and the Customizer, and it suits buyers who want a fixed design with simple settings. A block theme uses the Site Editor, and it suits buyers who want to move blocks around without code.</p>
<p>If you are new to theme building, a block theme is often the faster path, because it needs less PHP and leans on the built-in editor. If you are a developer, a classic theme gives you more control. Either way, follow the official standards in the <a href="https://developer.wordpress.org/themes/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">WordPress Theme Developer Handbook</a>.</p>
<p>If you cannot code at all, you still have options. You can learn the basics first with our guide to <a href="https://earnquill.com/digital-skills/how-to-learn-web-design-for-beginners" rel="noopener" target="_blank">learn web design for beginners</a>, or you can start with a simpler product instead, such as <a href="https://earnquill.com/digital-products/how-to-sell-templates-online" rel="noopener" target="_blank">selling templates online</a>. Do not sell a theme you cannot fix when it breaks.</p>
<h3 id="what-to-include-in-the-package">What to include in the package</h3>
<p>A complete theme package includes the theme files, a demo with realistic content, step-by-step setup documentation, and a clear statement of what support covers. Add a list of recommended plugins, and say which ones are required.</p>

<h2 id="step-3-test-the-theme-before-you-sell-it">Step 3: Test the theme before you sell it</h2>
<p>Never sell a theme you have only tested on your own computer. Install it on a fresh WordPress site, import the demo content, and click through every page.</p>
<p>Use the Theme Unit Test data, which is a standard set of test content from WordPress, and check that posts, pages, comments, images, and widgets all display correctly. Test on a phone and a tablet, and test with the most common plugins your buyers will use, such as a contact form and an SEO plugin.</p>
<p>Write down every bug you find and fix it before launch. A theme with a broken demo or a broken install will get one-star reviews that stay visible for years.</p>
<h2 id="step-4-choose-where-to-sell-it">Step 4: Choose where to sell it</h2>
<p>You have three main routes, and you can use more than one. The right choice depends on how much traffic you can bring yourself and how much support you want to handle.</p>
<h3 id="themeforest-and-other-marketplaces">ThemeForest and other marketplaces</h3>
<p><a href="https://themeforest.net/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">ThemeForest</a> is the largest marketplace for WordPress themes, and it brings buyers to you. That traffic is the main benefit, and the cost is the marketplace share of each sale plus strict review standards. Your theme must pass review before it lists, and that review can take days.</p>
<p>Creative Market and TemplateMonster are smaller options, and they may suit a design-led theme better. Check each marketplace's current terms before you commit, because fees change.</p>
<p>One warning on fees: sources disagree about the current Envato author fee. Some 2026 guides still show the old tiered rates, while others say Envato moved to a flat rate in July 2026. Do not price your theme on either figure. Check the live author fee page on Envato's own site before you set your price, and check it again before you change your price.</p>
<h3 id="the-free-wordpress-org-directory-as-a-funnel">The free WordPress.org directory as a funnel</h3>
<p>The <a href="https://wordpress.org/themes/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">WordPress Theme Directory</a> lists free themes, and it is one of the best funnels a theme seller can use. You submit a free lite version of your theme, and buyers who like it can upgrade to your paid Pro version on your own site.</p>
<p>The review process is strict, and your theme must meet the directory rules on code, licensing, and accessibility. A free version with thousands of active installs is strong proof that buyers trust your work.</p>
<h3 id="your-own-website-with-woocommerce-or-easy-digital-downloads">Your own website with WooCommerce or Easy Digital Downloads</h3>
<p>Selling from your own site keeps the most money from each sale, but it brings no buyers on its own. You need your own traffic, which usually comes from a free theme, content, or an audience you already have.</p>
<p>WooCommerce and Easy Digital Downloads are the two common WordPress tools for selling a theme from your own site. Both can handle licence keys, automatic updates, and payments. If you want a simpler hosted checkout, our guide to <a href="https://earnquill.com/digital-products/how-to-launch-digital-product-on-gumroad" rel="noopener" target="_blank">launch a digital product on Gumroad</a> shows the steps, and the same idea works for themes.</p>

<h2 id="step-5-price-the-theme">Step 5: Price the theme</h2>
<p>Most single WordPress themes sell for 39 to 79 US dollars for a one-year licence, and many sellers offer a higher tier for unlimited sites. That range is a starting point, not a rule. Your price should reflect your niche, your support load, and what similar themes charge.</p>

<p>Do not price at the bottom to win sales. A 19 US dollar theme attracts buyers who expect full support for very little money, and support is the real cost. Price for the support you will actually give, and say clearly what the licence includes.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-set-up-licences-updates-and-support">Step 6: Set up licences, updates, and support</h2>
<p>A licence key is a code that proves a buyer paid, and it controls access to updates and support. You need a system for it before launch, not after. WooCommerce and Easy Digital Downloads both have licence add-ons.</p>
<p>Automatic updates matter. Buyers expect to update from their WordPress dashboard, and a theme that needs manual file uploads will earn complaints. Set up the update server before your first sale, and test it with a real purchase.</p>
<p>Decide your support boundaries in writing: how many sites a licence covers, how long support lasts, your response time, and what is not included, such as custom design work. Keep a small FAQ on your site for the questions that repeat.</p>
<p>Refunds need a clear policy too. Say whether you refund, in what cases, and what happens to the licence after a refund. Deactivate the licence key when you refund, so the buyer cannot keep getting updates for free.</p>
<h2 id="gpl-licensing-in-plain-words">GPL licensing, in plain words</h2>
<p>WordPress themes fall under the GPL licence, which is the open licence WordPress itself uses. In plain words, the PHP code of a theme must be GPL, which means buyers can use it, change it, and share it. Images, fonts, and other assets can carry their own licences, but you must check each one.</p>
<p>Audit every bundled asset before you sell. A photo, icon, or font with the wrong licence can force you to remove it. Use assets with licences that allow commercial use in a product for sale, and keep a record of where each asset came from.</p>
<p>Marketplaces have their own rules on top of GPL, so read the rules for the channel you choose. If you are unsure, ask a lawyer who knows software licensing, because a mistake here is expensive to fix later.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-get-your-first-sales">How to get your first sales</h2>
<p>Your first sales will come from people who already know your work, not from strangers. Launch to your email list, your social followers, and the community in your niche. A short launch post that shows the demo and names the problem it solves works better than a long feature list.</p>
<p>A free lite version in the WordPress.org directory is the slowest but most reliable route to first sales. It takes time to build installs, but each install is a potential upgrade. <a href="https://earnquill.com/digital-products/how-to-sell-digital-products-with-pinterest" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Promote digital products with Pinterest</a> to bring steady traffic to your demo pages.</p>
<h2 id="what-it-costs-and-what-to-expect">What it costs and what to expect</h2>
<p>Example only: if you sell a theme for 59 US dollars and make 10 sales in a month, that is 590 US dollars in gross sales. 10 multiplied by 59 equals 590. That is gross sales, not profit. The marketplace share and payment fees all come off that figure, so check the live fee page before you plan around a number.</p>
<p>Your costs are mostly time. Building a good theme takes weeks to months, testing and documentation take another one to two weeks, and support takes one to three hours a week once sales begin.</p>
<p>Expect a slow start. A new theme with no reviews can take months to get its first sale on a marketplace, and a free version can take months to build installs. If you need income this month, theme selling is the wrong choice.</p>
<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Building a generic theme for everyone instead of a focused theme for one niche.</li>
<li>Skipping the Theme Unit Test data and shipping a theme with broken layouts.</li>
<li>Launching without a licence and update system, then scrambling to add one after the first sale.</li>
<li>Pricing on old fee figures instead of checking the live author fee page.</li>
<li>Offering unlimited support with no boundaries, so a cheap sale costs hours of work.</li>
<li>Writing documentation that only a developer can follow, when the buyer is a business owner.</li>
<li>Bundling images or fonts without checking their licences.</li>
<li>Promising features in the listing that the theme does not yet have.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="risks-and-things-to-watch-for">Risks and things to watch for</h2>
<p>Pirated copies of paid themes are common, and you cannot stop them fully. A licence system limits updates to paying buyers, which is the main protection. Do not pay for a service that promises to remove every pirated copy, because that promise cannot be kept.</p>
<p>Watch out for "theme review" services that charge a fee to approve your theme, because real marketplaces and the WordPress.org directory do not charge for review. Also be careful with buyers who ask for custom work before paying, or for a free copy in exchange for a review.</p>
<p>Tax is a real risk if you sell worldwide. Different countries tax digital products differently, and some marketplaces handle it for you while others do not. A merchant of record can help.</p>
<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 id="is-selling-wordpress-themes-profitable">Is selling WordPress themes profitable?</h3>
<p>It can be, if you pick a narrow niche, build a theme that is clearly better than the free options, and support it well. Most new themes earn little in the first months, and income grows slowly as reviews and installs build.</p>
<h3 id="how-much-can-you-make-selling-wordpress-themes">How much can you make selling WordPress themes?</h3>
<p>There is no typical figure, because results depend on price, channel, niche, and reviews. A theme that sells 10 copies a month at 59 US dollars brings 590 US dollars in gross sales before fees, but that is an example only, not a promise.</p>
<h3 id="do-i-need-coding-skills-to-sell-a-wordpress-theme">Do I need coding skills to sell a WordPress theme?</h3>
<p>Yes, unless you work with a developer. You need enough skill to build the theme and to fix it when it breaks. A block theme needs less code than a classic theme, and <a href="https://earnquill.com/digital-skills/how-to-learn-web-design-for-beginners" rel="noopener" target="_blank">learn web design for beginners</a> is a good place to start. If you cannot code, start with templates instead.</p>
<h3 id="can-i-sell-themes-in-the-wordpress-org-directory">Can I sell themes in the WordPress.org directory?</h3>
<p>You cannot sell in the directory itself, because it lists free themes only. You can submit a free lite version there and sell a paid Pro version from your own site, and that is a proven route. Your free theme must pass the directory review, which checks code, licensing, and accessibility.</p>
<h3 id="what-is-the-difference-between-selling-on-a-marketplace-and-your-own-site">What is the difference between selling on a marketplace and your own site?</h3>
<p>A marketplace brings buyers to you and takes a share of each sale, while your own site keeps more of each sale but brings no buyers. Most sellers start on a marketplace or with a free version, then move buyers to their own site once they have a reputation.</p>
<h3 id="can-i-offer-subscription-pricing-for-a-wordpress-theme">Can I offer subscription pricing for a WordPress theme?</h3>
<p>Yes. Most sellers use an annual licence, which is a subscription in practice: the buyer pays each year to keep getting updates and support. If the buyer stops paying, they can keep using the theme, but they lose updates and support.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion-how-to-sell-wordpress-themes-online-in-2026">Conclusion: how to sell WordPress themes online in 2026</h2>
<p>That is how to sell WordPress themes online in 2026: pick one narrow niche, build a theme that is clearly better than the free options, test it with real content, choose a channel that fits your traffic, price for the support you will give, and set up licences and updates before your first sale.</p>
