How to Start a Travel Blog and Make Money in 2026

Learning how to start a travel blog and make money is a good goal, but the honest answer starts with a warning. A travel blog can earn real money, yet almost no new travel blog earns anything in its first six months. The blogs that do earn have three things in common: a clear niche, at least 20 useful posts, and steady traffic from Google or Pinterest. This guide shows you how to build all three, in order, without paying for an expensive course.

This guide is for beginners who love travel and can write clearly. You will learn how travel blogs earn, how to pick a niche, how to set up WordPress, what to publish first, how to grow traffic, and when to add each income stream.

How travel blogs make money

A travel blog earns in five main ways. Most blogs start with one, then add the others as traffic grows.

Income method How it works When it starts to pay
Affiliate links You link to hotels, tours and gear, and earn a commission when a reader books or buys From your first posts, but only a few dollars until traffic grows
Display ads An ad network places adverts on your pages and pays you per 1,000 views After you have steady traffic, usually 10,000 sessions a month or more
Sponsored posts A brand pays you to write about a hotel, tour or product After you have traffic and an audience a brand wants to reach
Own products You sell guides, itineraries, presets or a course After readers trust you, usually in year two
Services You offer trip planning, writing or photography for clients As soon as you have samples to show

Affiliate income is the usual first step, because it needs no minimum traffic. Display ads usually pay more once traffic grows, but they need volume. Sponsored posts can pay well, yet they need an audience first. Own products earn the most per sale, but they take the longest to build. For the wider picture of blog income, read how to make money blogging.

Step 1: Pick a niche you can write 50 posts about

A niche is the narrow topic your blog covers. Broad travel blogs struggle, because they compete with huge sites on every topic. A narrow blog can rank faster, because Google sees it as the expert on one subject.

Before you pick a niche, ask three questions. Can you write 50 posts about it without running out of ideas? Do readers search for it, and do they spend money on it? And do you enjoy it enough to write about it for two years?

Niche idea Example posts Why it can earn
Budget travel in Southeast Asia Hostel guides, bus routes, daily budgets Affiliate links for hostels, tours and insurance
Solo female travel Safety guides, packing lists, city guides Affiliate links for gear, insurance and tours
Family travel with young children Kid friendly hotels, flight tips, itineraries Affiliate links for hotels, flights and gear
Hiking and national parks Trail guides, gear lists, permit advice Affiliate links for gear and guidebooks
Food travel in one country Street food guides, restaurant lists, markets Affiliate links for food tours and hotels
Slow travel and remote work Monthly costs, visas, coworking guides Affiliate links for insurance, stays and tools

Pick one niche and stay with it for your first 20 posts. You can widen the topic later, once Google trusts your blog. For the full method, read how to choose a blog niche.

Step 2: Choose a name and domain

Your blog name should be short, easy to spell, and easy to say out loud. Avoid names you will outgrow, such as a name tied to one trip or one age group. A name such as "Travels with Toddlers" stops working when the children grow up, and a name tied to one country stops working when you travel elsewhere.

Check three things before you buy a domain. First, the exact domain name should be free, ideally with a .com ending. Second, the name should be free as a social media handle on Pinterest and Instagram, because you will use those to grow traffic. Third, the name should not clash with a known brand or trademark.

Buy the domain from a domain registrar, and keep the registration in your own name. A domain costs about 10 to 15 US dollars a year. Do not buy extra services the registrar offers at checkout, such as premium email or site security, until you know you need them.

Step 3: Set up WordPress and hosting

Most travel bloggers use WordPress.org, which is self-hosted WordPress. It is not the same as WordPress.com. WordPress.org gives you full control over adverts, affiliate links and design, which is what you need to earn. WordPress.com on a free plan limits adverts and plugins, so it is a poor choice for a money blog.

You need two things: a domain name and web hosting. Hosting is the rented server space where your blog files live. Most hosts offer a one-click WordPress install, which sets up the blog for you in a few minutes.

Item Typical cost, year one Typical cost, later years
Domain name 10 to 15 US dollars 10 to 15 US dollars a year
Shared hosting 35 to 60 US dollars, first year offer 80 to 120 US dollars a year
Premium theme 0 to 60 US dollars, one-off 0 US dollars, unless you renew support
Backup plugin 0 US dollars, free options exist 0 US dollars
Total About 45 to 135 US dollars About 90 to 135 US dollars a year

Watch out for first-year hosting offers. Many hosts advertise a low first-year price, then charge two or three times more when you renew. Check the renewal price before you buy, and set a calendar reminder for the renewal date. For the full setup walkthrough, read how to start a blog.

After WordPress is installed, add a simple theme that loads fast and works well on phones. Then install only the plugins you need: a backup plugin, a security plugin, an SEO plugin, and an image compression plugin. Too many plugins slow your site down.

Step 4: Create the essential pages and your first 20 posts

Before you publish your first post, create five essential pages. The About page says who you are and what the blog covers. The Contact page gives readers a way to reach you. The Privacy Policy page explains what data you collect, which matters once you run adverts. The Affiliate Disclosure page says you may earn a commission from links, which is a legal duty in many countries. The Start Here page points new readers to your best posts.

Then plan your first 20 posts. Do not publish one post and wait. Google needs a library of content before it trusts a new blog, and readers need a reason to stay. Aim for 20 useful posts before you apply for adverts or pitch a brand.

A good first 20 looks like this: five destination guides, five practical how-to posts, five lists or comparisons, and five posts that answer common questions in your niche. Each post should solve one clear problem, such as "how to get from Bangkok airport to the city by train" or "the cheapest way to visit the temples of Angkor". Write posts that help a real traveller, not posts that show off your trip.

Use your own photos where you can. Phone photos are fine if they are sharp and well lit. Name each photo file clearly, and write alt text that describes what the photo shows. Alt text is the short description that helps search engines and screen readers understand an image.

Step 5: Learn basic travel SEO

SEO, or search engine optimisation, is the work of making your posts easy for Google to find and rank. Most travel blog traffic comes from Google, so this step matters more than social media in the long run.

Start with keyword research. A keyword is the phrase a reader types into Google. Use free tools such as Google autocomplete, the People Also Ask box, and Google Search Console once your blog is live. Look for phrases with a clear question, such as "best time to visit Kyoto" or "how much does a week in Vietnam cost". Avoid broad phrases such as "Vietnam travel", because huge sites already rank for them. For the full method, read keyword research for a blog.

Then write the post to match the question. Put the main phrase in the title, in the first paragraph, and in one heading. Use clear headings that describe each section. Add a table for costs or comparisons, because readers and Google both like clear data. Link to your own related posts where it helps the reader, and link to official sources such as a national park page or a transport operator for facts such as prices and opening times. For the writing craft, read how to write blog posts that rank on Google.

Check your facts before you publish. Transport prices, visa rules and opening times change often. If you cannot verify a price, say so, and link to the official page where the reader can check it.

Step 6: Grow traffic with Pinterest, email and guest posts

Google traffic takes months to build, so use Pinterest and email in the meantime. Pinterest works well for travel, because readers use it to plan trips. Create a business account, make vertical pins for each post, and link every pin to the post, not to your homepage. Many travel bloggers report that Pinterest brings half or more of their early traffic. For the method, read how to make money on Pinterest.

Start an email list early, even when you have ten readers. Offer one useful freebie, such as a packing checklist or a one-page itinerary, in exchange for an email address. Email readers are the most loyal traffic you have, because you can reach them whenever you publish.

Guest posting is the third traffic lever. Writing a post for a bigger travel blog puts your name in front of its readers and earns you a link back to your blog. For the full process, read how to guest post for blog growth.

Step 7: Monetise in order, from affiliate links to your own products

Add income streams in this order, and do not rush it. A blog with five posts and no traffic earns nothing from adverts, and brands will not pay for a sponsored post on a blog nobody reads.

First, add affiliate links to posts that already get traffic. Link to hotels, tours, insurance and gear you genuinely recommend. Booking.com, GetYourGuide and Amazon Associates are common starting points for travel bloggers. Only recommend things you would use yourself, and mark affiliate links clearly.

Second, apply for display ads once you have steady traffic. Google AdSense has no traffic minimum, and its eligibility page asks only for your own unique content, compliance with its programme policies, and an age of 18 or over. Read the Google AdSense eligibility page before you apply. Premium networks pay more, but they need more traffic. As of November 2026, Mediavine's main programme asks for at least 5,000 US dollars in annual ad revenue, not a session count, and its Journey programme opens at 1,000 sessions from the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom or Australia in 30 days. Raptive asks for at least 25,000 pageviews a month, with at least half of that traffic from the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, New Zealand or Australia. Check the live requirements on Mediavine's application page and Raptive's application page before you apply, because these figures changed in 2025 and 2026 and many guides still print the old ones. For the full display ads guide, read how to monetise a blog with display ads.

Third, pitch sponsored posts once you have a media kit. A media kit is a short document with your traffic, your audience, and your rates. Start with small brands and tourism boards in your niche, and charge for your time and your audience, not for a free trip alone.

Fourth, build your own product. A detailed itinerary, a city guide or a set of photo presets can earn more per sale than any advert. Build the product only after readers ask you for it, because that proves there is demand.

Worked example: what a small travel blog could earn

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Imagine a travel blog with 30,000 pageviews a month. At an RPM of 15 US dollars, which means 15 US dollars earned per 1,000 pageviews, display ads earn 30 times 15, which is 450 US dollars a month. Affiliate links earn 200 US dollars a month from hotel and tour bookings. Sponsored posts earn nothing yet, because the blog is still small. Total income is 650 US dollars a month. Hosting costs 10 US dollars a month, so the blog keeps 640 US dollars before tax. If the blogger spends 40 hours a month on the blog, that is 16 US dollars per hour. The same blog at 5,000 pageviews a month would earn about 75 US dollars from ads at the same RPM, which shows why traffic matters more than any single income stream.

What it costs and what to expect

Your money costs are low. Your time cost is high. Expect to spend 10 to 20 hours a week on writing, photos and promotion for at least a year.

Period What to do What income to expect
Months 0 to 3 Set up the blog, publish 20 posts, start Pinterest 0 US dollars
Months 3 to 6 Publish 2 posts a week, build an email list 0 to 50 US dollars a month, mostly affiliate
Months 6 to 12 Apply for AdSense, pitch small brands 50 to 200 US dollars a month
Year 2 50 to 100 posts live, apply for a premium ad network 300 to 800 US dollars a month
Years 2 to 3 Add products and sponsored posts 1,000 US dollars a month or more, if traffic keeps growing

These figures are typical ranges reported by travel blogging guides, not promises. Your results depend on your niche, your traffic, your country, and how much time you put in. Most travel blogs never reach year two, because the owner stops publishing. The blogs that earn are usually the ones that kept publishing when nothing was happening.

Note for the reader: tax rules differ by country. Keep records of your income and your costs from day one, and check your local tax authority or a qualified accountant before your first tax deadline. Earning results vary; nothing on this site is financial advice.

Common mistakes

  • Picking a broad niche such as "travel the world", then competing with huge sites on every post.
  • Publishing five posts and applying for adverts, then feeling disappointed when nothing earns.
  • Writing diary posts about your trip, instead of guides that answer a reader's question.
  • Copying photos from other sites, which breaks copyright rules and can get your blog taken down.
  • Accepting a free trip in exchange for a post, then finding the brand controls what you can say.
  • Ignoring Pinterest and email, then depending only on Google from day one.
  • Buying a paid course before publishing ten posts, instead of learning by writing.
  • Hiding affiliate links, which breaks trust and can break disclosure rules.

Risks and scams to watch for

Watch for paid travel blog courses that promise income in 90 days. No course can promise that, because income depends on traffic, and traffic takes months to build. Watch for fake press trips, where a company charges you a fee to join a trip that should be free for press. Real press trips do not ask the blogger to pay. Watch for pay-to-play brand deals, where a brand asks you to pay for a product and then write about it. A genuine sponsorship pays you, not the other way round.

Also watch for scams aimed at new bloggers. Never pay for a guest post on a site you have never heard of. Never share your login details with an "SEO expert" who contacts you out of the blue. And never click your own adverts, because ad networks can close your account for invalid clicks. If a job or brand deal looks too good to be true, read how to spot online job scams before you reply.

Frequently asked questions

Do travel blogs make money in 2026?

Yes, but slowly. A travel blog earns from affiliate links, display ads, sponsored posts and its own products, and each stream needs traffic first. Most new blogs earn nothing for six months, then a small amount that grows as the post library grows.

How much do travel bloggers earn?

There is no reliable typical figure, because earnings depend on traffic, niche and country. Guides report wide ranges, from nothing in the first year to a few thousand US dollars a month for established blogs with 100,000 pageviews or more. Treat every figure you read as a report, not a promise.

Can I start a travel blog for free?

You can start on a free platform, but a free blog limits adverts and plugins, so it is hard to earn from it. A self-hosted WordPress blog costs about 45 to 135 US dollars in year one, which is the cheapest realistic start.

How many pageviews do I need to make money travel blogging?

Affiliate income can start with very little traffic, if your posts answer buying questions. Display ads usually need at least 10,000 sessions a month to pay a useful amount, and premium networks need more. A blog with 30,000 pageviews a month at a 15 US dollar RPM earns about 450 US dollars a month from ads.

What is the best platform for a travel blog?

Self-hosted WordPress, known as WordPress.org, is the best choice for most travel bloggers, because it gives you full control over adverts, affiliate links and design. Free builders and hosted platforms limit what you can earn.

How long before a travel blog makes money?

Plan for six to twelve months before the first real income, and one to two years before it pays a useful amount. Blogs that publish two useful posts a week and promote them on Pinterest usually get there faster than blogs that publish once a month.

Conclusion: pick a niche, publish 20 posts, then monetise

Learning how to start a travel blog and make money comes down to three moves. Pick a narrow niche you can write 50 posts about, publish 20 useful posts that answer real traveller questions, and add income streams in order as traffic grows. Do not buy a course, do not chase free trips, and do not stop publishing when the first months earn nothing.
