37:T4bef,<h2 id="how-to-use-ai-to-write-blog-posts-in-2026">How to Use AI to Write Blog Posts in 2026</h2>
<p>Learning how to use AI to write blog posts is worth your time, because the tools can cut drafting time in half. The trap is to believe that the tool does the writing. It does not. It produces a first draft, and you do the thinking: the question, the facts, the examples, and the editing. A blog post written this way can rank and can sound like you. A post published as raw AI output usually sounds flat, gets facts wrong, and helps no one.</p>
<p>This guide shows you the workflow, not the shortcut. You will learn what Google actually says about AI content, how to choose a tool, the seven step workflow, a prompt library you can copy, how to fact-check, what to tell readers, and what it costs.</p>

<h3 id="what-google-actually-says-about-ai-content">What Google actually says about AI content</h3>
<p>Google's position is clear, and it is good news for bloggers who use AI well. Google says that appropriate use of AI or automation is not against its guidelines, and that AI content gets no special treatment. In Google's own words, it is just content. What matters is whether the content is helpful, original and written for people.</p>
<p>The rule that does matter is the spam policy on scaled content abuse. Google defines this as many pages generated mainly to manipulate rankings and not help users, including using generative AI tools to generate many pages without adding value. So one careful AI-assisted post is fine. A hundred thin AI posts published in a day is not. Read the <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Google guidance on generative AI content</a> and the <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Google spam policies</a> yourself before you build a workflow around AI.</p>
<p>Google also warns that AI models do not retrieve facts. They predict a likely sequence of words, so their outputs can contain inaccuracies, which are often called hallucinations. Google says it is critical to manually fact-check and review all AI-generated content before publishing, including title elements, meta descriptions, structured data and image alt text. That one line is the whole job: you are the fact checker, and the tool is not.</p>
<p>An old quote still causes confusion. In 2022, a Google spokesperson said AI-generated text could count as spam. Google changed that position in February 2023, and the current guidance is the one quoted above. If you read a guide that cites the 2022 quote as the current rule, that guide is out of date.</p>

<td>Gemini</td>
<td>Yes, with limits</td>
<td>Research summaries, drafts inside Google Docs</td>

<p>ChatGPT is the most flexible all-rounder, and it is the tool most readers already know. Claude is often better at long articles and at editing, because it keeps a steady tone over a long draft. Gemini is the easiest choice if you write in Google Docs, because it sits inside the tools you already use.</p>
<p>Do not pay for a specialist AI writing tool until you have published ten posts with a general tool. Specialist tools add templates and SEO scores, but they do not add judgement, and judgement is the part that matters. For the ranking craft itself, which this guide does not repeat, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/blogging/how-to-write-blog-posts-that-rank-on-google" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to write blog posts that rank on Google</a>.</p>
<h3 id="the-7-step-workflow">The 7 step workflow</h3>
<p>Use the same seven steps for every post, so nothing gets missed. The tool does steps three and four. You do the rest.</p>
<h4>Step 1: Pick one question a real reader asked</h4>
<p>Start with a question, not a prompt. A good question comes from a real reader, a customer, a comment, or a search phrase you found in keyword research. Write the question down in one sentence, and name the reader it is for. For the research method, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/blogging/keyword-research-for-a-blog" rel="noopener" target="_blank">keyword research for a blog</a>.</p>
<h4>Step 2: Dump what you know, in your own words</h4>
<p>Before you open the AI tool, spend ten minutes writing down what you know. Use a voice memo or a rough note. List the facts, the examples, the mistakes you have seen, and the opinion only you hold. This is the material that makes the post yours. If you skip this step, the AI will fill the gap with generic text, and the post will sound like everyone else's.</p>
<h4>Step 3: Ask for an outline, not an article</h4>
<p>Give the tool your question and your notes, and ask for an outline only. Then edit the outline yourself. Cut the headings that add nothing, reorder the rest, and add the heading the tool missed. A good outline has five to eight headings, and each one answers part of the reader's question.</p>
<h4>Step 4: Draft one section at a time</h4>
<p>Ask the tool to draft one section, not the whole post. Give it the heading, your notes for that section, and the tone you want. When the draft comes back, read it, cut the filler, and add your own detail. Then move to the next section. Drafting in sections keeps the quality steady, because the tool cannot drift off topic the way it does in a long single draft.</p>
<h4>Step 5: Add what only you could write</h4>
<p>Now add the parts no AI tool can write: your examples, your prices, your mistakes, your opinion, and your answer to the reader's real worry. This is the section that makes the post worth reading. If you cannot add anything the tool could not have written, the post is not ready.</p>
<h4>Step 6: Check every fact before you publish</h4>
<p>Check every number, date, name, price, quote and link against a page you opened yourself. Mark any claim you cannot verify with a [CHECK] note, and either verify it or cut it. This step is covered in full below, because it is where most AI-assisted posts fail.</p>
<h4>Step 7: Read it out loud, then publish</h4>
<p>Read the whole post out loud. If a sentence sounds stiff, rewrite it. If a paragraph says nothing new, cut it. Then finish the SEO: title, meta description, internal links, and image alt text. Publish, and note the date, so you can update the post when facts change.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/ai-writing-fact-check-notes.jpg" alt="Hands holding a red pen over a printed blog draft, with a notebook, a laptop and a reference book on the desk" /></p>
<h3 id="prompt-library-copy-and-paste-prompts-for-each-step">Prompt library: copy and paste prompts for each step</h3>
<p>Good prompts are specific. They name the reader, the question, and the shape of the answer. Copy these, adapt them to your topic, and keep the ones that work.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/ai-writing-prompt-library-desk.jpg" alt="A notebook open to a neat list of writing prompts beside a laptop showing a chat window, with a pen and a coffee cup on a wooden desk" /></p>
<p>For topic ideas: "Give me 10 blog post ideas for readers who [describe the reader]. Each idea should answer one clear question, and say what the reader will be able to do after reading it."</p>
<p>For an outline: "Here is my question: [question]. Here are my notes: [paste your notes]. Make an outline with 5 to 8 headings. Each heading should answer part of the question. Do not write the article, only the outline."</p>
<p>For drafting one section: "Write the section called [heading] for a blog post about [topic]. The reader is [describe the reader]. Use my notes: [paste notes for this section]. Keep it under 250 words. Use short sentences and plain words. Do not invent statistics, quotes or examples."</p>
<p>For editing: "Edit this draft for clarity. Cut filler, shorten long sentences, and keep the meaning. Do not add new facts. Here is the draft: [paste draft]."</p>
<p>For titles and meta descriptions: "Suggest 5 titles under 60 characters for a blog post about [topic], and a meta description under 160 characters for each one. Do not use hype words."</p>
<p>For a first paragraph: "Write a first paragraph of 50 to 90 words that answers this question directly: [question]. Start with the reader's problem, not a greeting."</p>
<p>Never paste private client data, passwords, or personal details into an AI tool. Check the tool's privacy terms if you handle client work.</p>
<h3 id="fact-check-checklist-check-every-number-date-name-price-quote-and-link">Fact-check checklist: check every number, date, name, price, quote and link</h3>
<p>AI tools invent facts that look real. They invent statistics, studies, quotes, and links to pages that do not exist. Your job is to catch every one before the reader sees it.</p>

<p>Keyword research is one place AI tools fail often. An AI tool can suggest keywords, but it cannot tell you real search volumes, because it has no access to search data. If it gives you a volume figure, treat it as invented. Use a real keyword tool or Google Search Console for volumes, and use AI only to group keywords and to suggest questions readers ask.</p>
<h3 id="disclosure-and-copyright-what-to-tell-readers">Disclosure and copyright: what to tell readers</h3>
<p>Disclosure means telling readers that AI helped make the post. Google suggests a note where readers would reasonably wonder how the content was created. You do not need a banner on every post, but a short line is honest and costs you nothing. For example: "This post was drafted with AI tools, then researched, checked and edited by a human." Do not list AI as the author, and do not use a fake author photo or a made-up byline. That is deception, and Google's guidance calls it out directly.</p>
<p>Copyright is less settled. The US Copyright Office published a report in January 2025 which says that copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or to material where there is not enough human control over the expressive parts. It also says that prompts alone do not provide enough control, but that human authors can hold copyright in the creative selection, arrangement or modification of AI output. In plain words, the more real creative work you add, the stronger your claim. Read the <a href="https://copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf" rel="noopener" target="_blank">US Copyright Office report on AI and copyrightability</a> if you sell or licence your writing, and check your local rules elsewhere, because copyright law differs by country.</p>
<p>Ownership also depends on the tool's terms. Read the terms of the tool you use, because they decide what you can do with the output. Most general tools let you use the output commercially, but check the terms on the day you rely on them.</p>
<h3 id="ai-images-for-blog-posts">AI images for blog posts</h3>
<p>AI tools can make images for your posts, and they are useful for illustrations and covers. They are not a replacement for real photos where the reader needs to see the real thing, such as a recipe result or a product. Use AI images for concepts, diagrams and covers, and use real photos for anything the reader must trust.</p>
<p>Write alt text for every AI image that describes what the image shows, and keep it honest. Do not claim an AI image is a real photo. Google's guidance says AI-generated images must carry metadata that marks them as AI-generated for ecommerce, and the same honesty helps readers everywhere. If your blog runs adverts, check your ad network's rules on AI images too, because some networks limit mass-produced, unedited AI content.</p>
<h3 id="what-it-costs-free-tiers-versus-paid-plans">What it costs: free tiers versus paid plans</h3>
<p>Your costs are low, which is the main attraction of this workflow. All three general tools have free plans, and a free plan is enough to write several posts a week. A paid plan costs about 20 US dollars a month, and it buys more capacity, faster replies, and access to newer models. Pay for a plan only when the free plan slows you down on paid work.</p>
<p>Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Imagine you pay 20 US dollars a month for a tool, and you write eight blog posts a month with it. That is 20 divided by 8, which is 2.50 US dollars per post. If each post takes two hours with AI help instead of four hours without it, you save 16 hours a month. At a freelance rate of 30 US dollars an hour, that saved time is worth 480 US dollars, which is far more than the 20 US dollar subscription. The value is in the time saved, not in the tool itself.</p>
<p>Do not pay for five tools at once. One general tool, used well, beats five subscriptions you barely open.</p>
<h3 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Asking the tool to write the whole post, then publishing the draft without editing it.</li>
<li>Skipping the notes step, so the post has nothing only you could write.</li>
<li>Trusting a statistic, quote or link the tool invented, then publishing it as fact.</li>
<li>Publishing many thin AI posts at scale, which Google treats as scaled content abuse.</li>
<li>Using the same prompt for every post, so every post sounds the same.</li>
<li>Hiding AI use from a client who asked for human-written work.</li>
<li>Paying for five AI tools before publishing ten posts with one free tool.</li>
<li>Forgetting to check the title, meta description and alt text, which the tool can also get wrong.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Can I use AI to write blog posts?</h4>
<p>Yes. Google says appropriate use of AI is not against its guidelines, and AI content gets no special treatment. What matters is whether the post is helpful, original and written for people, and whether you checked every fact before publishing.</p>
<h4>Will Google penalise AI written blog posts?</h4>
<p>No, Google does not penalise a post just because AI helped write it. Google penalises scaled content abuse, which means many pages generated mainly to manipulate rankings without adding value. One careful AI-assisted post is fine, and a hundred thin AI posts is not.</p>
<h4>Can Google detect AI written content?</h4>
<p>Google says it does not need to detect AI content, because its systems judge helpfulness, not authorship. AI detection tools are unreliable, and even OpenAI shut down its own classifier, so do not rely on a detector score to judge your post. Judge it by reading it.</p>
<h4>Do AI written blog posts rank?</h4>
<p>Yes, when they are helpful, original and well edited. A post that answers the reader's question, adds real examples, and links to useful sources can rank, whether AI helped draft it or not. A post that is thin, wrong or copied will not rank, however it was written.</p>
<h4>Should I tell readers AI helped write a post?</h4>
<p>Yes, a short note is honest and costs nothing. Google suggests sharing how content was created where readers would reasonably wonder. Do not list AI as the author, and do not use a fake author photo or a made-up byline.</p>
<h4>Who owns a blog post written with AI?</h4>
<p>You usually own the rights to use the output, under the tool's terms, but copyright protection depends on how much human creative work went into it. The US Copyright Office says purely AI-generated material is not protected, while human selection, arrangement and modification can be. Check the tool's terms and your local rules.</p>
<h3 id="conclusion-you-are-the-writer-the-tool-is-the-assistant">Conclusion: you are the writer, the tool is the assistant</h3>
<p>Learning how to use AI to write blog posts comes down to one rule. You stay in charge at four points: the question, the facts, the original detail, and the final edit. Pick one tool, start from a real reader question, dump what you know first, draft in sections, add what only you could write, check every fact, and read the post out loud before you publish.</p>
