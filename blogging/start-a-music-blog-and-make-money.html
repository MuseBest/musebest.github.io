How to Start a Music Blog and Make Money in 2026

To start a music blog and make money from it, you need a clear corner of music, reviews and guides that readers cannot find elsewhere, and a patient plan for income. A music blog is a website that publishes writing about artists, albums, songs, genres, concerts, music production, and industry news. It can cover one genre, one local scene, or one kind of reader. This guide walks you through each step in order, from picking your niche to adding your first income method, with honest costs, copyright care, and a realistic timeline.

Step 1: Pick your corner of music

A music blog fails when it tries to cover all music for everyone. Pick one corner first: one genre, one scene, one role, or one kind of reader. A blog about underground hip hop in one city reads very differently from a blog about classical music for beginners, and readers can tell at once which one is written for them. A narrow focus also helps you rank in search, because you can answer specific questions that large music sites ignore.

Think about what you know well enough to write about for years. You might cover indie releases, vinyl collecting, music production tips, concert photography, or the history of one genre. You might write for musicians, for collectors, or for casual listeners who want help finding new music. Pick a corner where you have real taste and real knowledge, because readers can tell at once when a writer is copying press releases.

Niche idea Reader Why it can work
Indie and underground releases Music fans who want new artists Large demand and many new releases
Vinyl collecting guides Collectors Loyal readers who buy records and gear
Music production for beginners Bedroom producers Step by step posts rank well
Local scene and gig guides Concert goers in one city Evergreen local content
Genre history and deep dives Curious listeners Long posts that keep readers on the page
Film and game soundtracks Soundtrack fans Few blogs cover this corner well

Check your niche before you commit. Search for the topic and read the top results. Ask what you could add that they do not cover, such as honest reviews of new artists, clear production advice, or guides to a local scene that nobody else writes about. Our guide on how to choose a blog niche walks through this choice in more detail.

Step 2: Choose a name and a domain

Your blog name should be short, easy to spell, and easy to say out loud. Avoid hyphens, numbers, and words with more than one spelling. A name that includes your genre or your angle helps readers understand the blog at a glance, such as a name built around vinyl, indie music, or your city scene. Check that the matching domain name is free, and check the same name on Instagram, TikTok, and X, because music readers live on those platforms.

A domain name is your web address, such as yourblog.com. It is usually registered for one year at a time and renewed each year. A domain often costs about 10 to 15 US dollars per year, but prices differ by provider and by ending, so check the current price before you buy. Keep the name short enough to fit on a business card and simple enough to spell over the phone.

Do not spend weeks on the name. A good name you can start with beats a perfect name you never use. You can change your design and your topics later, but changing a domain name later costs you search traffic, so pick something you can live with for years. Check that your name is not already used by a band, a label, or another blog, because a clash can cause confusion and legal trouble.

Step 3: Set up your blog on WordPress

Most music bloggers use self hosted WordPress, which is free blogging software that you install on your own hosting account. Self hosted means you pay for hosting and you control the site, including which ads and affiliate links you use. Free hosted blogs often place their own ads on your pages or limit how you can earn, so they are a poor choice if you plan to make money.

You need four things to get started. First, a domain name, which is your web address. Second, hosting, which is a server that stores your site files and makes them available online. Look for reliable uptime, regular backups, and a free SSL certificate, which encrypts traffic and shows a padlock in the browser. Third, WordPress itself, which most hosts install with a one click installer. Fourth, a theme, which is the template that controls how your site looks. Pick a clean theme that shows large images well and plays embedded audio cleanly, because music readers expect to hear the music you write about.

Our step by step guide on how to start a blog on WordPress covers the setup in full. Before you publish posts or add any ads or affiliate links, create three pages. An About page says who you are, what music you cover, and why readers should trust your taste. A Contact page gives artists, labels, and readers a way to reach you. A Privacy Policy page explains what data your site collects, including cookies. Google asks for a privacy policy before it approves AdSense, and most affiliate and advertising programmes expect one too.

Step 4: Plan your content pillars

Content pillars are the main kinds of post your blog publishes again and again. Pick three or four pillars and plan your first ten posts around them. A pillar might be album reviews, artist interviews, genre guides, or concert reviews. Pillars keep your blog focused, and they help readers know what to expect when they visit.

Plan your first ten posts before you launch, and aim to publish three to five of them in your first week. Each post should answer a question a reader might type into a search engine, or cover a release your readers care about. Good first posts for a music blog include a review of a new album, a beginner guide to one genre, a list of essential records for new collectors, and a guide to your local scene.

Content pillar Example post Why it helps
Album and EP reviews A review of one new release, with lyrics and production notes Brings in fans of that artist
Genre guides A beginner guide to shoegaze, with ten key records Evergreen and ranks well
Artist interviews A short interview with a local artist Builds relationships and shares
Concert and festival reviews A review of one local gig, with setlist notes Local readers and repeat visits
Playlists A themed playlist with notes on each track Easy to share on social media
Production guides How to record vocals at home on a budget Helps musicians who buy gear

Write each post to be genuinely useful. Answer the main question in the first paragraph. Use short headings to break up the text. Embed audio from official sources, such as a streaming player, so readers can hear the music you describe. Clear titles and short headings help both readers and search engines follow the post.

Step 5: Write reviews and guides that stand out

Music blogs fail when they copy press releases. A press release tells readers what the label wants them to hear. Your job is to tell readers what you actually heard. Listen to the record at least three times before you review it. Take notes on the lyrics, the production, the best tracks, and the weakest moments. Say what you liked and what you did not like, in plain words, and give reasons a reader can check by listening.

Go beyond good or bad. Explain the sound in words a reader can follow. Compare the record with the artist's earlier work, or with other records in the same genre. Name the producers, the songwriters, and the session players when you can find them, because credits help readers and they help search engines understand the post. Quote a short lyric line only when it helps your point, and keep quotes short.

For guides, show your working. A production guide should list the exact steps, the tools you used, and the mistakes you made. A genre guide should name the key records and say why each one matters. A collector guide should explain how to spot a good pressing, how to store records, and what to pay. Readers trust writers who show their work and admit what they do not know.

Step 6: Promote your blog to music lovers

Publishing is only half the work. New blogs get very little search traffic at first, so you need to bring readers to your posts. Music readers live on social media, so share each post where your audience already gathers. Short video clips on TikTok and Instagram Reels suit new releases and gigs. Longer posts suit X and Reddit, where music fans discuss records in detail. Pick one or two platforms and post there regularly, rather than trying to be everywhere at once.

Post on a steady schedule you can keep, such as one post a week. A regular schedule builds trust with readers and gives search engines fresh content to find. Write a short email newsletter for readers who want new reviews in their inbox. An email list is the income stream you control most, because you do not depend on search or social media rules to reach subscribers. Our guide on how to build an email list for a blog explains how to start one. Offer a free playlist or a list of essential records in exchange for an email address.

Build relationships with artists, labels, and other bloggers. Interview small artists, review their work honestly, and share their music. Guest posting on other music blogs can bring readers and links. Offer a useful post to a blog your readers already visit, and include a link back to your own blog. One review can become posts for several platforms if you cut it into short clips, quotes, and playlist notes. Video can help too: a short review video can link back to the full written guide. If you want to teach on video, read our guide on how to start a music YouTube channel and earn money.

Step 7: Add income methods in the right order

Most music bloggers use more than one income method. Relying on a single stream is risky, because one programme can change its rules or stop paying. Add income methods in this order: affiliate links first, then display ads, then sponsored posts, then your own digital products. Start with affiliate links because they need the least traffic. Wait for ads until you have steady visitors, and wait for digital products until you know what readers want.

An affiliate link is a special link. If a reader buys through it, you earn a commission, which is a share of the sale price. Music affiliate programmes include record shops, ticket sellers, streaming services, and gear shops. Recommend only products you would be comfortable with your readers buying, and check each programme's current terms on its own website, because commission rates change. Our guide on how to monetise a blog with affiliate marketing explains how to add affiliate links properly.

Display advertising means showing ads on your pages and earning money when readers view or click them. Google AdSense is the usual starting point. AdSense does not publish a fixed traffic minimum, but your site needs original content and a privacy policy, and you must be at least 18 years old to apply. Google pays you once your earnings reach its payment threshold, which is 100 US dollars for a US dollar account, as of November 2026. Check the current thresholds on Google's AdSense payment thresholds page before you apply. Our guide on how to monetise a blog with display ads covers this in more detail.

A sponsored post is a post a brand or artist pays you to write about a product, a release, or a tour. Agree the fee, the deadline, and what you will and will not say before you start writing. Never write a positive review of music you do not like, and always label sponsored content clearly. Our guide on how to monetise a blog with sponsored posts explains how to price and handle brand work.

Your own digital products can pay best of all, because you keep most of the sale price. A music blogger can sell a production guide, a set of sample packs, sheet music, or a short course. If you make music yourself, read our guide on how to sell music and sound packs online. Start with one small product that solves a problem your readers already ask about, and sell it from your own blog before you build a full shop.

Display ads Once traffic is steady Original content and a privacy policy
Sponsored posts Once brands notice your blog A clear niche and engaged readers

What it costs and what to expect

Starting a music blog costs very little, but it takes months of steady work before it earns anything. A domain name often costs about 10 to 15 US dollars per year. Shared hosting often costs a few dollars per month when you pay for a year at a time. A free theme and free plugins are enough to start, so you can launch for well under 100 US dollars in your first year. A paid theme, better headphones, or a microphone for interviews can wait until the blog earns money.

Expect slow growth. Most new blogs take six months to a year to earn a small amount, and several years to build a stable income. Music is a crowded niche, and ad rates for music content are often lower than for finance or business content, so traffic matters even more. Plan for months with no income at all, and treat early earnings as a bonus rather than a wage. The bloggers who succeed are usually the ones who keep publishing honest reviews long after the excitement of launch has faded.

Worked example

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Imagine your blog gets 4,000 page views in a month. Suppose 2.5 percent of those visitors click an affiliate link for music gear. That is 4,000 times 0.025, which equals 100 clicks. Suppose 5 percent of those clicks lead to a purchase. That is 100 times 0.05, which equals 5 sales. Suppose each sale is worth 60 US dollars, so total sales are 5 times 60, which equals 300 US dollars. Suppose your commission is 4 percent. That is 300 times 0.04, which equals 12 US dollars from affiliate links.

Now add display ads. Suppose your ad rate is 4 US dollars for every 1,000 views. That is 4,000 divided by 1,000, which equals 4, times 4 US dollars, which equals 16 US dollars. Add the two together: 12 plus 16 equals 28 US dollars for the month. This is a small amount, and it shows why traffic matters so much. At 40,000 views a month, the same rates would give about 280 US dollars. Your real numbers will differ, because rates, click rates, and buying rates all vary.

Common mistakes

  • Trying to cover all music instead of one genre, scene, or kind of reader.
  • Copying press releases instead of listening to the record and writing your own opinion.
  • Publishing one review and then going quiet for months, which loses the few readers you gained.
  • Writing only about big stars, which large sites already cover, and never writing about new artists.
  • Adding ads and affiliate links before you have useful content and regular readers.
  • Uploading full songs you do not own, which can break copyright law and get your blog taken down.
  • Hiding affiliate links or sponsored content instead of labelling them in plain words near the link.

Risks and scams to watch for

Copyright is the biggest risk for a music blog. Do not upload full songs, albums, or music videos that you do not own. Use official embeds from streaming services instead, so readers can hear the music without you hosting it. Only use photos you took yourself, photos sent to you with written permission, or photos with a clear free licence. Press photos sent by a label usually come with permission to use them for coverage, but check the terms that came with them, and ask in writing if you are unsure.

Disclosure rules apply to music blogs too. In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission sets rules for endorsements in its Endorsement Guides, which were revised in 2023. The rule is simple: if you are paid, or given something free, you must say so. Place the disclosure close to the recommendation and before any affiliate link, in plain words such as "This post contains affiliate links" or "The label sent me this record for free". Read the FTC's own Disclosures 101 guide for social media influencers before you publish sponsored content. Rules differ by country, so check the rules where you and your readers live.

Watch for fake offers. A real label or brand will contact you from a company email address and agree clear terms in writing. Be careful if anyone asks you to pay to review a record, pay for promotion, or share bank details early. Real affiliate programmes are free to join, and real labels do not charge bloggers to receive music. Never pay a company that promises to make your blog famous for a fee.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to start a music blog?

A music blog can start for well under 100 US dollars in the first year. A domain name often costs about 10 to 15 US dollars per year, and shared hosting often costs a few dollars per month. A free theme and free plugins are enough to begin, and you can add paid tools later when the blog earns money.

How long does it take to make money from a music blog?

There is no fixed timeline. Many bloggers take a year or two of steady posting to earn a meaningful amount, and several years to build a stable income. Music ad rates are often lower than in other niches, so traffic matters a lot. Plan for slow growth and keep publishing honest reviews.

Do I need to be a musician to start a music blog?

No, you do not need to play an instrument or work in the music industry. Many of the best music blogs are run by passionate listeners, critics, and collectors. Your ear, your taste, and your honesty matter more than a music degree. Say clearly who you are, and do not claim skills you do not have.

Can I use songs and album art on my music blog?

Use official embeds from streaming services for songs, so readers can hear the music without you hosting it. Do not upload full songs you do not own. For album art and press photos, use images sent to you with permission, or images with a clear free licence, and credit the source when asked.

What should I write about first on a music blog?

Start with posts that solve a clear problem or cover a release your readers care about, such as a review of a new album, a beginner guide to one genre, or a list of essential records for new collectors. These posts bring in search traffic and show your taste. Add interviews and opinion posts once you have a base of useful guides.

How do music blogs get free records and concert tickets?

Labels and promoters send music and tickets to blogs with a clear niche and a real audience, so focus on useful content and steady traffic first. You can also contact small labels and local promoters directly with a short email that shows your blog and your best posts. Always disclose free products in plain words, and never promise a positive review in exchange.

Conclusion

To start a music blog and make money from it, pick one corner of music, set up your own WordPress blog, publish honest reviews and useful guides on a steady schedule, and add income methods in the right order. Start with affiliate links, then ads, then sponsored posts, then your own products. Respect copyright, disclose free products and paid links, and give yourself at least a year before you judge the results.
