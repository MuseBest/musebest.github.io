How to Do Keyword Research for a Blog in 2026

You have written a good post, but nobody finds it. That is often a keyword problem, not a writing problem. Keyword research for a blog is the habit of checking what people actually type into a search engine before you write. It helps you pick topics readers want, in words they use, at a level of competition your blog can handle.

This guide shows you a free method you can use in one afternoon. You will learn how to find seed ideas, expand them with free tools, judge whether a keyword is winnable, group keywords into post plans, and track results after you publish. It is written for a new or small blog with no paid tools and no existing traffic.

What keyword research is, in plain language

A keyword is a word or phrase a person types into a search engine. A search query is the full phrase they type. Keyword research is the process of finding those phrases, learning what the searcher wants, and choosing the ones your blog can serve well.

Guessing topics wastes good writing. You might write a brilliant post on a phrase nobody searches for. You might also target a broad phrase, such as "blogging", where large sites fill page one and a new blog has little chance. Research sits between those two traps. It points you to specific phrases with real interest and realistic competition.

Step 1: List your main topics and reader questions

Start with your blog, not with a tool. Write down three to five main topics your blog covers. If you blog about budget cooking, your topics might be cheap weeknight meals, pantry basics, and packed lunches. If you have not chosen a focus yet, read our guide on how to choose a blog niche before you go further.

Next, list the questions readers already ask you. Check blog comments, emails, social media replies, and questions friends ask in real life. Write each question in the reader's own words. These are seed keywords, the starting phrases you will expand in Step 2.

Aim for 20 to 30 seed phrases at this stage. Do not judge them yet. A weak seed can still lead to a strong long tail phrase later.

Step 2: Expand your list with Google suggestions

Now use Google itself, which is free and shows real search behaviour. Type a seed phrase into Google, but do not press Enter. The autocomplete suggestions that appear are phrases people search for. Note the useful ones.

Next, search the phrase and scroll to the "People also ask" box. These are real questions tied to the topic. Open two or three of them, because each one can reveal more questions. Then scroll to the bottom of the page and read "Related searches". Add any phrase that fits your blog and sounds like something a reader would type.

Step 3: Check interest with Trends and Keyword Planner

Autocomplete shows that people search for a phrase, but it does not show how much interest exists or whether it is rising or falling. Two free tools help here.

Google Trends shows whether interest in a topic is rising, steady, or falling over time, and whether it is seasonal. Search your seed phrase, then compare it with a close alternative. If one phrase rises each December and falls in January, plan that post for November, not February. Trends does not give exact search counts, so treat it as a direction check, not a demand figure.

Google Keyword Planner is part of Google Ads, and you can use it without running paid adverts. It gives rough volume ranges, such as 100 to 1,000 searches a month, rather than exact counts. Treat those ranges as estimates.

Step 4: Judge each keyword on intent, specificity, and page one competition

This is the step most beginners skip, and it is the step that matters most. For each phrase, ask three questions.

First, what is the intent? A phrase that starts with "how to" usually means the reader wants to learn. A phrase with "best" or "versus" usually means the reader is comparing options. A phrase with a brand name may mean the reader wants that brand's own page. Match the post to the intent. A learning phrase needs a guide. A comparison phrase needs a comparison. If you write the wrong type of post, readers will leave quickly, even if the post is well written.

Second, how specific is the phrase? Broad phrases are hard for a new blog. Long tail phrases are more winnable because fewer strong pages target them, and the reader's need is clearer. As a rule, a new blog should favour phrases of four or more words, with a clear question or problem in them.

Third, what sits on page one? Search the exact phrase and look at the top ten results. Ask whether the pages are large authority sites, thin forum threads, or small blogs like yours. If page one is full of major publishers with long, detailed guides, that phrase is probably too hard for now. If page one has forums, short posts, or pages that miss part of the question, you may have a real opening. Also check the format: if page one is mostly videos, a text post may struggle, and if it is mostly lists, a list may fit better. For more on matching a post to what ranks, read how to write blog posts that rank on Google.

Keep only phrases that pass all three checks: clear intent you can serve, enough specificity to be winnable, and a page one you can beat with a better, fuller answer. Aim to keep 15 to 25 phrases from your long list.

Intent The reader's goal is clear from the words A how to question you can answer fully A brand name or a buying phrase you cannot serve
Specificity The phrase names one problem Four or more words, one clear problem One or two broad words, many possible meanings

Step 5: Group keywords into clusters and post plans

Do not write one post per phrase. Group related phrases into clusters, and give each cluster one post. One post can serve a main phrase plus several close variations, as long as they share the same intent.

Take the packed lunch example. The phrases "cheap packed lunch ideas for work", "packed lunch ideas for adults", and "easy packed lunches for the office" share one intent: an adult wants simple lunch ideas for work. One post can serve all three, with the main phrase in the title and the others in headings and body text where they fit naturally.

Give each cluster a working title, a main keyword, and two to four supporting phrases. Then order the clusters by value. Start with the cluster that answers the most common reader question, or the one where page one looks weakest. That becomes your next post. For help turning a cluster into a full post plan, see how to grow blog traffic.

Step 6: Build your keyword spreadsheet

A simple spreadsheet keeps research usable. You do not need a paid tool. Use any free spreadsheet app, and use these columns: keyword, intent, volume range, page one strength, cluster name, working title, status, and published URL once the post is live.

Fill one row per keyword, not per cluster, so you can sort and filter. Mark the main keyword for each cluster, and mark the rest as supporting phrases. The status column tracks progress: researched, planned, drafted, published, or refresh needed. Update it as you write, so you always know what is next.

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Imagine a food blog with the cluster "cheap packed lunches for work". The main keyword is "cheap packed lunch ideas for work", with a rough volume range of 100 to 1,000 searches a month. Supporting phrases are "packed lunch ideas for adults", "easy packed lunches for the office", and "cheap lunch ideas for work week". Page one shows two forums, one short list post, and several large recipe sites that focus on dinners, not packed lunches. The working title is "15 Cheap Packed Lunch Ideas for Work That Actually Fill You Up". That cluster is worth writing, because the intent is clear, the phrases are specific, and page one leaves room for a fuller, lunch focused guide.

Step 7: Track results and refresh old posts

Keyword research does not end at publishing. After four to eight weeks, check Google Search Console, which is Google's free tool that shows which queries bring people to your site. Look at the Queries report for your new post. It shows the exact phrases people typed before they clicked, and the phrases where your post appeared but got few clicks.

Use that data in two ways: add a section for a close phrase your post nearly ranks for, or rewrite a title that gets impressions but few clicks. Do not change the URL when you refresh a post.

Repeat keyword research every three months, or when you plan a new batch of posts. Search behaviour changes, new questions appear, and old posts slip. A short refresh round, checking Search Console queries for your top ten posts, is often more valuable than researching ten brand new topics. What keyword research costs, and how long it takes

The method in this guide costs nothing in tools. It costs time. A first research round for one blog takes about three to four hours: one hour for seeds and expansion, one hour for Trends and Keyword Planner checks, one hour for page one checks, and one hour for clustering and the spreadsheet. Later rounds are faster, because you already have a spreadsheet and a sense of your topics.

Keyword research does not bring traffic by itself, and it does not guarantee rankings. A new post may take weeks to appear in search results, and months to reach page one, if it reaches page one at all.

The main risk is researching for too long and never writing. Once you have 15 to 25 usable phrases in clusters, stop and start writing.

Common mistakes

  • Targeting a broad phrase, such as "blogging", that large sites already own, instead of a specific phrase a new blog can win.
  • Copying a competitor's keyword list without checking whether the intent fits your readers.
  • Treating tool volume ranges as promises of traffic, rather than rough estimates for comparison.
  • Writing one thin post per phrase, instead of one strong post per cluster.
  • Skipping the page one check, and publishing into a results page you cannot beat yet.
  • Stuffing the exact phrase into every sentence, which reads badly and does not help rankings.
  • Researching once and never returning, so old posts slowly slip while search behaviour changes.

Frequently asked questions

What is keyword research for a blog?

Keyword research for a blog is checking what phrases readers type into a search engine, what they want when they type them, and how strong the current top results are, before you choose a post topic. It helps you write posts that match real demand instead of guessing.

Do I need a paid tool for keyword research?

No, not at the start. Google Autocomplete, People also ask, Related searches, Trends, Keyword Planner, and Search Console cover the full method in this guide for free. A paid tool can save time later, but it does not replace the page one check or good judgement about intent.

How many keywords should one blog post target?

Target one main keyword, plus two to four supporting phrases that share the same intent. That gives the post a clear focus while letting it serve close variations naturally. If two phrases need different answers, they belong in two posts, not one.

How often should I do keyword research?

Do a full round when you plan a new batch of posts, and a short refresh round every three months using Search Console queries for your existing posts. That rhythm keeps your content plan fresh without turning research into a full time job.

How do I know if a keyword is too hard for a new blog?

Search the phrase and study page one. If the top ten are large authority sites with long, detailed guides that fully answer the question, the phrase is likely too hard for now. Look instead for a longer, more specific version of the phrase, or a related question that page one answers poorly.

Conclusion: start with one cluster this week

Pick one main topic, expand it with Google suggestions, check intent and page one, and build one cluster with a working title. Then write that post. Good keyword research for a blog is a short, repeatable habit, not a one off project. It turns guessing into a plan, and a plan into posts that have a real chance in search.
