<p>Learning how to start a food blog and earn money is a good goal, but the honest answer starts with time. A food blog can earn real money, yet most food blogs take 12 to 24 months to earn a useful amount. The blogs that do earn have four things in common: a clear niche, recipes that work, photos that make readers hungry, and posts that rank on Google. This guide shows you how to build all four, in order, with a phone camera and a small budget.</p>
<p>This guide is for beginners who like to cook and can follow a recipe. You do not need to be a professional chef. You will learn how to pick a niche, set up WordPress, choose a recipe plugin, take better food photos, write recipes that rank, grow traffic, and monetise your blog.</p>

<h3 id="step-1-pick-a-niche-you-can-own">Step 1: Pick a niche you can own</h3>
<p>A niche is the narrow topic your blog covers. Broad food blogs compete with huge recipe sites on every dish, and a new blog cannot win that fight. A narrow blog can rank faster, because Google sees it as the expert on one kind of cooking.</p>
<p>Before you pick a niche, ask three questions. Can you cook 50 recipes in it without running out of ideas? Do readers search for it, and do they buy ingredients and tools for it? And do you enjoy cooking it enough to test recipes for two years?</p>

WordPress.org gives you full control over adverts, affiliate links and recipe plugins, which is what you need to earn. WordPress.com on a free plan limits adverts and plugins, so it is a poor choice for a money blog.</p>
<p>You need a domain name and web hosting. Hosting is the rented server space where your blog files live. Most hosts offer a one-click WordPress install, which sets up the blog for you in a few minutes.</p>

<h3 id="step-3-choose-a-theme-and-a-recipe-plugin">Step 3: Choose a theme and a recipe plugin</h3>
<p>A food blog theme is a design template made for recipe sites. Look for three things: it loads fast, it works well on phones, and it shows recipe cards clearly. Popular food themes cost 50 to 130 US dollars as a one-off payment. A free theme can work at first, but a food theme saves you hours of setup.</p>
<p>A recipe plugin is the tool that turns your recipe into a neat card with ingredients, steps, times and a print button. It also adds recipe schema markup to your page. Schema markup is structured data, which is code that tells Google your page has a recipe, with its cooking time, ratings and ingredients. Google can then show your recipe as a rich result, which is a search result with a photo, a rating and a cooking time. Rich results get more clicks than plain blue links.</p>

<h3 id="step-4-learn-food-photography-basics">Step 4: Learn food photography basics</h3>
<p>Food photography is what makes a reader stop and click. You do not need an expensive camera. A modern phone camera is enough to start, if you learn three skills: light, angles and styling.</p>
<p>Light matters most. Use natural window light, and never use your camera flash, because flash makes food look flat and grey. Place the dish near a window, with the light coming from the side or from behind. A white foam board on the opposite side bounces light back and softens shadows. Foam board costs a few dollars at a craft shop.</p>
<p>Angles matter next. Shoot from above, which is called a flat lay, for bowls, salads and baked trays. Shoot at 45 degrees for burgers, cakes and tall dishes, because that angle shows height and layers. Take both angles for every recipe, then pick the best.</p>
<p>Styling is simple. Use a plain plate, a clean surface, and one or two props, such as a linen cloth or a sprig of herbs. Do not crowd the photo with props, because the food should be the star. Take six to twelve photos for each recipe: the finished dish, two or three process shots, and a close-up. Readers trust a recipe more when they can see each step.</p>

<h3 id="step-5-write-recipes-that-rank">Step 5: Write recipes that rank</h3>
<p>A recipe post has a clear shape, and readers expect it. Start with a short introduction of two or three sentences that says what the dish is and why readers will like it. Then give the recipe card with ingredients, steps, times and servings. Then add the useful detail below the card: substitutions, storage advice, and common mistakes.</p>
<p>Test every recipe at least twice before you publish it. Cook it once to get it right, then cook it again to check the times and amounts. Write down the exact amounts you used, not the amounts you guessed. If you did not test a substitution, say so plainly, and do not claim it works.</p>
<p>Food safety matters too. Say how long the dish keeps in the fridge, and whether it freezes well. If a recipe uses raw eggs or undercooked meat, say so, and give the safe cooking temperature where you know it. Rules differ by country, so link to your local food safety authority where relevant.</p>
<p>Keyword research is the other half of ranking. A keyword is the phrase a reader types into Google. Use Google autocomplete and the People Also Ask box to find real questions, such as "easy chicken curry for beginners" or "how long to bake banana bread". Target one search phrase per post, and use it in the title, the first paragraph and one heading. Avoid broad phrases such as "chicken recipe", because huge sites already rank for them. For the full method, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/blogging/keyword-research-for-a-blog" rel="noopener" target="_blank">keyword research for a blog</a>.</p>
<p>Write the recipe in your own words. Ingredient lists are facts, so they cannot be owned, but the way you explain a recipe is your own work. Never copy another blogger's recipe text or photos.</p>
<h3 id="step-6-build-traffic-with-seo-pinterest-and-email">Step 6: Build traffic with SEO, Pinterest and email</h3>
<p>Google traffic takes months to build, so use Pinterest and email in the meantime. Pinterest works well for food, because readers use it to plan meals. Create a business account, make vertical pins in a 2 to 3 ratio for each recipe, and link every pin to the recipe, not to your homepage. Write a clear pin description of up to 500 characters that names the dish and who it suits. Many food bloggers report that Pinterest brings more early traffic than Google. For the method, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/social-media-earning/how-to-make-money-on-pinterest" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to make money on Pinterest</a>.</p>
<p>Short video helps too. A 30 second video of the dish coming together, posted as a Reel or a Short, can bring readers who never search Google. Film with your phone, keep it simple, and link back to the full recipe. Do not spend hours editing video before you have 30 recipes published.</p>
<p>Start an email list early, even when you have ten readers. Offer one useful freebie, such as a weekly meal plan or a printable shopping list, in exchange for an email address. Send one email a week with your new recipe and one older recipe worth a second look. For the method, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/blogging/how-to-build-email-list-for-a-blog" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to build an email list for a blog</a>.</p>
<p>Publish one quality recipe a week at first, then two a week once you are faster. A library of 30 to 50 recipes is the point where Google starts to take a food blog seriously. Every three months, audit your old posts: update the photos, fix the times, and improve the posts that already get some traffic.</p>

<h3 id="worked-example-what-a-small-food-blog-could-earn">Worked example: what a small food blog could earn</h3>

Affiliate links earn 150 US dollars a month from kitchen tools and ingredients. One sponsored post earns 300 US dollars that month. Total income is 1,450 US dollars a month. Hosting costs 10 US dollars a month and the recipe plugin costs about 7 US dollars a month, so the blog keeps about 1,433 US dollars before tax. If the blogger spends 60 hours a month on the blog, that is about 24 US dollars per hour. The same blog at 5,000 pageviews a month would earn about 100 US dollars from ads at the same RPM, which shows why traffic matters more than any single income stream.</p>

<h3 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Picking a broad niche such as "all food", then competing with huge recipe sites on every dish.</li>
<li>Publishing five recipes and applying for adverts, then feeling disappointed when nothing earns.</li>
<li>Writing a long life story above the recipe, instead of a short introduction and a clear recipe card.</li>
<li>Using dark, blurry photos, which stop readers from clicking your pins.</li>
<li>Copying a recipe from another blog, which breaks trust and can break copyright rules.</li>
<li>Quoting a median blogger income figure without saying it comes from established bloggers, which misleads beginners.</li>
<li>Ignoring Pinterest and email, then depending only on Google from day one.</li>
<li>Buying an expensive camera before publishing ten recipes, instead of learning with a phone.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="risks-and-scams-to-watch-for">Risks and scams to watch for</h3>
<p>Watch for brands that offer "exposure" instead of payment, then ask you to buy their product first. A genuine sponsorship pays you, and it never asks you to pay for the product you are meant to review. Watch for paid food blog courses that promise income in 90 days. No course can promise that, because income depends on traffic, and traffic takes months to build. Watch for guest post offers that ask you to pay for a link, because paid links must be marked clearly under Google's rules, and a site that sells links without marking them can harm your blog.</p>
<p>Also watch for the risk in your own recipes. If you publish a recipe you did not test, a reader may waste good ingredients on a dish that fails. Test every recipe twice, and say plainly when a substitution was not tested. If a job or brand deal looks too good to be true, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-jobs/how-to-spot-online-job-scams" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to spot online job scams</a> before you reply.</p>
<h3 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Do food bloggers get paid?</h4>
<p>Yes, but slowly. Food bloggers earn from display ads, affiliate links, sponsored posts and their own products, and each stream needs traffic first. Most new food blogs earn nothing for 6 to 12 months, then a small amount that grows as the recipe library grows.</p>
<h4>Do I need to be a professional chef to start a food blog?</h4>
<p>No. Readers want recipes that work, clear photos and honest advice, not a chef qualification. Home cooks who test their recipes carefully and write clearly do well. Say what you tested and what you did not, and readers will trust you.</p>
<h4>What camera do I need for food photography?</h4>
<p>A modern phone camera is enough to start. Good window light, a white foam board reflector and a clean plate matter more than an expensive camera. Upgrade to a better camera only after you have 30 recipes published and you know food blogging suits you.</p>
<h4>How often should I post on a food blog?</h4>
<p>One quality recipe a week is a good start, then two a week once you are faster. A library of 30 to 50 recipes is the point where Google starts to take a food blog seriously. Posting daily with weak recipes helps no one.</p>
<h4>Should I use WordPress for a food blog?</h4>
<p>Yes, self-hosted WordPress, known as WordPress.org, is the best choice for most food bloggers, because it supports recipe plugins, adverts and affiliate links without limits. Free builders and hosted platforms limit what you can earn.</p>
<h4>How long does it take a food blog to make money?</h4>
<p>Plan for 12 to 24 months before a useful income, and longer before it pays a full wage. Research on food bloggers reports an average of about 21 months to earn, with about a quarter earning within six months, but those figures come from surveys of bloggers who kept going, so treat them as a rough guide, not a promise.</p>
<h3 id="conclusion-pick-a-niche-test-your-recipes-publish-every-week">Conclusion: pick a niche, test your recipes, publish every week</h3>
<p>Learning how to start a food blog and earn money comes down to four moves. Pick a narrow niche you can cook 50 recipes in, test every recipe twice, take bright photos with window light, and publish one recipe a week while you build Pinterest and email traffic. Do not buy an expensive camera, do not copy other bloggers, and do not stop publishing when the first months earn nothing.</p>
