How to start a finance blog and make money is a question many people ask when they enjoy talking about money and want to earn from it. You may know how to budget, save, or pay off debt, but turning that knowledge into a blog that earns income feels unclear. This guide shows you how to pick a narrow angle, set up the blog, write content readers can trust, and add income in layers, without hype and without promises.

Can you make money with a finance blog?

Yes, a finance blog can earn money, but it is a slow build, not a quick win. Finance blogs earn through display ads, affiliate links, sponsored posts, and digital products. Advertisers pay well for finance readers because those readers often buy bank accounts, cards, insurance, and investing tools. That is why finance is a popular niche, and also why it is competitive.

A new blog usually earns little or nothing for the first few months. You need readers first, and readers need useful posts first. Most blogs that earn steady income have been publishing for a year or more. If you start with that honest picture in mind, you will not quit too early.

You do not need to be rich to blog about money. You do need to be honest about what you know, careful with facts, and clear that you share general education, not personal advice.

Step 1: Pick a narrow finance angle

A narrow angle helps you stand out. A blog about "money" competes with huge finance sites. A blog about one clear topic for one clear reader has a real chance to rank and to build loyal readers.

Finance angles that work for new bloggers

Pick an angle that matches your own experience and your reader's problem. Good starting angles include budgeting for beginners, paying off debt on a low income, saving for a first home, money for new parents, or investing basics for complete beginners. Each of these has clear questions readers search for.

Check the angle before you commit. Search for your topic and read the top results. If the first page is full of banks and big publishers, look for a smaller corner of the topic. For example, instead of "how to invest", try "how to start investing with a small monthly amount". Smaller corners are easier to win.

Your angle should also fit the products you may recommend later. A budgeting blog can recommend budgeting apps and bank accounts. A debt payoff blog can recommend debt tools and books. If the angle has no honest product or service attached to it, earning from it will be harder.

If you want a wider method for picking a topic, read our guide on how to choose a blog niche before you decide.

Angles to avoid at the start

Avoid angles that need a licence or deep expertise, such as personalised investment advice, tax planning for specific cases, or complex pension advice. These topics carry legal risk and readers expect expert credentials. Avoid hot stock tips and crypto predictions too. They date fast, they attract the wrong readers, and they can mislead people who act on them.

Avoid angles you do not care about. A finance blog takes months of writing before it earns, so you need a topic you can write about week after week.

Step 2: Work out what the blog will cost

A finance blog is cheap to start, but the costs are real and repeat each year. Work them out before you buy anything, so a renewal price never surprises you. The ranges below are typical first year ranges. Prices change, so check each provider's live pricing page before you buy.

Item Typical first year cost Notes
Domain name 10 to 15 US dollars per year Renewal is often higher than the first year price
Web hosting 30 to 100 US dollars per year Basic shared hosting is enough to start
WordPress theme Free to 60 US dollars A free theme works well at the start
Email tool Free to 120 US dollars per year Free plans cover small lists
Total About 40 to 295 US dollars Plus your time, which is the biggest cost

Your time is the real cost. Writing one good post can take 4 to 8 hours, including research and editing. Keep costs low at the start. You do not need paid tools, a premium theme, or a designer to publish your first 10 posts.

Step 3: Set up the blog

Set up the blog in one focused weekend, then move on to writing. Do not spend weeks on design.

First, buy a short domain name that is easy to spell and say out loud. Avoid hyphens and numbers. A name that includes your angle helps readers understand the blog at a glance, such as a name built around budgeting, saving, or debt free living.

Next, buy hosting and install WordPress. WordPress is free software, and most hosts offer a one click install. You can read more on the official WordPress.org site. Choose a fast, simple theme and keep the design clean. Finance readers want clear text and easy navigation, not fancy graphics.

Then create four essential pages before you publish your first post: an About page, a Contact page, a Privacy Policy page, and a Disclaimer page that says the blog shares general money education and is not financial advice. These pages build trust and they matter for ad networks and affiliate programs later.

Finally, install a backup plugin and a basic security plugin, and set up free analytics so you can see which posts readers visit. Keep the plugin count low.

Step 4: Plan your first 10 posts

New bloggers often publish random posts and wonder why nothing ranks. A plan fixes that. Your first 10 posts should cover the core questions in your angle.

Content pillars for a finance blog

Split your content into three or four pillars. A budgeting blog might use pillars such as budgeting basics, saving money, debt payoff, and earning more. Each pillar gets two or three of your first 10 posts.

Plan the first 10 posts as a mix. About six posts should answer common beginner questions, such as how to build a budget or how to start an emergency fund. About three posts should compare options, such as two savings accounts or two budgeting apps. One post can be a personal story about your own money habit, clearly labelled as your experience, not advice for everyone.

Write the titles as questions readers actually ask. Use keyword research for a blog to check which phrases people search for. Long, specific phrases are easier for a new blog to rank for than short, general ones.

Step 5: Write finance content readers can trust

Finance is a "your money or your life" topic, which means Google and readers hold it to a higher standard. Sloppy money facts can cost a reader real money, so accuracy is not optional.

General education versus personalised advice

General education explains how something works. Personalised advice tells a specific person what to do with their money. Your blog should stay on the education side. You can explain how an index fund works or how a budget is built. You should not tell a reader which fund to buy or whether to sell an investment.

Use plain language for every money term the first time it appears. Explain "compound interest" as interest earned on interest. Explain "RPM" as the money a blog earns from ads per 1,000 page views.

How to check a money fact before you publish

Check every fee, rate, rule, and limit against an official source before you publish. Use the bank's own page, the government's page, or the product's help centre. Do not copy figures from other blogs, because they may be out of date. Add a note with the month and year you checked a changing fact, and update the post when the fact changes.

Show your sources in the text, with a link to the official page. It tells the reader you checked and it tells Google the claim is backed up. If you cannot verify a fact, leave it out or write around it in general terms.

Step 6: Get readers

A new finance blog gets readers from search first, then from other channels. Search takes time, so start the other channels early.

Learn basic SEO. Use one clear keyword per post, write a useful title, and structure the post with clear headings. Link to your own related posts where it helps the reader.

Pinterest works well for finance content, especially budgeting, saving, and debt payoff. Make a simple image for each post and pin it to a board that matches the topic.

Start an email list from the first month, with a simple free download such as a one page budget sheet in exchange for an email address. Our guide on how to build an email list for a blog walks through the setup.

Step 7: Add income in layers

Do not add every income method on day one. Add them in layers, as the blog grows, so each one has a real chance to work.

When to add each income method

Display ads come first for most finance blogs, once traffic reaches the level an ad network accepts. Google's own guide to how to make money blogging explains the main models. Ad income grows with traffic, so it is slow at first and steadier later.

Affiliate marketing comes next, once you have posts that recommend products honestly. A budgeting app or a bank account can earn a commission when a reader signs up through your link. Only recommend products you have checked, and disclose the link clearly near it.

Sponsored posts come later, when brands can see your traffic and audience. Say no to sponsors whose products you would not use yourself, and disclose every sponsored post at the top. Digital products come last, once you know what readers will pay for. A budget template or a short course can earn more per sale than ads or affiliate links, but only if readers already trust you.

What it costs and what to expect

Be honest with yourself about the timeline. A finance blog is a long project, and the stages below are typical, not promised. Your results will vary with your niche, your pace, and your traffic.

Stage Time What usually happens
Setup and first posts Months 1 to 3 10 to 20 posts published, almost no traffic, no income
Early growth Months 4 to 9 Search traffic starts, a few hundred visits a month, first small ad or affiliate income possible
Steady growth Months 10 to 18 Traffic grows, email list grows, income becomes more regular but still modest
Established After 18 months A larger library, steadier traffic, and several income layers working together

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Imagine a finance blog that reaches 50,000 page views in a month. At an ad rate of 20 US dollars per 1,000 views, the maths is 50,000 divided by 1,000, which equals 50, then 50 times 20 US dollars, which equals 1,000 US dollars for that month from ads. Check the maths: 50,000 divided by 1,000 is 50, and 50 times 20 is 1,000. That figure is only an example. Most new blogs take a year or more to reach that traffic, and ad rates vary widely by country, season, and topic.

Common mistakes

  • Picking a broad angle such as "money" instead of one narrow topic you can win.
  • Publishing random posts with no plan, so readers cannot tell what the blog is about.
  • Copying money facts from other blogs instead of checking official sources.
  • Giving personalised financial advice instead of general education.
  • Adding affiliate links before the blog has useful content, which erodes trust.
  • Choosing a host or theme for looks instead of speed and simplicity.
  • Ignoring the email list until the blog is big.
  • Quitting after three months because the income has not arrived yet.

Risks and scams to watch for

Finance blogging attracts scams, so stay alert. Be wary of anyone who offers to pay you to publish a fake review, or to promote an investment scheme with guaranteed returns. There is no guaranteed return in investing, and promoting one can harm your readers and your reputation. Watch out for courses that promise a profitable finance blog in 30 days. A blog takes months of steady work, and no course can change that. Check any affiliate program before you join it, and never pay a fee to join. Real programs are free to join.

Also protect your readers. Never ask readers for bank details or passwords, and never publish a reader's personal financial details. If a sponsor asks you to make a claim you cannot verify, say no.

Frequently asked questions

Can I start a finance blog with no experience?

Yes, you can start with basic money habits and a willingness to research carefully. You do not need a finance degree to explain budgeting or saving. You do need to check every fact against an official source, and avoid topics that need a licence.

Do I need financial credentials to blog about money?

No, you do not need credentials to write general money education. Credentials help for advanced topics, but a beginner blog can earn trust through accurate, well sourced posts. Be open about your background.

How much can a finance blog earn?

There is no typical figure, because earnings depend on traffic, country, topic, and income methods. Some finance blogs earn very little for a year, then grow slowly. Treat any income figure you read online with care, and never plan spending around income that has not arrived yet.

How do finance blogs make money?

Most finance blogs earn through a mix of display ads, affiliate commissions, sponsored posts, and digital products. Ads pay per page view, affiliate links pay when a reader buys, sponsored posts pay a flat fee, and products pay per sale.

What should my first finance blog posts be?

Start with the core beginner questions in your angle, such as how to build a budget and how to start an emergency fund. Add one or two comparison posts, such as two budgeting methods. These posts answer real searches and show new readers what the blog covers.

Conclusion: how to start a finance blog and make money in 2026

That is how to start a finance blog and make money in 2026: pick one narrow angle, keep costs low, set up a simple WordPress site, publish your first 10 posts around clear pillars, check every money fact, grow readers through search and email, and add income in layers as traffic grows.
