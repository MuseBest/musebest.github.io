39:T59e6,<h2 id="how-to-guest-post-for-blog-growth-in-2026">How to Guest Post for Blog Growth in 2026</h2>
<p>Learning how to guest post for blog growth is worth your time, because one good guest post can do three things at once: put your name in front of a new audience, send readers to your blog, and earn you a link that helps your search rankings. The trap is to treat guest posting as a link scheme. Google is clear that links placed mainly to gain ranking credit count as link spam, and blogs that sell guest post links without marking them can harm both sites. Guest posting works when you write a genuinely useful post for a real audience, and the link is a by-product, not the goal.</p>

You will learn what Google says, how to set a goal, how to build your assets, how to find blogs that accept guest posts, how to qualify them, how to pitch, how to write a post editors accept, what to do after publishing, and how to measure results.</p>

<h3 id="what-guest-posting-is-and-what-google-says-about-it">What guest posting is, and what Google says about it</h3>
<p>Guest posting means writing an article that is published on someone else's blog, with your name on it and usually a short author bio with a link back to your blog. The host blog gets a useful post for free. You get exposure, referral traffic, and a link. Both sides benefit, which is why the practice has lasted for years.</p>
<p>Google's rules are the part most guides skip, so read them first. Google's spam policies list buying or selling links for ranking purposes as link spam, including exchanging money for links or posts that contain links. They also list advertorials, which are articles where payment is received and the article includes links that pass ranking credit, and links with optimised anchor text in articles, guest posts or press releases distributed on other sites, as examples of link spam. Read the <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Google spam policies</a> yourself before you pitch anywhere.</p>
<p>The rules leave room for honest guest posting. A link is not a violation if it is qualified with rel="nofollow" or rel="sponsored", which are small code attributes that tell Google not to pass ranking credit through the link. A link in your author bio that points to your homepage is usually fine. A link in the body of the post that points to a genuinely useful resource on your blog is usually fine too, when the post itself is useful and the link helps the reader. What Google objects to is guest posts written mainly to place links, especially with keyword-heavy anchor text, and guest posts bought or sold as a service.</p>
<p>Google also has a site reputation abuse policy, which covers third-party content published mainly to borrow a host site's ranking signals. In plain words, a site that publishes guest posts on topics it does not cover, just to rank for them, can be penalised. So pitch only blogs that cover your topic, and write only posts that fit the host blog.</p>
<h3 id="step-1-set-your-goal">Step 1: Set your goal</h3>
<p>Before you pitch anyone, write down one goal in one sentence. A goal keeps you honest, because it decides which blogs you pitch and what you measure.</p>

<p>Pick one goal per campaign, and pick the blogs that fit it. A blog with a small but engaged audience is better for reputation than a big blog whose readers never click. A blog in your exact niche is better for a link than a general blog with higher traffic. And a link alone is a weak goal: if the post brings no readers, the link is worth little.</p>
<h3 id="step-2-build-your-assets-before-you-pitch">Step 2: Build your assets before you pitch</h3>
<p>Editors judge you by your assets, which are the things that prove you can write. Build them before you send your first pitch.</p>
<p>First, write a 40 to 60 word author bio. It should say who you are, what you write about, and where readers can find you. Write it in third person, because that is how most blogs print it. For example: "Sara Ahmed writes about budget travel in Southeast Asia at her blog, Wander Light. She has visited 12 countries by bus and train, and she writes practical guides for travellers who want to see more and spend less."</p>
<p>Second, prepare two or three writing samples. These should be your best posts on your own blog, on topics close to what you will pitch. If your blog is brand new and you have no samples yet, write two strong posts on your own blog first, then pitch. Editors rarely accept a writer with nothing to show.</p>
<p>Third, build one pillar page on your own blog. A pillar page is a long, useful guide on your core topic, and it is the page your guest post links will point to. A link to a strong pillar page helps readers more than a link to your homepage. For the method, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/blogging/how-to-write-blog-posts-that-rank-on-google" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to write blog posts that rank on Google</a>.</p>
<p>Fourth, decide your link policy. You will usually ask for one link in the author bio and, if the host allows it, one contextual link in the body of the post to a useful page on your blog. Never ask for three or four links, and never ask for a link with keyword-heavy anchor text, because that is the pattern Google calls link spam.</p>
<h3 id="step-3-find-blogs-that-accept-guest-posts">Step 3: Find blogs that accept guest posts</h3>
<p>Finding hosts is easier than most beginners think, because many blogs say openly that they accept guest posts. Use four routes, and build a target list of 10 to 30 blogs.</p>
<p>First, use Google search operators. A search operator is a special phrase that narrows a Google search. Try your topic plus "write for us", your topic plus "accepting guest posts", your topic plus "guest post guidelines", and your topic plus "contribute". For example, "budget travel" plus "write for us" finds travel blogs with a write for us page. A write for us page is the page where a blog explains its guest post rules.</p>
<p>Second, look at where your competitors guest post. Search for a competitor's name plus "guest post", and you will find the blogs they wrote for. Those blogs already accept guest posts in your niche, so they are good targets.</p>
<p>Third, ask in communities. Blogging groups on Facebook, Reddit and X often have threads where bloggers look for guest writers. Read the group rules first, and do not spam your pitch in a group that forbids it.</p>
<p>Fourth, read the blogs you already follow. If a blog publishes guest posts, it usually says so on its about page or at the end of a guest post. Note the blogs that publish guest posts often, because they are the most likely to say yes.</p>
<p>Put every target in a simple spreadsheet with five columns: blog name, blog URL, editor name and email, topic fit, and pitch status. A list of 10 to 30 blogs is enough for your first campaign.</p>
<h3 id="step-4-qualify-hosts-with-a-checklist">Step 4: Qualify hosts with a checklist</h3>
<p>Not every blog that accepts guest posts is worth writing for. A post on a weak blog wastes your time, and a post on a spammy blog can harm your reputation. Check each target against five signals before you pitch.</p>

<thead>
<tr>
<th>Signal</th>
<th>What to check</th>
<th>Red flag</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody><tr>

<td>The blog published at least three recent posts on your topic</td>
<td>The blog covers everything and has no focus</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Editorial quality</td>
<td>Posts are well written, original and useful</td>

<p>A do-follow link is a normal link that passes ranking credit. A blog that charges you for a do-follow link is selling links, which Google treats as link spam. Walk away, and do not pay. A blog that marks all guest post links as nofollow or sponsored is being honest, and a post there is still worth writing if the audience is real.</p>
<p>Also check the blog's traffic, if you can. Tools such as Similarweb give a rough estimate, but they are not exact. Do not trust a blog that claims huge traffic but shows no comments, no shares and no social following. And do not trust advice that tells you to judge a blog by its Alexa ranking, because Alexa was shut down, and any guide that still recommends it is out of date.</p>
<h3 id="step-5-pitch-with-three-copy-and-paste-templates">Step 5: Pitch, with three copy and paste templates</h3>
<p>A pitch is a short email to an editor that proposes a guest post. The best pitches are short, specific and personal. They name the editor, show you read the blog, propose two or three clear headlines, and link to one sample. They are under 150 words. Here are three templates you can copy and adapt.</p>
<p>Template 1 is for a blog you know well, where you can be specific. It gets the highest reply rate, so use it for your top five targets.</p>
<p>Subject: Pitch: practical guide on [topic] for [blog name]</p>
<p>Hello [editor name],</p>
<p>I read your post on [specific post title], and I noticed you have not covered [the gap your post would fill]. I would like to write a guest post for [blog name] called "[your headline]".</p>
<p>The post would cover [two or three points, in one sentence]. I write about [your topic] at [your blog], and here is a sample: [link to one sample].</p>
<p>Would this fit your blog? I can send a draft within a week.</p>
<p>Thank you,
[Your name]</p>
<p>Template 2 is for a blog you know less well, where you can still show you read it. Use it for your next ten targets.</p>
<p>Subject: Guest post idea for [blog name]</p>
<p>Hello [editor name],</p>
<p>I enjoy [blog name], especially your recent post on [post title]. I would like to propose a guest post on one of these topics:</p>
<ol>
<li>[Headline one]</li>
<li>[Headline two]</li>
<li>[Headline three]</li>
</ol>
<p>I write about [your topic] at [your blog]. Here is a sample of my work: [link to one sample].</p>
<p>I would be happy to follow your guidelines. Please let me know if any of these ideas fit.</p>
<p>Thank you,
[Your name]</p>
<p>Template 3 is a quick pitch for a blog with clear guidelines that ask for a short pitch. Use it only where the blog asks for brevity.</p>
<p>Subject: Guest post pitch: [headline]</p>
<p>Hello,</p>
<p>I would like to write "[headline]" for [blog name]. It is a practical guide for [reader], covering [two points]. I write at [your blog], and here is a sample: [link].</p>
<p>Thank you,
[Your name]</p>
<p>Send five pitches a week, not fifty in a day. Follow up once, four to six days after the first email, with a short note. Follow up a second time seven to ten days after that, if you heard nothing. Then stop. Three messages is the limit. More than that is spam, and editors remember writers who spam them.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/guest-post-pitch-template-notes.jpg" alt="A notebook with a short handwritten outline for a guest post beside a laptop and a pen on a wooden desk" /></p>
<h3 id="step-6-write-a-post-editors-accept">Step 6: Write a post editors accept</h3>
<p>Once an editor says yes, read the guidelines again and follow them exactly. Guidelines tell you the word count, the heading style, the image rules, and the link policy. Editors reject posts that ignore their guidelines, however good the writing is.</p>
<p>Read five to ten recent posts on the host blog before you write. Match their voice and their format. If they use short paragraphs and many headings, do the same. If they use a formal tone, do not write slang. Write the post as well as your best work on your own blog, or better, because this post is your audition for a new audience.</p>
<p>Put the value first. The post should teach the reader something useful, not promote your blog. Mention your blog only where it helps the reader, and link to it only where the host allows it. Use your own images or images you have the right to use, and say where they came from. Check every fact, number and link before you send the draft, because the editor will not fact-check for you.</p>
<p>Meet your deadline. If you said a week, send it in a week. If you need more time, tell the editor before the deadline, not after. Editors work with writers who deliver on time, and they stop replying to writers who do not.</p>
<h3 id="step-7-after-publishing-promote-and-track">Step 7: After publishing, promote and track</h3>
<p><img src="/covers/guest-post-published-promotion.jpg" alt="A laptop and a smartphone on a desk showing a published blog article and a simple traffic chart, with a notebook beside them" /></p>
<p>Your job does not end when the post goes live. Share it on your social media, link to it from your own blog where it fits, and reply to every comment on it. The host blog notices writers who promote their posts, and those writers get invited back.</p>
<p>Add UTM tags to the links in your guest post, so you can see the traffic in your analytics. A UTM tag is a small code added to a link that tells your analytics where the click came from. For example, a link to your pillar page with a UTM tag that says source, guest post, and host blog name will show up in your analytics as traffic from that post. If the host blog does not allow UTM tags, use a short link to your pillar page instead, and check your referral traffic in your analytics.</p>
<p>Stay in touch with the editor. Thank them, ask how the post performed, and offer a second post if the first did well. One good relationship with an editor is worth more than ten one-off posts, because it leads to repeat invitations and introductions to other blogs.</p>
<h3 id="step-8-measure-results">Step 8: Measure results</h3>
<p>Guest posting takes time, so measure whether it is worth it. Check three numbers for each post, one week and one month after it goes live.</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Metric</th>
<th>Where to find it</th>
<th>What good looks like</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody><tr>
<td>Referral visits</td>
<td>Your analytics, referral traffic</td>
<td>20 to 100 visits in the first month for a small host, more for a big one</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Email signups</td>
<td>Your email tool, traced to the post</td>
<td>5 to 20 new subscribers per post</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Link and ranking</td>
<td>Your analytics and search console</td>
<td>A link to your pillar page, and a small ranking lift over 6 to 12 weeks</td>
</tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>Referral traffic usually arrives within days, while search impact takes 6 to 12 weeks, if it comes at all. Do not judge a guest post by its search impact in the first week. Judge it by the readers it sent you and the subscribers it earned. If a post brought no visits and no signups, ask why: was the topic wrong, was the audience wrong, or was the link buried where nobody saw it? Then fix it in the next post.</p>
<p>If a link changes or is removed later, email the editor politely and ask about it. Links break when blogs redesign, and most editors will fix a broken link if you point it out. Do not demand a link back, and do not threaten anyone. A polite note works better than a complaint.</p>
<h3 id="worked-example-what-three-guest-posts-could-return">Worked example: what three guest posts could return</h3>
<p>Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Imagine you write three guest posts in one month, and each one takes four hours to pitch, write and promote. That is 12 hours in total. Each post brings 50 referral visits in its first month, which is 150 visits in total. If 10 percent of those visitors join your email list, that is 15 new subscribers. If your time is worth 25 US dollars an hour, the 12 hours cost 300 US dollars of your time, and 300 divided by 15 is 20 US dollars per subscriber. That sounds expensive, but those 15 subscribers are readers who chose your blog, and they are worth more than 150 casual visitors. Over a year, if each subscriber reads your posts and a few buy your products, the return grows. The point is not the exact figure. It is that guest posting pays in audience and trust, not in quick cash.</p>
<h3 id="common-mistakes-and-red-flags">Common mistakes and red flags</h3>
<ul>
<li>Pitching a blog you have never read, with a generic email that could go to anyone.</li>
<li>Proposing a topic the host blog already covered, which shows you did not check.</li>
<li>Asking for three or four links with keyword-heavy anchor text, which Google treats as link spam.</li>
<li>Paying for a guest post link, which is a paid link and must be marked as sponsored or nofollow.</li>
<li>Writing a thin post for the host blog, then wondering why the editor never invites you back.</li>
<li>Missing your deadline without telling the editor.</li>
<li>Following up five times, which turns a pitch into spam.</li>
<li>Trusting a blog that claims huge traffic but shows no comments, no shares and no social following.</li>
<li>Judging a blog by its Alexa ranking, which is out of date advice, because Alexa was shut down.</li>
<li>Writing guest posts only for links, and ignoring the readers they could bring you.</li>
</ul>
<p>Watch for red flags in the other direction too. A blog that charges for a do-follow link, a blog that publishes guest posts on unrelated topics, and a blog that asks you to include commercial links with exact-match anchors are all signs of a link scheme. Walk away, and spend your time on a real blog instead. If a brand deal or a guest post offer looks like a scam, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-jobs/how-to-spot-online-job-scams" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to spot online job scams</a> before you reply.</p>
<h3 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Is guest posting still worth it in 2026?</h4>
<p>Yes, when it is done honestly. A guest post on a real blog in your niche can bring new readers, new subscribers and a useful link. It is not worth it as a link scheme, because Google treats links placed mainly for ranking credit as link spam, and a post written only for links rarely brings readers either.</p>
<h4>Are paid guest posts against Google guidelines?</h4>
<p>Paying for a guest post is not itself against the rules, but the link is. If money changes hands, the link must be qualified with rel="sponsored" or rel="nofollow", so it does not pass ranking credit. A blog that sells do-follow links without marking them is breaking Google's rules, and a link from such a blog can harm your site.</p>
<h4>How do I find blogs that accept guest posts?</h4>
<p>Use Google search operators, such as your topic plus "write for us" or your topic plus "accepting guest posts". Look at where competitors guest post, ask in blogging communities, and read the blogs you already follow. Build a target list of 10 to 30 blogs, and qualify each one before you pitch.</p>
<h4>What should a guest post pitch include?</h4>
<p>A good pitch is under 150 words. It names the editor, shows you read the blog, proposes two or three clear headlines, links to one writing sample, and says when you can deliver a draft. Follow up once after four to six days, and stop after three messages in total.</p>
<h4>Should guest post links be nofollow?</h4>
<p>It depends on the link. A link you paid for must be marked rel="sponsored" or rel="nofollow". A link in your author bio or a body link to a useful resource on your blog can be a normal link, if the post is genuinely useful and the link helps the reader. Follow the host blog's link policy, and never ask for keyword-heavy anchor text.</p>
<h4>How do I guest post when my blog is brand new?</h4>
<p>Write two or three strong posts on your own blog first, so you have samples to show. Then pitch smaller blogs in your niche, where editors are more likely to say yes to a new writer. Offer a genuinely useful post, meet your deadline, and use the published post as your next sample.</p>
<h3 id="conclusion-pitch-real-blogs-write-your-best-post-measure-the-return">Conclusion: pitch real blogs, write your best post, measure the return</h3>
<p>Learning how to guest post for blog growth comes down to three moves. Pitch real blogs in your niche with a short, personal email, write a post as good as your best work on your own blog, and measure the readers and subscribers it brings you. Do not buy links, do not spam editors, and do not write for blogs you would not read yourself.</p>
