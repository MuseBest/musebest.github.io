How to Monetise a Blog with Affiliate Marketing in 2026

Learn how to monetise a blog with affiliate marketing in 2026. Join the right programs, write content that converts, disclose clearly, and track results.

How to monetise a blog with affiliate marketing is a question many bloggers ask when they have useful posts and want income without creating a product. Affiliate marketing lets you earn a commission when a reader buys a product through your link, but it only works when the products fit your readers and your content is honest. This guide shows you the full method, from joining programs to tracking what works.

What is affiliate marketing for bloggers?

Affiliate marketing is a simple deal. A company gives you a special link for its product. You place that link in a blog post. When a reader clicks the link and buys, the company pays you a commission, which is a share of the sale. The reader pays the same price either way. You earn because you sent the buyer.

How it works in 6 steps

First, you join an affiliate program and get your links. Second, you write a post that helps a reader solve a problem, and you mention the product where it fits naturally. Third, a reader clicks the link and lands on the product page. Fourth, a small file called a cookie records that the reader came from your blog. A cookie duration is the number of days that record lasts, often 30 days, so you can still earn if the reader buys a few days later. Fifth, the reader buys, and the program records the sale. Sixth, the program pays you, usually after a delay of weeks, once the sale is confirmed and any return period passes.

You do not need a huge audience to start. You need readers who trust you and posts that match what they want to buy.

What can you really earn?

There is no typical figure, because earnings depend on traffic, the products you promote, commission rates, and how many readers buy. Some blogs earn a few dollars a month for a year, then grow slowly. Treat any income figure you read online with care, and never plan spending around income that has not arrived yet.

Commission rates vary widely by product type. Physical products often pay a small share, such as 1 to 4 percent of the sale price. Digital products and software often pay more, sometimes 30 to 50 percent, because they cost little to deliver. Check each program's own page for its current rate, because rates change and other blogs may quote old figures.

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Imagine a review post that gets 1,000 visitors in a month. If 4 percent of those visitors click an affiliate link, that is 1,000 times 0.04, which equals 40 clicks. If 5 percent of those clicks turn into a sale, that is 40 times 0.05, which equals 2 sales. At 25 US dollars commission per sale, that is 2 times 25 US dollars, which equals 50 US dollars for that post in that month. Check the maths: 1,000 times 0.04 is 40, 40 times 0.05 is 2, and 2 times 25 is 50. That figure is only an example. Most new blogs take months to reach that traffic, and click and conversion rates vary widely.

Step 1: Pick products your readers already need

Start with your readers, not with the commission. List the products your readers already buy, or already ask you about. A finance blog's readers buy budgeting apps, and a tech blog's readers buy phones and software. If a product does not solve a real problem for your reader, do not promote it.

Check each product yourself before you recommend it. Use it if you can. If you cannot use it, read its official page and honest user reviews, and compare it with similar products. Never recommend a product only because its commission is high. Readers can tell when a recommendation is driven by money.

Make a short list of 5 to 10 products that fit your blog. You will add to it as the blog grows, and drop products that disappoint your readers.

Step 2: Find affiliate programs worth joining

You can join programs in two ways. Direct programs are run by the company itself, such as Amazon Associates, which is Amazon's own affiliate program. Networks are platforms that host many programs in one place, such as Impact, Awin, ShareASale, and CJ Affiliate. Networks let you apply to many brands from one account, and they handle tracking and payments.

Check the program's own page for the current terms before you join. A payout threshold is the minimum amount you must earn before the program pays you.

Direct programs versus networks

Direct programs suit you when one brand matters most to your blog. Networks suit you when you want choice, because you can compare many brands in one place. Start with one network and one or two direct programs.

If you want program ideas for specific niches, read our guides on finance affiliate programs and how to earn with Amazon Associates.

Step 3: Apply and get accepted

Most programs ask for the same things: your blog URL, a short description of your blog and audience, how you plan to promote products, and your traffic or audience size. Prepare those answers once, in a short paragraph, and reuse them. A clear description helps: say who reads your blog, what topics you cover, and how you will link to products, for example in honest reviews and tutorials.

Apply to programs that fit your blog. A new blog can be accepted, especially if it has 10 or more useful posts and a clear topic. Programs prefer a focused blog with 10 good posts over a scattered blog with 100 thin ones. If your traffic is small, say so honestly and explain your plan to grow it.

What to do if your application is rejected

Rejection is common, and it is not the end. Read the rejection message carefully. It often says why, such as too little content or a topic that does not fit. Fix what you can, then reapply after a month or two, with a short note about what changed.

If a program rejects you twice, move on. There are many programs, and one rejection does not mean your blog cannot earn. Try a similar brand, or a network that hosts similar products. Do not pay anyone who offers to get you accepted. Real programs are free to join, and no one can guarantee acceptance.

Step 4: Set up link management before your first link

Set up link management from your first post. Link management means using short, tidy links that you can update in one place. A link management plugin, such as ThirstyAffiliates or Pretty Links, turns a long affiliate URL into a short link on your own domain, such as your blog address followed by a short product name. If the product URL changes, you update it once in the plugin, and every post updates at once.

Without link management, a changed product link means editing every post by hand. Set it up now, while you have few posts, and it will save you hours later.

Keep a simple spreadsheet of your links too, with the product, the program, the short link, and the date you added it. That list helps you check for broken links each month.

Step 5: Write affiliate content that converts

Content that converts is content that helps a reader decide. A reader who trusts your post is more likely to click and buy than a reader who feels sold to.

Content types that convert

Reviews work best when they are honest and detailed, with strengths, weaknesses, and who the product suits. Comparisons help a reader choose between two products, with a clear table of differences. Roundups list the best options for one need, such as the best budgeting apps, with a short verdict on each. Tutorials show how to use a product to solve a problem, and the link fits naturally inside the steps.

Write for the reader's problem first, and place the link where it helps. A link in the introduction feels pushy. A link after you have explained the problem and shown how the product solves it feels helpful. Use two or three links per post at most, not ten.

How to write an honest review

Test the product if you can, and say so. List what is good and what is not, and say who should buy it and who should skip it. Compare it with one alternative. Our guide on how to write affiliate product reviews explains the review structure in more detail.

Step 6: Disclose clearly

Disclosure is not optional. It is a legal duty in many countries, and a trust duty everywhere. A disclosure tells the reader that you may earn a commission if they buy through your link. It must be clear, early, and easy to see.

Place a short disclosure at the top of any post with affiliate links, in plain words, such as: "This post contains affiliate links. If you buy through a link, I may earn a commission at no extra cost to you." Place a second short note near the first affiliate link in the post, so a reader who skips the top still sees it. Link the disclosure to a full affiliate disclosure page on your blog, such as the EarnQuill affiliate disclosure page.

The US Federal Trade Commission explains when affiliate relationships must be disclosed in its guide to the FTC Endorsement Guides: What People Are Asking. The guide is written for US readers, but its plain rule is useful everywhere: if you earn from a recommendation, say so, clearly, where readers will see it before they act on it.

Search engines also expect affiliate links to be marked. Use the rel="sponsored" attribute on affiliate links, which tells search engines the link is a paid placement. Your link management plugin can add this automatically. Do not hide affiliate links, and do not bury the disclosure where no one sees it.

Disclosure rules for email and Pinterest differ by program. Some programs allow affiliate links in email newsletters, and some do not. Some allow them on Pinterest, and some do not. Check each program's own terms before you share a link outside your blog, and add a short disclosure wherever the link appears.

Step 7: Track clicks and sales, then improve what works

Tracking tells you which posts earn and which do not. Set it up in three steps.

First, use your link management plugin to count clicks on each link. That shows which posts send readers to products. Second, check each affiliate program's dashboard for sales and commissions. That shows which clicks turn into money. Third, record both figures each month in a spreadsheet, next to the post that earned them. Our guide on how to track affiliate earnings explains the tracking setup in more detail.

Then improve what works. If a review post earns well, update it or write a follow up on a related product. If a post gets many clicks but no sales, the product may not fit the readers, or the price may be too high. Try a different product, or rewrite the post to explain it better. If a post gets no clicks, the link may be badly placed. Move it higher, or write a post that targets buyers more directly.

Where else to share affiliate links

Your blog is the home for affiliate links, but two other places can help. Pinterest can send readers to your posts, and those posts contain the links. Pin each post to a board that matches the topic, and let the post do the selling. Do not paste raw affiliate links into pins unless the program clearly allows it.

Email can work well too, because email readers already trust you. Mention a product in a newsletter only when it fits the topic, and add a short disclosure in the email itself. A resources page on your blog can also list the products you recommend most, with a short note on why each one is there.

You can also earn through social posts and other channels, but a blog gives you control and a lasting home for your links, which is why this guide focuses on the blog route.

What it costs and what to expect

Affiliate marketing costs little to start, but it takes time to earn. The main costs are the blog itself and a link management plugin, which is often free or low cost. Your time is the real cost. Writing one good review can take 5 to 10 hours, including testing and editing.

Be honest with yourself about the timeline. The stages below are typical, not promised. Your results will vary with your niche, your traffic, and the products you choose.

Stage Time What usually happens
Setup and first links Months 1 to 3 Programs joined, links placed, almost no clicks, no income
Early clicks Months 4 to 9 Clicks start, a few sales possible, income still very small
Steady growth Months 10 to 18 More posts earn, income becomes more regular but still modest
Established After 18 months Several posts earn, and income grows with traffic

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Imagine one review post that earns 50 US dollars in a month, as in the worked example above. If 10 posts each earn the same, that is 10 times 50 US dollars, which equals 500 US dollars in that month. Check the maths: 10 times 50 is 500. Most posts earn far less, some earn nothing, and a few earn more.

Common mistakes

  • Promoting products your readers do not need, because the commission is high.
  • Placing too many affiliate links in one post, so it reads like a sales page.
  • Writing reviews of products you have never checked, and pretending you tested them.
  • Hiding the disclosure, or leaving it out entirely.
  • Joining every program you find instead of a few that fit your blog.
  • Ignoring tracking, so you never learn which posts actually earn.
  • Quitting after three months because the income has not arrived yet.
  • Copying another blogger's review instead of writing your own honest test.

Risks and scams to watch for

Affiliate marketing attracts scams, so stay alert. Be wary of any program that asks you to pay a fee to join. Real affiliate programs are free to join, and a joining fee is a warning sign. Be wary of programs that promise high commissions for products with no real buyers, or that ask you to recruit other affiliates to earn.

Watch out for cookie stuffing, which means placing affiliate cookies on a visitor's device without a real click. It is against the rules of every honest program, and it can get your account closed and your earnings removed. Never use tricks to force clicks. Earn clicks by writing useful posts.

Also check the products you promote. A product with many complaints, a poor refund policy, or a misleading sales page will cost you reader trust. If readers buy a bad product through your link, they blame you, not just the company. Drop products that disappoint your readers, even if the commission is good.

Tax rules differ by country. Affiliate income is usually taxable, so keep records of every payment from the start, and check your local tax authority or a qualified accountant for the rules where you live.

Frequently asked questions

What is affiliate marketing for bloggers?

Affiliate marketing is a way for bloggers to earn a commission when a reader buys a product through a special link in a post. The blogger joins a program, places links in useful posts, and earns when a reader clicks and buys. The reader pays the same price either way.

How do I join an affiliate program?

Find a program that fits your blog, either directly from the brand or through a network such as Impact, Awin, ShareASale, or CJ Affiliate. Apply with your blog URL, a short description of your blog and audience, and how you plan to promote products. A focused blog with 10 or more useful posts has a good chance of acceptance.

How many affiliate links should a blog post have?

Two or three links per post is enough for most posts. Place them where they help the reader, such as after you have explained the problem and shown how the product solves it. One clear link beats five pushy ones.

Do I have to disclose affiliate links?

Yes, you must disclose them. Place a short disclosure at the top of any post with affiliate links, and a second note near the first link. Link to a full affiliate disclosure page. The US Federal Trade Commission guide on endorsement rules explains the duty in detail, and the plain rule is simple: if you earn from a recommendation, say so clearly.

Can I use affiliate links on Pinterest?

It depends on the program. Some programs allow affiliate links on Pinterest, and some forbid them. Check each program's own terms before you share a link outside your blog. A safer route is to pin your blog post and let the post carry the links, with a clear disclosure.

How long until affiliate marketing earns money?

Most blogs earn little or nothing from affiliate links for the first few months, because they need traffic and trust first. A first small commission often comes after several months of steady publishing. There is no fixed timeline, and anyone who promises one is selling a course, not a fact.

Conclusion: how to monetise a blog with affiliate marketing in 2026

That is how to monetise a blog with affiliate marketing in 2026: pick products your readers already need, join a few programs that fit, set up link management before your first link, write honest reviews and comparisons, disclose clearly at the top of every post, and track clicks and sales so you can improve what works. Start with one product and one honest post this week, and build from there.
