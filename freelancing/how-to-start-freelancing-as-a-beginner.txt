1e:Tea4,<h2 id="start-with-one-service-not-ten">Start with one service, not ten</h2>
<p>New freelancers often list every skill they have. Clients do the opposite: they search for one specific problem solved well. Pick the service you can already deliver to a decent standard — writing, design, video editing, data entry, virtual assistance, translation — and make that your offer.</p>
<p>Write it down in one sentence: "I help [who] do [what] so they get [result]." If you cannot fill in the blanks, you are not ready to pitch yet.</p>
<h2 id="set-up-the-basics-before-you-look-for-clients">Set up the basics before you look for clients</h2>
<p>You need four things before your first pitch:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A way to be paid.</strong> A bank account or payment service that works in your country, and a simple invoice template.</li>
<li><strong>A portfolio, even a small one.</strong> Three sample pieces beat an empty profile. Create them for fictional clients if you have no real ones yet, and label them as samples.</li>
<li><strong>A rate.</strong> Research what others charge for similar work in your market, then price by project or by hour — and write the rate down before a client asks.</li>
<li><strong>A contract habit.</strong> Even a short email confirming scope, deadline, revisions, and payment terms prevents most disputes.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="where-to-find-your-first-clients">Where to find your first clients</h2>
<p>There are three realistic routes, and most beginners should try all three:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Route</th>
<th>How it works</th>
<th>Watch out for</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody><tr>
<td>Freelance marketplaces</td>
<td>Clients post jobs; you bid</td>
<td>High competition, platform fees</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Direct outreach</td>
<td>You email businesses that need your service</td>
<td>Low reply rates at first</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Your own network</td>
<td>Former colleagues, local businesses, friends</td>
<td>Small at first, but converts best</td>
</tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>On marketplaces, your first reviews matter more than your first paycheque. A small, well-delivered job that earns a good review is worth more than a larger job that ends badly.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-price-your-first-jobs">How to price your first jobs</h2>
<p>Start by estimating the hours a job will take, multiply by a modest hourly rate, and add a margin for revisions and communication. If a client wants a fixed price, agree the scope in writing first — "one logo, two revision rounds, delivered in five days" — so the price has a boundary.</p>
<p>Raise your rate after every three to five successful jobs, or when you are turning work away. That is the market telling you that you are underpriced.</p>
<h2 id="common-beginner-mistakes">Common beginner mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Saying yes to everything.</strong> A bad-fit client costs more time than the fee is worth.</li>
<li><strong>Working without a deposit.</strong> For new clients, ask for part of the fee upfront.</li>
<li><strong>No boundaries.</strong> Agree working hours and revision limits in advance.</li>
<li><strong>Hiding your work.</strong> Share what you make. Clients hire people they have seen do the work.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="your-first-month-in-order">Your first month, in order</h2>
<ol>
<li>Choose one service and write your one-sentence offer.</li>
<li>Make three portfolio samples.</li>
<li>Create profiles on one or two marketplaces and a simple personal page.</li>
<li>Send five tailored pitches a day for two weeks.</li>
<li>Deliver the first job well, ask for a review, and ask for a referral.</li>
</ol>
<p>Freelancing is a slow build, not a lottery ticket. The first client is the hardest; the system that found them is what you repeat.</p>
