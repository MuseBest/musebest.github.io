<h2 id="what-a-freelance-email-marketer-does">What a freelance email marketer does</h2>
<p>A freelance email marketer is an independent worker who runs email programmes for different clients. The work includes writing campaigns, building automated flows, managing lists and segments, setting up templates, testing subject lines, and reporting results in plain language. A flow is a set of emails sent automatically when a person takes an action, such as joining a list or leaving items in a cart.</p>
<p>A segment is a defined group of subscribers, such as recent buyers or people who have not opened in months. Deliverability means whether emails reach the inbox instead of the spam folder. Good freelancers protect deliverability as carefully as they write copy, because undelivered emails earn nothing.</p>

<h2 id="step-1-pick-one-platform-and-one-type-of-client">Step 1: Pick one platform and one type of client</h2>
<p>Learn one email platform deeply instead of five platforms lightly. As of November 2026, common client platforms include Klaviyo for online shops, Mailchimp for general small businesses, and Kit or MailerLite for creators and newsletters. Clients tend to stay on the platform they already use, so depth on one platform is a real selling point.</p>
<p>Pick one client type to start, such as small online shops, local service businesses, or course creators. Each type needs different emails, offers, and measures of success. Depth in one niche helps you reuse what works and explain results with confidence.</p>
<p>Write your offer in one sentence that names the platform, the client type, and the first service. For example, you could offer welcome and cart flows for small online shops on Klaviyo. A narrow offer is easier to price and easier for a client to say yes to.</p>
<h2 id="step-2-build-the-skills-clients-pay-for">Step 2: Build the skills clients pay for</h2>
<p>Clients pay for emails that reach inboxes and produce clicks, replies, and sales. The core skills are clear writing with one call to action, platform setup, segmentation and automation logic, deliverability basics, and honest reporting. A call to action is the single next step you ask the reader to take.</p>
<p>Deliverability basics include SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, which are technical records that prove an email really comes from the business domain. You also need list hygiene, which means removing inactive or invalid addresses and honouring unsubscribes at once. Learn these before you send for a client, because mistakes can damage their sender reputation.</p>

<h2 id="step-3-build-samples-and-a-small-portfolio">Step 3: Build samples and a small portfolio</h2>
<p>Build samples before you pitch. Write a welcome series of three to five emails for a sample business, and label it clearly as a sample. Add a sample campaign, an abandoned cart email, and a one page sample report with made up numbers that are labelled as sample numbers.</p>
<p>Set the samples up in a free platform account so you can show real screens, not just documents. Study good brand emails for structure, but write your own copy in your own words. Our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-create-a-freelance-portfolio" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to create a freelance portfolio</a> explains how to present samples so clients can judge them quickly.</p>
<p>Run a small list of your own if you can, even with a handful of subscribers who chose to join. It proves you can operate the tools and follow consent rules. Never buy addresses or add people without permission, in samples or in client work.</p>

<h2 id="step-4-set-your-rates-and-packages">Step 4: Set your rates and packages</h2>
<p>Sell packages, not open ended hours, wherever you can. A setup package could cover a welcome flow plus one automation. A flow package could cover cart, post purchase, and win back flows. A monthly package could cover a set number of campaigns, basic tests, and a plain language report.</p>
<p>Estimate the hours for strategy, writing, building, testing, and reporting, then add time for client approvals and revisions. Charge extra for design work, rush deadlines, and extra revision rounds, and write those rules down. Our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-set-freelance-rates" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to set freelance rates</a> shows how to build a rate from your costs and billable hours.</p>
<p>If you work through a marketplace, check the fee before you quote. As of November 2026, Upwork states that its freelancer service fee ranges from 0 percent to 15 percent per contract, fixed once the contract begins. Read the detail on <a href="https://support.upwork.com/hc/en-us/articles/211062538-Learn-about-the-Freelancer-Service-Fee" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Upwork's official freelancer service fee page</a> and build the fee into your price.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-find-your-first-clients">Step 5: Find your first clients</h2>
<p>Start with warm outreach to businesses you know and past contacts who run shops or services. Then subscribe to newsletters in your niche and note clear gaps, such as no welcome email or a broken discount code. Send a short pitch through the business contact route that names one specific gap and offers a small audit.</p>
<p>Offer a low risk first step, such as a fixed price email audit with three quick wins. It gives the client a useful report and gives you a safe way to learn their account. Marketplaces can add early projects, but competition is strong. Our guides on <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-find-freelance-clients" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to find freelance clients</a> and <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-find-high-paying-freelance-clients" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to find high paying freelance clients</a> cover both routes. If freelancing itself is new, start with <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-start-freelancing" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to start freelancing</a>.</p>
<p>Ask happy clients for referrals to similar businesses, and stay in touch with designers and web developers who serve the same niche. Referrals convert better than cold pitches because trust travels with them. Never promise specific revenue results to win work.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-send-safely-and-report-honestly">Step 6: Send safely and report honestly</h2>
<p>Get written permission and proper user access to the client platform. Never use shared passwords, and never import bought or scraped lists. Only email people who gave permission, include an unsubscribe link in every email, and follow the email and privacy laws that apply in the client country.</p>
<p>Test before every send. Check links, personalisation, mobile layout, and the authentication records on the sending domain. Get client approval in writing before campaigns go out, and keep records of approvals and results.</p>
<p>Report in plain language each month. Show clicks, conversions, revenue per recipient, list growth, and any deliverability problems, with one clear next step. Put scope, payment terms, confidentiality, and data handling in a written agreement before you start.</p>
<h2 id="worked-example-pricing-a-welcome-flow-project">Worked example: pricing a welcome flow project</h2>
<p>Example only: these numbers are not typical results, and your rates and hours will differ.</p>
<p>A small online shop asks for a welcome flow of four emails, built and tested in their platform. You estimate 2 hours for strategy and account checks, 5 hours for writing and building, 2 hours for testing and fixes, and 1 hour for handover and a short report. That is 10 hours in total.</p>
<p>Your target rate is 35 US dollars per hour. The project price is 10 times 35, which equals 350 US dollars. If the work runs through a marketplace with a 10 percent fee in this example, the fee is 35 US dollars, and you receive 315 US dollars. Recheck the maths: 350 minus 35 equals 315, and 315 divided by 10 hours equals 31.50 US dollars per hour after the fee.</p>
<p>If the client later wants monthly campaigns, quote a separate monthly package with a set number of emails and a report. Written packages stop extra sends from becoming free work.</p>
<h2 id="what-it-costs-and-what-to-expect">What it costs and what to expect</h2>
<p>Your main costs are a reliable computer, internet access, and time. Free platform tiers and free platform courses keep starting costs low. You may later pay for design tools or a portfolio site, but neither is needed for your first client.</p>
<p>Expect a slow start. Learning one platform and building samples can take several weeks of part time work. Your first paid project may take one to three months to win, and early income is often uneven. Results vary with your niche, your writing skill, demand, competition, and how much time you spend on outreach.</p>
<p>Tax rules differ by country. In the United States, the IRS states that freelance and gig income is taxable and must be reported, even for part time work. Read the detail on the <a href="https://www.irs.gov/businesses/gig-economy-tax-center" rel="noopener" target="_blank">IRS Gig Economy Tax Center</a> and check your local rules with a qualified accountant.</p>
<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Emailing people who never gave permission, which breaks the law and ruins deliverability.</li>
<li>Reporting open rates as success while clicks and sales stay flat.</li>
<li>Skipping authentication checks on the sending domain before big sends.</li>
<li>Sending the same message to the whole list with no segments.</li>
<li>Promising specific revenue results to win a client.</li>
<li>Taking on too many clients at once and missing send dates.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="risks-and-scams-to-watch-for">Risks and scams to watch for</h2>
<p>Watch for clients who ask you to pay for tools, lists, or training before work starts. Honest clients pay you; they do not charge you to work. The United States Federal Trade Commission lists warning signs on its <a href="https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/job-scams" rel="noopener" target="_blank">job scams page</a>, including upfront fees and promises of fast money.</p>
<p>Protect yourself with a written agreement, a deposit for new clients, and proper platform access in your own name. Never share customer data outside the client systems, and be careful with customer data in AI tools. A single bad send to a bought list can damage a client domain for months, so refuse that work plainly.</p>
<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 id="do-i-need-marketing-experience-to-be-a-freelance-email-marketer">Do I need marketing experience to be a freelance email marketer?</h3>
<p>No, but you do need clear writing and platform skill. Build samples, learn one platform through its free courses, and start with a small audit offer. Proof of work matters more than job titles for most small clients.</p>
<h3 id="which-email-platform-should-i-learn-first">Which email platform should I learn first?</h3>
<p>Learn the platform your target clients already use. Online shops often use Klaviyo, general small businesses often use Mailchimp, and creators often use Kit or MailerLite. Depth on one platform beats shallow knowledge of many.</p>
<h3 id="how-is-email-marketing-different-from-copywriting">How is email marketing different from copywriting?</h3>
<p>Copywriting focuses on persuasive text. Email marketing adds platform setup, automation flows, segmentation, deliverability, and measurement. The writing matters, but the system around it decides whether emails reach inboxes and produce results.</p>
<h3 id="should-i-charge-by-the-hour-or-by-the-package">Should I charge by the hour or by the package?</h3>
<p>Charge by the package for flows and monthly campaigns, because clients prefer a known total. Use hourly rates for audits or advice with no fixed scope. Write deliverables, revision limits, and reporting into every package.</p>
<h3 id="can-i-use-ai-tools-in-client-email-work">Can I use AI tools in client email work?</h3>
<p>Yes, with care and client agreement. Use AI for drafts and ideas, then edit in the client voice and check every fact and link. Never paste private customer data into tools the client has not approved.</p>
<h3 id="what-results-should-i-report-to-clients">What results should I report to clients?</h3>
<p>Report clicks, conversions, revenue per recipient, list growth, and deliverability health. Open rates are inflated by mail app features, so treat them as a weak signal. Always tie the report to one clear next step.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Working as a freelance email marketer means selling flows and campaigns as clear packages on one platform. Pick one platform and one client type, build honest samples, price the full work including testing and reporting, and protect deliverability and consent on every send. Start with a small paid audit, and let honest reporting win retainer work.</p>
