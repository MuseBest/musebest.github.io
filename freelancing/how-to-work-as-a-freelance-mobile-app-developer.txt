<p>A freelance mobile app developer is an independent worker who designs, builds, tests, and maintains mobile applications for different clients. The work includes turning designs into working screens, connecting the app to data services, handling sign in and notifications, testing on real devices, and preparing store submissions.</p>
<p>An API is a defined way for the app to request data or actions from a server. Most useful apps depend on one, so integration skill matters as much as screen building. Clients also need honest advice on scope, because an app idea can grow faster than a budget.</p>

<h2 id="step-1-pick-one-platform-path-and-one-starting-service">Step 1: Pick one platform path and one starting service</h2>
<p>Choose one path to start: native iOS with Swift, native Android with Kotlin, or cross platform with Flutter or React Native. Native means building separately for each phone system with its own tools. Cross platform means one shared codebase that runs on both, with some platform specific work still needed.</p>
<p>Pick a narrow starting service that matches your proof. Examples include fixing bugs in an existing Flutter app, building a small native iOS utility, or connecting app screens to a documented API. A narrow offer helps clients judge your fit and helps you price with confidence.</p>
<p>Write your offer in one sentence that names the platform, the service, and the client type. Avoid claiming you can build any app for anyone, because clients cannot test that claim. Specialising by industry, such as booking apps for local services, can come later once you have proof.</p>
<h2 id="step-2-build-the-skills-clients-pay-for">Step 2: Build the skills clients pay for</h2>
<p>Clients pay for apps that work on real phones, not for long lists of tools. As of November 2026, the core skills are one platform stack in depth, API and sign in handling, local storage and offline behaviour, testing on real devices, and release management. Release management means preparing signed builds, test versions, and store materials in a repeatable way.</p>

<h2 id="step-3-build-a-portfolio-of-complete-small-apps">Step 3: Build a portfolio of complete small apps</h2>
<p>Build two or three complete small apps that show more than pretty screens. Each app should handle loading, errors, empty states, and at least one real integration, such as a public API. Record a short walkthrough video for each app and write a case note that explains the problem, your scope, your decisions, and how you tested it.</p>
<p>Label practice projects honestly as practice projects. Never imply that a personal project was paid client work, and never invent download or revenue numbers. Our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-create-a-freelance-portfolio" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to create a freelance portfolio</a> shows how to present projects so clients can judge them quickly.</p>
<p>If you can publish a small app to a store under your own account, do so. A live listing proves you can finish the release process, which many beginners cannot show. Keep the app simple and useful, and maintain it so it stays working.</p>

<h2 id="step-4-set-your-rates-and-packages">Step 4: Set your rates and packages</h2>
<p>Price by milestone where you can. A sensible sequence is paid discovery, a prototype, core build, release preparation, and maintenance. Each milestone should end with something the client can test, and payment should be tied to that delivery.</p>
<p>Estimate more than coding time. Include meetings, testing on devices, store materials, fixes after review, and handover documents. Separate defects from changes in writing: a defect is work that fails an agreed requirement, and a change is a new or altered requirement. Our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-set-freelance-rates" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to set freelance rates</a> shows how to build a sustainable rate from your costs and billable hours.</p>
<p>If you work through a marketplace, check the fee before you quote. As of November 2026, Upwork states that its freelancer service fee ranges from 0 percent to 15 percent per contract, fixed once the contract begins. Read the detail on <a href="https://support.upwork.com/hc/en-us/articles/211062538-Learn-about-the-Freelancer-Service-Fee" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Upwork's official freelancer service fee page</a> and build the fee into your price.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-find-your-first-clients">Step 5: Find your first clients</h2>
<p>Start with people who already know your work, such as former colleagues, designers, and small agencies. Agencies often need mobile help for client projects without a full time hire. Then approach businesses in one niche with a specific observation about their app or their booking process.</p>
<p>Marketplaces can add early projects, but competition is strong and many briefs are vague. Use them for defined tasks while you build direct relationships. Our guides on <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-find-freelance-clients" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to find freelance clients</a> and <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-find-high-paying-freelance-clients" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to find high paying freelance clients</a> cover both routes. If freelancing itself is new, start with <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-start-freelancing" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to start freelancing</a>.</p>
<p>Offer a small paid discovery task as a first step, such as a review of an existing app or a clickable prototype. It lowers client risk and shows how you work. Never build a full app for free in the hope of later payment.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-deliver-submit-and-maintain-the-app">Step 6: Deliver, submit, and maintain the app</h2>
<p>Agree acceptance criteria before you build. For each feature, write what the user can do and how it will be tested. Demo working builds at each milestone, and keep a written list of what is in scope and what is not.</p>
<p>Store submission is not the same as approval. As of November 2026, Apple states that the Apple Developer Program costs 99 US dollars per membership year, and membership is needed to distribute apps on the App Store. You can check the current fee on <a href="https://developer.apple.com/help/account/membership/program-enrollment/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Apple's official programme enrolment page</a>. The client should own the developer accounts and the code repository, with access granted to you, so they are never locked out of their own product.</p>
<p>Agree maintenance before launch. Phone systems, libraries, and server services change, so apps need updates to keep working. Offer a monthly retainer with clear response times, or a paid check every few months. Put ownership, confidentiality, and payment terms in a written agreement before you start.</p>
<h2 id="worked-example-pricing-a-small-booking-app-build">Worked example: pricing a small booking app build</h2>
<p>Example only: these numbers are not typical results, and your rates and hours will differ.</p>
<p>A local service business wants an app where customers view times and book a slot using their existing booking system. You estimate 6 hours for discovery and setup, 30 hours for core screens and API work, 8 hours for device testing and fixes, and 6 hours for store preparation, documentation, and handover. That is 50 hours in total.</p>
<p>Your target rate is 35 US dollars per hour. The project price is 50 times 35, which equals 1,750 US dollars. If the work runs through a marketplace with a 10 percent fee in this example, the fee is 175 US dollars, and you receive 1,575 US dollars. Recheck the maths: 1,750 minus 175 equals 1,575, and 1,575 divided by 50 hours equals 31.50 US dollars per hour after the fee.</p>
<p>Split the price into milestones so the client pays as testable work lands. If the client later asks for payments inside the app, treat it as new scope and quote it separately.</p>
<h2 id="what-it-costs-and-what-to-expect">What it costs and what to expect</h2>
<p>Your main costs are a reliable computer, test phones, internet access, and time. iOS development needs a Mac for building and signing, which is a real cost to plan for. Store accounts are usually paid by the client for client apps, and that should be agreed in writing.</p>
<p>Expect a slow start. Two or three complete portfolio apps can take several months of part time work. Your first paid project may take one to three months to win, and early income is often uneven. Results vary with your experience, your platform choice, demand, competition, and how much time you spend on outreach.</p>
<p>Tax rules differ by country. In the United States, the IRS states that freelance and gig income is taxable and must be reported, even for part time work. Read the detail on the <a href="https://www.irs.gov/businesses/gig-economy-tax-center" rel="noopener" target="_blank">IRS Gig Economy Tax Center</a> and check your local rules with a qualified accountant.</p>
<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Quoting a fixed price for a vague idea with no written scope or acceptance criteria.</li>
<li>Assuming the client backend is ready without testing the key integrations early.</li>
<li>Leaving device testing until the end, then finding crashes on real phones.</li>
<li>Publishing under your own store account so the client cannot control their app.</li>
<li>Promising store approval dates that only the store can decide.</li>
<li>Launching with no maintenance plan, so the app breaks as systems change.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="risks-and-scams-to-watch-for">Risks and scams to watch for</h2>
<p>Watch for clients who ask you to pay for tools, training, or store fees on their behalf before work starts. Honest clients pay you; they do not charge you to work. The United States Federal Trade Commission lists warning signs on its <a href="https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/job-scams" rel="noopener" target="_blank">job scams page</a>, including upfront fees and promises of fast money.</p>
<p>Protect yourself with a written agreement, milestone payments, and a deposit for new clients. Never share your store account password or signing keys, and use proper user access instead. Be careful with apps that handle payments, health data, or children, because extra rules apply and you should check them before you accept the work.</p>
<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 id="do-i-need-a-degree-to-work-as-a-freelance-mobile-app-developer">Do I need a degree to work as a freelance mobile app developer?</h3>
<p>No, a degree is not required. Clients hire on proof, such as working apps, store listings, and clear case notes. Strong testing and release skills matter more than paper qualifications for most small clients.</p>
<h3 id="should-i-learn-native-development-or-a-cross-platform-framework-first">Should I learn native development or a cross platform framework first?</h3>
<p>Pick one path and learn it in depth. Native suits products tied to one phone system, and cross platform suits products that need both systems with shared features. Your portfolio should prove one path well rather than several paths badly.</p>
<h3 id="can-i-build-ios-apps-without-a-mac">Can I build iOS apps without a Mac?</h3>
<p>Building and signing iOS apps needs macOS tools, so most iOS freelancers use a Mac. Cloud build services exist, but they add cost and limits. Plan for the hardware cost if you choose the iOS path.</p>
<h3 id="who-should-own-the-store-accounts-and-the-code">Who should own the store accounts and the code?</h3>
<p>The client should own both, with access granted to you for the project. That keeps the client in control of their product and protects you from blame for account problems. Write ownership and handover terms into the agreement.</p>
<h3 id="how-do-i-price-an-app-when-the-scope-is-unclear">How do I price an app when the scope is unclear?</h3>
<p>Sell a paid discovery phase first. Use it to test integrations, confirm designs, and write acceptance criteria. Then quote the build in milestones with a written scope and a change process.</p>
<h3 id="what-happens-after-the-app-launches">What happens after the app launches?</h3>
<p>The app needs updates as phone systems and services change. Agree a maintenance retainer or paid periodic checks before launch. Without maintenance, even a good app slowly stops working.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Working as a freelance mobile app developer means shipping complete, tested apps as a clear service. Pick one platform path and one starting service, build small complete apps as proof, price in milestones with written acceptance criteria, and agree maintenance before launch. Start with a small paid discovery task, and let reliable delivery win repeat work.</p>
