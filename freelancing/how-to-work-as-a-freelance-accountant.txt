<h2 id="what-a-freelance-accountant-does">What a freelance accountant does</h2>
<p>A freelance accountant is an independent professional who sells accounting help to businesses, usually small and medium ones. You are not an employee.</p>
<p>The work is wider than bookkeeping. A bookkeeper records what happened, such as sales, bills, and payments.</p>
<p>Your clients are often business owners who cannot afford a full time accountant, such as shop owners, online sellers, tradespeople, and startups. They hire you because you cost less than a full time salary and only need a few hours of help each month. In return, they expect accurate work, clear reports, and plain answers.</p>
<p>This is different from a salaried accounting job. If you want a remote job with one employer, a fixed salary, and set hours, read our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-jobs/how-to-work-as-a-remote-accountant" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to work as a remote accountant</a> for that route. This guide is only about running your own freelance practice.</p>
<h2 id="step-1-decide-which-services-you-will-sell">Step 1: Decide which services you will sell</h2>
<p>New freelance accountants often fail by offering everything to everyone. A clear offer is easier to sell and easier to deliver well. Start with the services you can already do to a good standard, then add more as you learn.</p>

<p>Pick one service as your main offer and one type of client as your main market. For example, monthly bookkeeping for online sellers, or tax preparation for tradespeople. A narrow offer helps you learn the tools and rules for that market fast, and it makes your marketing much clearer.</p>
<p>Check demand before you commit. Look at freelance marketplaces and job boards for the service you plan to offer. If you see many open jobs for it, that is a good sign. Also think about what you enjoy. Bookkeeping is steady and repetitive. Advisory work pays more per hour but needs confidence. Tax work is seasonal and deadline heavy.</p>
<h2 id="step-2-check-the-qualifications-and-rules-in-your-country">Step 2: Check the qualifications and rules in your country</h2>
<p>What you can offer, and what you can call yourself, depends on your qualifications and on the rules in your country. Check this before you market any service.</p>
<p>In the United States, you do not need a licence to offer bookkeeping or most advisory services. You do need a Preparer Tax Identification Number, known as a PTIN, if you prepare federal tax returns for pay. <a href="https://www.irs.gov/tax-professionals/ptin-requirements-for-tax-return-preparers" rel="noopener" target="_blank">The IRS page on PTIN requirements</a> states that anyone who prepares federal tax returns for compensation must have a valid PTIN, and the fee is $18.75 as of November 2026. A Certified Public Accountant, known as a CPA, is licensed by a state board of accountancy, and only a CPA working through a licensed firm can do audits and other attest work.</p>
<p>In the United Kingdom, accountants often hold membership of a professional body such as ACCA or ICAEW. In other countries, the titles and licences differ again. The safe rule is simple: find your local professional body or tax authority, read its rules for independent accountants, and follow them.</p>
<p>Qualifications also affect your rates. Clients pay more for a qualified accountant than for a beginner, because a qualification signals training and accountability. See our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/digital-skills/how-to-learn-bookkeeping-online" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to learn bookkeeping online</a> for free and low cost courses.</p>
<p>You also need to know what you cannot do. You cannot sign an audit opinion without the right licence.</p>
<h2 id="step-3-set-up-your-freelance-practice">Step 3: Set up your freelance practice</h2>
<p>Set up the business side before you take your first client.</p>
<p>First, choose a business structure. Many freelancers start as a sole trader, which is the simplest form, because there is little paperwork and the business income is your income. In the United States this is called a sole proprietorship.</p>
<p>Next, register the business if your country requires it, and open a separate bank account for business money. Never mix client money with your personal money.</p>
<p>Then buy professional liability insurance, also called errors and omissions insurance. It covers you if a client claims your work caused them a loss, for example a missed filing deadline that led to a fine.</p>
<p>Write a standard engagement letter before you start work for any client. An engagement letter is a short written agreement that says what you will do, what you will not do, what the client must provide, what you charge, and when payment is due.</p>
<p>Finally, keep a simple system for your own records: a list of clients, deadlines, invoices, and payments. Set aside money for your own tax bill each month, because no employer will do it for you.</p>
<h2 id="step-4-choose-your-tools">Step 4: Choose your tools</h2>
<p>Your tools decide how fast you work and how professional you look. You do not need many tools, but you do need the right ones, and you need to know them well.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/freelance-accountant-tools.jpg" alt="A laptop showing a cloud accounting dashboard with charts and invoices, beside a tablet, notebook and pen on a tidy desk" /></p>
<p>Start with cloud accounting software. Most small business clients use QuickBooks Online or Xero, so learn at least one of them well. <a href="https://quickbooks.intuit.com/accountants/proadvisor/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">The QuickBooks ProAdvisor programme</a> offers training, certification, and badges that show clients you know the software. Xero has a similar partner programme and <a href="https://www.xero.com/us/advisors/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">the Xero advisor directory</a> lists accountants and bookkeepers that clients can find.</p>
<p>You also need a way to share documents safely. Use a client portal or a secure file sharing tool, not plain email, for tax returns, bank statements, and identity documents.</p>
<p>Add a practice management tool when you have more than a few clients. This can be simple at first, such as a shared calendar, a task list, and a folder per client.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-set-your-prices-and-packages">Step 5: Set your prices and packages</h2>
<p>Pricing is where many new freelance accountants go wrong. They charge too little to win work, then find they cannot live on the income. Set your prices from your costs and your time, not from what feels cheap.</p>

<h3 id="worked-example-pricing-a-first-month">Worked example: pricing a first month</h3>
<p>Example only: these numbers are made up to show the maths.</p>
<p>Imagine you have 5 clients, and each one pays a fixed monthly fee of $400 for bookkeeping and monthly reports. Your gross income is 5 times $400, which is $2,000 per month. Now subtract your monthly business costs. Say your accounting software costs $80, your insurance costs $50, and other costs such as phone, internet, and file storage cost $70. That is $80 plus $50 plus $70, which is $200 in costs. So $2,000 minus $200 leaves $1,800 before tax.</p>
<p>Raise your prices as you gain proof. After your first year, you will know how long each service takes and which clients are worth keeping.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-find-your-first-clients">Step 6: Find your first clients</h2>
<p>Finding clients is the hardest part of freelance accounting, and it is the part most guides rush past. Your first clients will almost always come from people who already know your work.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/freelance-accounting-clients.jpg" alt="A freelance accountant on a video call with a small business client, both smiling, with papers and a coffee cup on the desk" /></p>
<p>Start with your own network. Tell former colleagues, former employers, friends, and local business owners that you are taking on accounting clients.</p>
<p>Next, build a simple online presence. A one page website that says what you do, who you help, what you charge, and how to contact you is enough.</p>
<p>Freelance marketplaces can help you find early work, but treat them as a starting point, not a long term plan. Platforms such as Upwork list accounting jobs from clients around the world, and a good profile with a clear service list can win small jobs. The fees on these platforms take a share of your income, and competition is strong, so use them to build proof and reviews, then move clients to direct work where you keep the full fee.</p>
<p>Referral partners are a strong source of steady work. Lawyers, financial advisers, business coaches, and other accountants often meet clients who need bookkeeping or tax help.</p>
<h2 id="step-7-deliver-the-work-and-keep-clients">Step 7: Deliver the work and keep clients</h2>
<p>Winning a client is only half the job.</p>
<p>Start every client with a clear onboarding. Ask for the documents you need, agree on how you will communicate, and set the deadlines for the month. If their records are messy, say so at the start and agree a clean up fee, rather than finding the mess later and working for free.</p>
<p>Deliver on time, every time. Tax and payroll deadlines do not move, so build your calendar around them.</p>
<p>Ask for feedback after the first month. A short message asking what worked and what could be better shows you care, and it catches small problems before they become reasons to leave.</p>

<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Offering every service to every client.</strong> A wide offer is hard to sell and hard to deliver well, so pick one main service and one type of client first.</li>
<li><strong>Charging too little to win work.</strong> Low prices attract clients who leave for the next cheaper offer, and they leave you with no margin for tax, insurance, or slow months.</li>
<li><strong>Working without an engagement letter.</strong> Without a written agreement, the scope can grow, payment can be late, and a dispute has no clear answer.</li>
<li><strong>Mixing business money with personal money.</strong> One bank account for everything makes your tax return hard and looks unprofessional to clients and banks.</li>
<li><strong>Forgetting your own tax bill.</strong> No employer sets tax aside for you, so put money aside from every payment as soon as it arrives.</li>
<li><strong>Taking on clients you cannot serve.</strong> If a client needs audit work or advice outside your qualifications, say so and refer them on, because a mistake there costs far more than the fee.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring data security.</strong> Client tax returns and bank details are sensitive, so use secure file sharing, two factor authentication, and a written security plan where your country requires one.</li>
<li><strong>Never raising prices.</strong> Costs rise every year, and your skill grows too, so review your fees at least once a year and raise them for new clients first.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="risks-and-scams-to-watch-for">Risks and scams to watch for</h2>
<p>Freelance accountants face real risks, and some of them come from the clients themselves.</p>
<p>Be careful with clients who ask you to move money, hide income, or file a return you know is wrong. That is not a normal client request. It is a request to break the law, and it can cost you your licence and your reputation. Refuse it in writing and end the relationship.</p>
<p>Scammers also target accountants. A common trick is a fake client who sends a large cheque or payment, then asks you to send part of it back or on to someone else.</p>
<p>Protect client data as if it were your own money. Use strong passwords, two factor authentication, and secure file sharing.</p>
<p>Late payment is a common risk too. Agree payment terms in the engagement letter, send invoices on time, and stop work if a client falls far behind.</p>
<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 id="do-i-need-to-be-a-cpa-to-work-as-a-freelance-accountant">Do I need to be a CPA to work as a freelance accountant?</h3>
<p>No, you do not need to be a CPA to work as a freelance accountant in most cases. Bookkeeping, payroll, and most advisory services do not need a licence, and you can offer them with the right skills and experience.</p>
<h3 id="how-much-can-a-freelance-accountant-earn">How much can a freelance accountant earn?</h3>
<p>There is no fixed figure, because earnings depend on your experience, your location, the services you offer, the number of clients you keep, and the fees you charge. A freelance accountant with a few monthly retainer clients can earn a steady income, but the first months are often slow and uneven.</p>
<h3 id="how-do-freelance-accountants-find-clients">How do freelance accountants find clients?</h3>
<p>Most freelance accountants find their first clients through people who already know their work, such as former colleagues, employers, and local business owners. Referral partners, professional directories, a clear LinkedIn profile, and a simple website also help.</p>
<h3 id="what-software-do-freelance-accountants-use">What software do freelance accountants use?</h3>
<p>Most freelance accountants use cloud accounting software such as QuickBooks Online or Xero, because that is what their clients already use. They also use a secure client portal or file sharing tool for documents, an invoicing and payment tool, and a simple task list or practice management tool for deadlines.</p>
<h3 id="can-i-work-as-a-freelance-accountant-without-experience">Can I work as a freelance accountant without experience?</h3>
<p>It is hard to start with no experience, because clients trust accountants with their money and their tax filings. You can build experience first by working in an accounting job, doing bookkeeping for a small business, or taking a recognised bookkeeping course.</p>
<h3 id="is-freelance-accounting-the-same-as-a-remote-accountant-job">Is freelance accounting the same as a remote accountant job?</h3>
<p>No, they are different. A freelance accountant runs their own practice, finds their own clients, sets their own prices, and pays their own tax and insurance. If you want the employee route, read our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-jobs/how-to-work-as-a-remote-accountant" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to work as a remote accountant</a>.</p>

<p>A freelance accountant builds a practice one client at a time, starting with one service, one type of client, and a clear price. Check the rules in your country first, set up the business properly, choose a small set of tools, and find your first clients through people who already know your work.</p>
