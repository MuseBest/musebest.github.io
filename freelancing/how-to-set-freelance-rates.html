How to Set Freelance Rates: A Practical Guide

A practical guide to setting freelance rates: calculate your minimum rate from real costs, check market rates, choose a pricing model, and raise your rates.

Setting freelance rates is the part most beginners get wrong, usually by guessing. The most common mistake is copying your old hourly wage, or charging whatever the client suggests. Both leave you underpaid, because a freelance rate has to cover things an employer used to pay for: taxes, insurance, retirement, holidays, and all the hours you cannot bill. This guide shows you how to calculate a rate that pays your bills, check it against the market, choose a pricing model, and raise it with confidence.

Why your old salary is the wrong starting point

An employee earning $50,000 a year costs their employer much more than $50,000. The employer also pays payroll taxes, health insurance, retirement contributions, paid holidays, equipment, and office costs. As a freelancer, you pay all of that yourself, out of your rate.

That is why freelance rates need to be much higher than the equivalent hourly wage. If your old job paid $25 an hour, a freelance rate of $25 an hour means a pay cut, not a match. The exact multiple depends on your costs and your country, but a rate roughly two to three times the equivalent employee wage is common. The formula below shows you how to work out your own number instead of guessing.

Step 1: Add up your real costs

Start with what you need to live on, then add the costs of running your business. Use your own currency and your own numbers. The figures in the table are examples in US dollars to show the method.

Cost What to include Example (annual)
Target take-home pay What you want to earn after tax $60,000
Income tax and self-employment tax Set aside a share of every payment; rates depend on your country $12,000
Health insurance Premiums for you, and family if relevant $6,000
Retirement savings What you would have saved as an employee, plus any employer match you lose $6,000
Business expenses Software, equipment, internet, phone, accounting, marketing $4,000
Time off Holidays and sick days you now pay for yourself $4,000
Total revenue needed $92,000

If you are outside the United States, replace the tax line with your own country's rules. In the US, self-employed workers pay a self-employment tax of about 15.3 percent on most net earnings, on top of income tax. In other countries the structure is different, so check your local tax authority or an accountant before you set your rate.

Step 2: Work out your billable hours

You will not bill 40 hours a week. Between finding clients, writing proposals, invoicing, admin, and learning, most freelancers bill 20 to 30 hours a week. Plan for it honestly.

A realistic estimate:

  • 52 weeks minus 4 weeks of holiday and sick days: 48 working weeks.
  • 25 billable hours a week (the rest is admin, sales, and learning).
  • 48 times 25: 1,200 billable hours a year.

If you assume 40 billable hours a week, your rate will look comfortable on paper and leave you short in real life. Track your hours for a month before you commit to a number; it is the fastest way to learn your real billable ratio.

Step 3: Calculate your minimum rate

Divide the revenue you need by your billable hours:

$92,000 divided by 1,200 hours = about $77 an hour

That is your floor: the lowest rate that meets your goals. It is not your target. Your target should sit above it, because projects run over, clients pay late, and some months are slow. Many freelancers add a margin of 20 to 30 percent above their floor as a buffer, and treat the floor as the number they never go below.

Try the same calculation with your own numbers before you quote your next project. It takes ten minutes and it changes how you negotiate.

Step 4: Check market rates

Your minimum rate tells you what you need. Market research tells you what clients will pay. If your floor is far above the market, you need to cut costs, bill more hours, specialise in a higher-paying niche, or move to project pricing. If your floor is far below the market, you are leaving money on the table.

Where to find real data:

  • Salary data for your skill. Search job sites for full-time roles in your field. Divide the annual salary by about 2,000 hours for an hourly baseline, then add 30 to 50 percent, because freelancers do not get benefits. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook lists median pay by occupation and is a useful anchor.
  • Rate surveys in your field. Professional associations and industry publications often publish annual rate surveys.
  • Other freelancers. Ask peers in communities and forums what they charge for similar work. People share ranges more often than you expect.
  • Your own results. Track what you win and lose. If you win every proposal, your rates are probably too low.

Step 5: Choose a pricing model

Model How it works Best for Watch out for
Hourly You bill tracked time Unclear scope, ongoing support Punishes efficiency; clients watch the clock
Project (fixed fee) One price for defined deliverables Clear scope: websites, logos, articles Scope creep without a good contract
Day rate Fixed price per day On-site work, workshops Define what a day includes
Retainer Monthly fee for ongoing work Long-term clients Define hours or deliverables included
Value-based Price based on the outcome's worth to the client Experienced freelancers with measurable impact Requires understanding the client's business

For your first projects, hourly or project pricing is simplest. Project pricing rewards efficiency and is easier for clients to budget, as long as the scope is written down. A useful hybrid for beginners is a fixed project price with an hourly rate for extra work: "This project is $1,500 for the defined scope. Additional work beyond the scope is billed at $80 an hour."

How to quote a project price

Quoting by project is a skill. Follow these steps:

  1. Break the project into tasks and estimate hours for each.
  2. Multiply by your hourly rate.
  3. Add a buffer of 15 to 25 percent for communication, revisions, and surprises.
  4. Round to a clean number and present it as a fixed fee with a clear scope.
Task Hours At $77 an hour
Discovery call and brief 2 $154
Draft 5 website pages 12 $924
Two revision rounds 4 $308
Project management and email 2 $154
Subtotal 20 $1,540
Buffer (20 percent) $308
Quoted project fee $1,850

Put the scope and the number of revision rounds in your contract, so extra work is billed separately instead of given away.

What to do when a client says your rate is too high

This is not always a rejection. It is often the start of a negotiation. Do not discount your rate on the spot. Instead:

  • Ask what range they had in mind. That tells you whether the gap is small or impossible.
  • Reduce the scope instead of the price. "I can do the first three pages within that budget. The full five pages would be $1,850."
  • Explain what is included: revisions, research, communication, and delivery. Clients compare prices, but they buy the whole package.
  • Say your rate once, then stop talking. Silence is not rudeness; it gives the client room to answer.

If nobody ever pushes back on your rates, you are probably undercharging. A healthy close rate is roughly one in three proposals, not ten in ten.

When and how to raise your rates

Review your rates at least once a year, and raise them when you are fully booked, when you add a skill, or when your costs rise. Apply the new rate to new clients first. For existing clients, give 30 to 60 days of notice and keep the message short:

"Starting 1 March, my rate for new projects will be $85 an hour. Anything we agree before then stays at the current rate. Thank you for working with me, and I look forward to our next project."

You can also build a simple rate card: a one-page list of your standard prices for common services. It speeds up quoting, keeps you consistent, and helps clients understand what they are buying. Review it every six months. If you are consistently booked, raise the prices on the card first.

Common pricing mistakes

  • Using your old hourly wage as your freelance rate.
  • Assuming 40 billable hours a week.
  • Forgetting taxes, insurance, and retirement.
  • Discounting heavily to win a first client and getting stuck at that rate.
  • Not charging for revisions, rush work, or scope changes.
  • Quoting on the spot instead of taking a day to scope the work properly.
  • Charging the same rate for every client, regardless of urgency or complexity.
  • Never revisiting the numbers as costs and skills change.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my freelance hourly rate?

Add your target income, business expenses, insurance, retirement savings, and taxes, then divide by your realistic billable hours per year. Compare the result with market rates and adjust.

How many billable hours do freelancers work?

Most bill 1,000 to 1,300 hours a year, or roughly 20 to 30 hours a week across 46 to 48 working weeks. The rest goes to admin, marketing, and learning.

Should freelancers charge hourly or per project?

Hourly works for unclear or ongoing work. Project pricing rewards efficiency and is easier for clients to budget, as long as the scope is clearly defined in a contract. Many beginners start hourly and move to project pricing as they gain experience.

How much higher should a freelance rate be than a salary?

Often around two to three times the equivalent hourly wage, because you cover taxes, benefits, expenses, and unbillable time. The exact multiple depends on your costs and billable hours, so run the formula with your own numbers.

How often should I raise my freelance rates?

Review them at least once a year. Raise rates for new clients first and give existing clients notice before changing theirs. If you are fully booked, that is the clearest sign your rates are too low.

Conclusion

A freelance rate is a calculation, not a guess. Add up your real costs, divide by the hours you can actually bill, check the market, and treat the result as your floor. Then choose a pricing model that fits the work, quote projects with a buffer, and raise your rates when demand proves you can. Run the numbers once, write them down, and quote with confidence.
