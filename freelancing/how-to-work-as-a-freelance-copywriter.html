How to Work as a Freelance Copywriter in 2026

Learning how to work as a freelance copywriter starts with a clear idea. A copywriter writes words that persuade a reader to act, such as buying or signing up for a list. This guide shows you the skills you need, how to build samples with no clients yet, how to set a rate, and how to find your first paying work.

How to work as a freelance copywriter: what the job really involves

A freelance copywriter sells words that help a business make money: website pages, sales pages, landing pages, email sequences, adverts, and product descriptions. A client hires you when their own words are not bringing in sales or signups, and your job is to fix that.

Copywriters usually charge by the project, not by the word. A landing page is one project with one price. Project pricing rewards you for writing well and working fast, while per word pricing rewards long copy even when short copy sells better.

Copywriting versus content writing

Copywriting and content writing look alike, but clients buy them for different reasons. Copywriting aims to persuade the reader to take one clear action now. Content writing aims to inform, entertain, or build trust over time. A blog post that explains a topic is content writing. The sales page at the end of that blog post is copywriting.

The two skills overlap, and many freelancers offer both. But your portfolio, your pitch, and your prices should make it clear which one you sell. To compare the two paths, read our guide on how to become a freelance writer.

Feature Copywriting Content writing
Main goal Persuade the reader to act now Inform, entertain, or build trust
Typical work Sales pages, landing pages, emails, adverts Blog posts, guides, newsletters, articles
How success is measured Sales, signups, clicks, enquiries Reads, shares, search rankings, time on page
Usual pricing By the project, tied to value By the word, by the article, or by the project
Research focus Customer pains, objections, and buying reasons Topic depth, search intent, and accuracy

The skills you actually need

You do not need a degree to do this work. You need five working skills, and you can build all of them with practice.

First, reader research. Good copy starts with the customer, not the product. Read reviews, support tickets, forum posts, and survey answers to learn what buyers worry about and what words they use. When your copy uses the customer's own words, it feels honest and it converts better.

Second, clear headlines. A headline must say what the reader gets and why it matters, in one short line. Cut clever wordplay and say the benefit plainly. If the headline is unclear, most readers never reach the first paragraph.

Third, benefits over features. A feature is what a product has. A benefit is what it does for the buyer. A laptop with a 14 hour battery is a feature. Working a full day without a charger is a benefit. Clients pay for benefits, so lead with them.

Fourth, simple editing. Cut every word that does not earn its place. Short sentences beat long ones. One idea per sentence keeps the reader moving. Read your draft out loud, and rewrite any sentence that sounds stiff or confusing.

Fifth, basic marketing sense. Learn the classic formulas that structure persuasive copy. AIDA stands for attention, interest, desire, and action. PAS stands for problem, agitation, and solution. FAB stands for features, advantages, and benefits. Using them makes your drafts stronger.

A word processor, a shared document tool for client reviews, and a simple invoicing tool cover most of the work. Our guide on how to learn copywriting sets out the main skills and learning routes.

Step 1: Pick a niche by industry and by format

Beginners who try to serve everyone end up competing with everyone. A niche makes your samples stronger, your pitches sharper, and your rates easier to defend. Pick a niche in two parts: one industry and one format.

For the industry, choose a field you know or enjoy learning about. Fitness, finance, software, travel, beauty, and home services all hire copywriters regularly.

For the format, choose the type of copy you enjoy writing most, and build your first samples around it. You can add a second format later, once the first one brings in work.

Step 2: Build three to five honest spec samples

Clients hire proof, not promises. Without past clients, your proof is spec work: samples you write for real businesses without being paid. Spec work is normal for beginners, but it must be labelled honestly.

Write three to five samples in your chosen niche and format. Pick a real business, study its website and reviews, and rewrite one page or write one email sequence for it. Say clearly on the sample that it is a practice piece, not paid client work.

Each sample should show one clear skill: a strong headline, an email that handles a common objection, or a product description that turns features into benefits. Add one line that explains what the piece was meant to do and who it was written for.

Put the samples on a simple page, and rewrite each one until it reads well out loud. Three strong samples beat ten weak ones. Read our guide on how to create a freelance portfolio for the full method.

Step 3: Set up a simple online presence

You need three things: a one page website or portfolio page with your name, your niche, your samples, and contact details; a short bio that says who you help and what you write; and one social profile where your clients spend time, usually LinkedIn for business clients.

Keep the site simple. A visitor should understand what you do within five seconds. Your headline should name your service and your niche, and your samples should be one click away.

Step 4: Work out your rate

New copywriters usually make one of two errors. They charge so little that the work cannot pay their bills, or they copy a top earner's rate with no proof to back it up. The floor rate method avoids both errors, because it starts from your own costs.

Work out a monthly income target first. Then count your billable hours, which are the hours you can actually charge for in a month. Beginners often manage 40 to 60 billable hours a month. Divide the target by the billable hours, and the result is your floor rate.

Once you know your floor, quote by the project, because a short page that brings in sales is worth more than the hours it took to write. The table below shows project price ranges reported in 2026 guides. Treat them as rough guides, not fixed rates.

Copy type Typical project range reported in 2026 guides
Blog post 75 to 400 US dollars per post
Email sequence 500 to 2,500 US dollars
Landing page 500 to 5,000 US dollars
Website copy 1,000 to 5,000 US dollars
Sales page 2,000 to 25,000 US dollars
Monthly retainer 1,500 to 10,000 US dollars per month

Your first projects will sit at the low end of these ranges. Raise your rate each time your calendar fills up. For the full pricing method, read our guide on how to set freelance rates.

Step 5: Find your first clients

Your first clients rarely come from job boards alone. Use four channels together, and keep at it every week.

Start with people you already know. Tell friends, past colleagues, and local business owners what you do now. A small business with a weak website is a good first client, because the owner can see the problem.

Next, try direct outreach. Find a business in your niche whose copy is weak, study its customers, and send a short email that names one specific problem and offers one specific fix. Attach one relevant sample, keep the email under 150 words, and follow up once after a week.

Then use freelance platforms. Upwork and similar sites list copywriting jobs every day, and a strong profile with samples can win small jobs that lead to repeat work. Check the fee before you bid, because it comes out of your pay. Upwork's freelancer service fee ranges from 0 to 15 percent per contract, as of November 2026.

LinkedIn also helps: one useful post a week beats ten adverts for your services. Our guide on how to find freelance clients covers these channels in more detail.

Step 6: Send a clear proposal and run a discovery call

A good proposal is short and specific. It names the work, the price, the number of revision rounds, the deadline, and the payment terms. It also says what is not included, so the client cannot add extra pages for free later.

Before you write the proposal, run a discovery call. This is a short call, usually 20 to 30 minutes, where you learn about the client's business before you quote.

  • What is the page or email meant to do, and how will you measure success?
  • Who is the reader, and what worries them most before they buy?
  • What has the client tried before, and what happened?
  • What must the copy include, and what must it never say?
  • What is the deadline, and what happens if it slips?

End the call by repeating back what you heard, and by naming the next step and the date you will send the proposal. Clients trust writers who listen and confirm, because it shows the copy will match the brief. For a full proposal template, read our guide on how to write freelance proposals.

Step 7: Run a smooth client process

A smooth process has five parts. First, agree the scope in writing, including pages, revision rounds, and deadline. Second, ask for a deposit, usually 50 percent upfront. Third, send the draft on the date you promised. Fourth, collect feedback in one round and revise within the agreed limits. Fifth, send the invoice with the final files, and ask for a short testimonial while the client is happy.

Keep every agreement in writing, even with friendly clients. Read our guide on freelance contracts explained before your first project.

A worked example: pricing your first landing page

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. They show the maths, so you can repeat it with your own figures.

Imagine your monthly income target is 1,000 US dollars after costs, and you can bill 50 hours a month. Your floor rate is 1,000 divided by 50, which is 20 US dollars per hour.

Now imagine a client wants one landing page. You estimate 10 hours of paid work: research, a draft, and two revision rounds. Your floor price is 10 times 20, which is 200 US dollars. You quote 400 US dollars for the project, because the page should bring the client new sales and the reported range for landing pages starts at 500 US dollars for experienced writers, so 400 is a fair beginner price.

The client accepts, and the work comes through Upwork with a 10 percent fee. The fee is 10 percent of 400, which is 40 US dollars, so you receive 360 US dollars. You set aside 20 percent for tax, which is 72 US dollars, so you keep 288 US dollars before your own costs.

You also spent 3 hours of unpaid time on the proposal, the discovery call, and invoicing. Your total time was 13 hours. Your effective rate was 288 divided by 13, which is about 22 US dollars per hour. That is above your 20 US dollar floor, so the project worked.

If you had quoted at your floor price of 200 US dollars instead, the fee would be 20, you would receive 180, tax of 36 would leave 144, and 144 divided by 13 hours is about 11 US dollars per hour. Quoting by value, not by your floor, matters.

Recheck the maths: 400 minus 40 is 360, and 20 percent of 360 is 72, and 360 minus 72 is 288, and 288 divided by 13 is 22.15. The method is simple, but it only works if you count the unpaid hours and the fees honestly.

What it costs and what to expect

Copywriting has low startup costs. You need a computer, an internet connection, and a word processor. Most of the cost is your time.

Item Typical cost
Computer and internet You probably already have these
Portfolio page or website 0 to 100 US dollars a year
Invoicing tool 0 to 15 US dollars a month
Learning (books and short courses) 0 to 200 US dollars
Tax set aside 20 to 30 percent of income, depending on your country
Total to start 0 to 300 US dollars, plus your time

Expect a slow start. One 2026 guide suggests 3 to 6 months to land the first regular clients, and 12 to 18 months to replace a full time salary. That timeline is realistic, not a promise. Keep a part time income while you build, if you can.

Getting paid as an international freelancer needs a plan. PayPal is not available in every country, including Pakistan, so check what works where you live before you quote. Services such as Payoneer let freelancers receive payments in many countries, and you can read how it works on Payoneer's website. Set aside money for tax from the first payment, because tax rules differ by country.

Common mistakes

  • Writing about the product instead of the customer, so the copy lists features and never says why they matter.
  • Skipping research and guessing what the customer wants, which produces generic copy that could fit any business.
  • Quoting a price before you know the scope, then doing extra pages for free.
  • Presenting spec samples as real client work, which breaks trust the moment a client checks.
  • Waiting for clients to find you, instead of doing outreach every week.

Risks and scams to watch for

The main risks in freelance copywriting are unpaid work, scope creep, and clients who vanish after delivery. Reduce them with a deposit, a written scope, and staged payments for large projects. Never hand over final files before the final payment clears.

Watch for scams aimed at new writers. Be wary of any client who asks you to pay a fee to join, buy a starter kit, or pay for training before you can work. Be wary of clients who ask for a free trial project that is really a full piece of work, or who ask for your bank login or a verification code. Real clients pay you, not the other way round. If a job offer asks for money upfront, walk away.

AI tools are changing the low end of this work, because simple product descriptions are now easy for a client to generate. Your defence is the work AI does poorly: deep customer research, original angles, and copy tied to real results.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a freelance copywriter charge?

Rates vary widely with skill, niche, and market. Guides published in 2026 report project ranges from 75 to 400 US dollars for a blog post, 500 to 2,500 for an email sequence, and 500 to 5,000 for a landing page. Work out your floor rate from your monthly target and billable hours, then quote by the project.

How do I become a freelance copywriter with no experience?

Pick one niche and one format, write three to five spec samples for real businesses in that niche, put them on a simple portfolio page, then do direct outreach and apply for small jobs on freelance platforms.

What is the difference between copywriting and content writing?

Copywriting persuades the reader to take one clear action, such as buying or signing up. Content writing informs or entertains, and builds trust over time. Your portfolio should make it clear which service you are selling.

Conclusion

Learning how to work as a freelance copywriter comes down to a simple order of work. Pick one niche and one format, build three to five honest samples, set a floor rate from your own costs, and do outreach every week until the first client says yes.
