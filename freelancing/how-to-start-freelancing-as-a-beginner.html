How to Start Freelancing as a Beginner

Freelancing rewards clarity: one service, one audience, one way to get paid. This guide walks through the first month, step by step.

Maya ChenPublished 17 October 2026Updated 27 October 20263 min read

  • Where to find your first clients
  • How to price your first jobs

Start with one service, not ten

New freelancers often list every skill they have. Clients do the opposite: they search for one specific problem solved well. Pick the service you can already deliver to a decent standard — writing, design, video editing, data entry, virtual assistance, translation — and make that your offer.

Write it down in one sentence: "I help [who] do [what] so they get [result]." If you cannot fill in the blanks, you are not ready to pitch yet.

Set up the basics before you look for clients

You need four things before your first pitch:

  • A way to be paid. A bank account or payment service that works in your country, and a simple invoice template.
  • A portfolio, even a small one. Three sample pieces beat an empty profile. Create them for fictional clients if you have no real ones yet, and label them as samples.
  • A rate. Research what others charge for similar work in your market, then price by project or by hour — and write the rate down before a client asks.
  • A contract habit. Even a short email confirming scope, deadline, revisions, and payment terms prevents most disputes.

Where to find your first clients

There are three realistic routes, and most beginners should try all three:

Route How it works Watch out for
Freelance marketplaces Clients post jobs; you bid High competition, platform fees
Direct outreach You email businesses that need your service Low reply rates at first
Your own network Former colleagues, local businesses, friends Small at first, but converts best

On marketplaces, your first reviews matter more than your first paycheque. A small, well-delivered job that earns a good review is worth more than a larger job that ends badly.

How to price your first jobs

Start by estimating the hours a job will take, multiply by a modest hourly rate, and add a margin for revisions and communication. If a client wants a fixed price, agree the scope in writing first — "one logo, two revision rounds, delivered in five days" — so the price has a boundary.

Raise your rate after every three to five successful jobs, or when you are turning work away. That is the market telling you that you are underpriced.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Saying yes to everything. A bad-fit client costs more time than the fee is worth.
  • Working without a deposit. For new clients, ask for part of the fee upfront.
  • No boundaries. Agree working hours and revision limits in advance.
  • Hiding your work. Share what you make. Clients hire people they have seen do the work.

Your first month, in order

  1. Choose one service and write your one-sentence offer.
  2. Make three portfolio samples.
  3. Create profiles on one or two marketplaces and a simple personal page.
  4. Send five tailored pitches a day for two weeks.
  5. Deliver the first job well, ask for a review, and ask for a referral.

Freelancing is a slow build, not a lottery ticket. The first client is the hardest; the system that found them is what you repeat.
