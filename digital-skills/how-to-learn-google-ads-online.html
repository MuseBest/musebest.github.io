What is Google Ads, in plain words?

Google Ads is Google's paid advertising platform. You choose the search terms you want to appear for, you write an ad, and you pay only when someone clicks on it. This is called pay per click, or PPC. It is different from SEO, which earns free clicks over months. With Google Ads, you can appear at the top of the search results on the same day you launch a campaign.

The platform covers more than search. The main campaign types are Search, Display, Shopping, Video (YouTube), and Performance Max, which mixes several formats in one campaign. Beginners should start with Search, because it is the simplest to measure.

Google Ads is a skill employers and clients pay for. Agencies hire PPC specialists, small businesses hire freelancers to run their accounts, and marketers add it to a broader role. It is also a skill you can use for your own business. If you sell digital products, our guide on how to sell digital products with Google Ads shows the product side. This guide is about learning the skill itself.

Step 1: Learn the basics free with Skillshop

Google Skillshop is Google's own free training platform, and it is where you should start. The Google Ads courses cover Search, Display, Shopping, Video and Measurement, each broken into short modules you work through at your own pace. The material is accurate and kept current with platform changes, which matters because Google Ads changes often.

Work through the Search course first, then Measurement. Do not skip the assessments. Each certification ends in an exam of about 50 to 90 minutes, and you can retake it after 24 hours if you do not pass. The certificates are free, and they are the credentials Google itself endorses, so they carry weight on a CV or a freelance profile.

Skillshop teaches you how Google wants the platform used. It teaches the mechanics well, but not campaign strategy, creative judgement, or client management. Treat it as the foundation, then add depth from a hands-on course and a real campaign. The Google Ads Help Centre answers setting questions, and the official Google Ads blog is where Google announces changes.

Step 2: Learn account structure, keywords and match types

Every optimisation decision flows from clean account structure. The structure has four levels: account, campaign, ad group, and then ads and keywords inside each ad group. A campaign holds a budget and a goal. An ad group holds a tight set of closely related keywords and the ads that match them. Keep ad groups small and themed, so each ad matches the search terms inside it.

Keywords are the search terms you bid on. Learn the three match types early:

Match type What it does Example
Broad match Shows for related searches, including synonyms and related topics The keyword running shoes may show for jogging trainers
Phrase match Shows for searches that include the meaning of your phrase The phrase "running shoes" may show for best running shoes for women
Exact match Shows for searches with the same meaning as your keyword The keyword [running shoes] shows for running shoes and close variants

Negative keywords are just as important. These are terms you tell Google never to show your ads for, such as "free" when you sell a paid product. Check the search terms report every week to find wasted clicks and add them as negatives. This single habit saves more money than any other beginner skill.

Keyword research is the work of finding the terms your customers actually type. Google's free Keyword Planner, inside a Google Ads account, shows search terms and rough volume ranges. Use it to build your first keyword lists, grouped by intent: people looking to buy, people comparing, and people just learning.

Step 3: Learn bidding, budgets and Quality Score

You do not set the price of a click directly. You set a bidding strategy, and Google's auction decides the price each time. Learn these four strategies:

  • Manual CPC. You set the maximum you will pay per click. Best when you are learning, because you control the spend.
  • Maximise clicks. Google spends your budget to get as many clicks as possible. Simple, but it can buy low-quality clicks.
  • Target CPA. You tell Google what a new customer or lead is worth to you, and it bids to hit that average cost per action. Needs conversion data before it works well.
  • Target ROAS. You tell Google the return you want on your ad spend. This is for accounts with solid sales data, not beginners.

Quality Score is Google's rating of your keyword, ad and landing page, on a scale of 1 to 10. It is based on expected click-through rate, ad relevance, and landing page experience. A higher score usually means a lower cost per click and a better ad position. Improve it by writing ads that match the keyword, and by sending clicks to a page that matches the ad. Never send paid traffic to a generic homepage.

Set a daily budget you can afford to lose while you learn. Start small, watch the numbers for a week, and only raise the budget when you understand what each click is doing.

Step 4: Learn conversion tracking

Without tracking, every optimisation is a guess. Conversion tracking tells you which clicks turned into something useful: a sale, a sign-up, a phone call, or a form fill. Set it up before you spend anything serious.

The basic setup has three parts. First, define a conversion action in Google Ads, such as a purchase or a lead form submission. Second, install the Google tag on your website. Third, check that the tag fires by testing it yourself. Google Tag Manager can help if you do not want to edit website code directly.

Learn Google Analytics 4 alongside this, because it shows what visitors do after they click. The two tools together tell you the full story: which keyword brought the click, and whether that visitor did anything useful. Skillshop has a free Measurement course that covers both.

Step 5: Run a small real campaign

Training without a real account does not stick. Even a small amount of real spend teaches you more than hours of video, because real money makes you read the numbers properly.

Set up a practice campaign like this:

  1. Pick one simple offer. Your own blog, a friend's small business, or a test landing page. Do not practise on a client's money.
  2. Choose one campaign type. Search only, with five to ten keywords in one ad group.
  3. Write three ads. Same keywords, different headlines, so you can compare them.
  4. Set a small daily budget. An amount you can afford to lose for two weeks, and no more.
  5. Add conversion tracking first. Do this before the campaign goes live, not after.
  6. Check it daily for the first week. Read the search terms report, add negative keywords, and pause anything that spends without results.
  7. Write down what you learned. One short note per day: what changed, what you did, and what happened next.

Do not expect the practice campaign to make money. Its job is to teach you how the platform behaves. Treat the spend as tuition, and keep it small enough that a bad week does not hurt you.

Step 6: Get certified and build proof

Certification and proof work together. A certificate opens doors, and proof closes the deal.

  • Pass the Google Ads Search certification on Skillshop. This is free, and it is the one employers and clients ask about most.
  • Add the Measurement certification. It shows you can track results, which is what clients actually pay for.
  • Document your practice campaign. Write a one-page case study: the goal, the budget, the structure, what you changed, and what the numbers showed. Use real numbers from your own test, and label it clearly as a practice account.
  • Offer one small free or low-cost job. A local business or a friend's shop is a good first client. One real account with real results is worth more than five certificates.

The most common path that works is Skillshop first, then one hands-on Udemy course, then a real practice campaign. That combination gives you the credential, the practical skill, and the proof.

This plan assumes five to seven hours a week. Adjust the hours to your life.

1 to 2 Skillshop Search course, account structure, keyword basics Search certification in progress, one keyword list built
3 to 4 Match types, negative keywords, Quality Score, ad writing Search certification passed, three practice ads written
5 to 6 Bidding strategies, budgets, conversion tracking setup A test landing page with tracking installed

11 to 12 Optimise the campaign, write a one-page case study, plan the next step A certificate, a case study, and a clear next goal

Worked example: a realistic first 90 days

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. They show the time and the maths, not what you will earn.

Ahmed is a complete beginner with six hours a week. He follows the plan above.

  • Weeks 1 to 4: 24 hours. He finishes the Skillshop Search course, passes the exam on the second try, and builds a keyword list of 40 terms for a friend's bakery.
  • Weeks 5 to 8: 24 hours. He finishes the Measurement course, sets up conversion tracking on a simple landing page, and writes three ads.
  • Weeks 9 to 12: 24 hours of study, plus a practice campaign. He sets a daily budget of 5 US dollars, so four weeks of ads cost about 140 US dollars. The campaign brings 60 clicks and 4 form fills. The cost per lead is about 35 US dollars, which is high, but the campaign was for learning, not profit.
  • Total learning time: 72 hours. Total money spent: about 140 US dollars on ads, and 0 on courses.

At the end he has two free certificates, one documented practice campaign, and a clear sense of what he still needs to learn. He has not earned anything yet. That is normal. The next step is one small real client. For the client side, read how to find freelance clients.

Expect the basics to take two to four weeks of steady study, and you can pass the Search certification in that time. Getting confident with real accounts takes three to six months. Do not expect income in the first month. Google Ads pays when you manage real budgets well, and that trust is earned slowly. Common mistakes

  • Skipping conversion tracking. If you cannot see what a click did, you cannot improve anything. Set up tracking before you spend.
  • Using one big ad group. Twenty unrelated keywords in one ad group means ads that match nothing well. Keep ad groups small and themed.
  • Ignoring the search terms report. This report shows what people actually typed. Check it weekly and add wasted terms as negatives.
  • Sending traffic to a homepage. A homepage tries to do too many things at once. Send paid clicks to a page built for one action.
  • Raising the budget before the campaign works. More spend on a broken campaign just buys more bad clicks. Fix the targeting and the ads first.
  • Learning only from one source. Skillshop teaches the official way. A hands-on course teaches how practitioners actually work. You need both.

Risks and scams to watch for

  • Courses that promise guaranteed results. No course can promise a return on ad spend. Anyone who guarantees profit from Google Ads is selling a fantasy.
  • Fake "Google Ads experts" for hire. Check a freelancer's real account history before you pay them to manage your ads. Ask for a case study with real numbers, and be wary of screenshots that cannot be verified.
  • Account rental schemes. Some sellers offer "aged" Google Ads accounts or promise to run ads that break Google's policies. Using these can get your account suspended.
  • Policy violations and suspensions. Google suspends accounts that break its advertising policies, including misleading claims and restricted products. Read the policy pages in the Help Centre before you launch, and never promise results your product cannot deliver.
  • Spending without a limit. Always set a daily budget and a clear stop rule. Never let a campaign run unchecked while you are still learning, because a single mistake can spend your whole budget in a day.

Frequently asked questions

Is Google Ads hard to learn?

It feels like a lot at first, because the platform has many settings. The core ideas are simple: pick the right keywords, write a matching ad, send the click to a good page, and measure what happens. Most beginners can run a basic campaign after two to four weeks of study, and the Skillshop courses are built for complete beginners.

Can I learn Google Ads for free?

Yes. Google Skillshop is free, including the certification exams. The Google Ads Help Centre and the official blog are also free. You only pay when you choose a paid course or when you run real ads, and both are optional while you learn the basics.

How long does it take to learn Google Ads?

You can pass the Search certification in two to four weeks at five to seven hours a week. Getting confident with real accounts takes three to six months, because you need live campaign experience. Most practitioners keep learning for years, because Google changes the platform regularly.

Do I need a certification to get Google Ads clients?

No, but it helps. Clients ask for proof, and the free Skillshop Search certification is the most recognised proof for beginners. A short case study from a real or practice campaign matters more than the certificate alone, so build both.

What is the difference between Google Ads and SEO?

Google Ads buys clicks at the top of the search results, and you pay for each click. SEO earns free clicks over time by ranking in the organic results. Ads work fast and cost money per click. SEO is slower and costs time. Many businesses use both. If you want the organic side, read how to learn SEO for beginners.

Conclusion

Learning Google Ads online comes down to a clear order: start free with Skillshop, learn structure and keywords, understand bidding and Quality Score, set up tracking, run a small real campaign, then get certified and document your work. Do not pay for a course until you know what it adds.
