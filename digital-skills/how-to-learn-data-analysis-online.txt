<p>You open a course site, and you see hundreds of data courses. One promises a job in three months. Another lists ten tools you have never heard of. A third starts with maths you last saw at school. <h3 id="what-data-analysis-is-and-what-a-data-analyst-does-all-day">What data analysis is, and what a data analyst does all day</h3>
<p>Data analysis is the work of turning raw numbers into clear answers. A shop owner wants to know which products sell best on weekends. A charity wants to know which emails bring donations. A hospital wants to know where waiting times are longest. A data analyst finds the data, cleans it, checks it, and presents the answer in a form a busy person can use.</p>
<p>A typical day mixes four kinds of work. First, the analyst asks a clear question with the person who needs the answer. Second, the analyst gathers data from a spreadsheet, a database, or a report, and cleans it. Cleaning means fixing missing values, removing duplicates, and making dates and labels consistent. Third, the analyst explores the data with simple counts, averages, and charts to find patterns. Fourth, the analyst shares the result in a short report or a dashboard, which is a page of charts that updates when the data changes.</p>
<p>You do not need advanced maths to start. You need curiosity, care with detail, and the patience to check your work. The maths you need at the start is mostly averages, percentages, and simple comparisons. You can learn the rest as you go.</p>

<p>Spreadsheets teach you how data behaves. Statistics teaches you how to describe it honestly. SQL teaches you how to get it. Python or R teaches you how to handle more of it. Visualisation teaches you how to show it. Communication is the skill that makes the other five useful, because an answer nobody understands helps nobody.</p>
<p>If you are choosing between related skills, read our guide on the <a href="https://earnquill.com/digital-skills/best-digital-skills-to-learn" rel="noopener" target="_blank">best digital skills to learn</a> first. It will help you see where data analysis fits beside other options such as copywriting and SEO.</p>
<h3 id="step-1-spreadsheets-first">Step 1: Spreadsheets first</h3>
<p>Start with a spreadsheet, not with code. Open Google Sheets, which is free, or Excel if you already have it. Make a small table of your own spending, your reading log, or a list of 50 products with prices and sales. Then practise the five moves that matter most.</p>
<p>First, sort the data by one column, such as price from high to low. Second, filter it, so you see only one group, such as only books or only weekend sales. Third, use a pivot table, which is a tool that groups and summarises data for you, to count sales by product or by month. Fourth, use simple formulas for totals, averages, and percentages. Fifth, make one clear chart from the result, with a title that states the finding, such as "Weekend sales were higher than weekday sales".</p>
<p>Spend two to three weeks here, for about five hours a week. Your goal is not to memorise every formula. Your goal is to feel comfortable asking a question, finding the answer in the data, and showing it in a chart. That habit is the core of the whole job.</p>
<h3 id="step-2-statistics-basics-in-plain-words">Step 2: Statistics basics in plain words</h3>
<p>Statistics sounds hard, but the basics are simple ideas with long names. Learn these five, and you will understand most beginner reports.</p>
<p>The mean is the average. Add the numbers up, then divide by how many there are. The median is the middle value when the numbers are in order. It is useful when one very large number would pull the average up, such as one huge sale in a month of small sales. A percentage shows a part of a whole out of 100. A trend is how a number changes over time, such as sales rising each month. A comparison shows how two groups differ, such as sales in one city against sales in another.</p>
<p>Watch out for two common traps. A small sample can mislead you. If three customers say they love a product, that does not prove most customers love it. A correlation can also mislead you. Two things can rise together without one causing the other, such as ice cream sales and sunburns both rising in summer. The cause is the hot weather, not the ice cream.</p>
<p>Spend one to two weeks on this step. Use free lessons, and test yourself with your own spreadsheet data. Ask a question, work out the mean and the median, and write one sentence that states what you found.</p>
<h3 id="step-3-sql">Step 3: SQL</h3>
<p>SQL is a language for asking a database questions. The letters stand for Structured Query Language, but you only need to remember that it lets you pull out exactly the rows you want. Most companies store their data in a database, so this skill is often the first one employers ask about.</p>
<p>Start with four commands. SELECT chooses the columns you want to see. FROM names the table the data lives in. WHERE filters the rows, such as only orders from 2026. ORDER BY sorts the result, such as sales from high to low. With those four, you can answer most beginner questions.</p>
<p>Practise on a free practice database, such as the ones in the Mode SQL tutorial. Do not buy a course yet. Write 20 to 30 small queries of your own, each answering one real question, such as "Which five products sold most in March?" Check each answer against the data by hand for the first few queries, so you trust your results.</p>
<p>Spend three to four weeks here, for about five hours a week. SQL feels strange for the first few days, then it clicks. If you get stuck, read the error message slowly. It usually tells you which line has the problem.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/digital-skills-data-analysis-spreadsheet.jpg" alt="A laptop screen showing a clean spreadsheet with rows of simple figures and a small chart beside a notebook" /></p>
<h3 id="step-4-python-or-r-and-how-to-choose">Step 4: Python or R, and how to choose</h3>
<p>Python and R are both programming languages used for data work. Python is a general purpose language, which means it is also used for websites, automation, and apps. R is a language built mainly for statistics and charts. Both are free, and both are used by real analysts.</p>
<p>For most beginners, Python is the safer first choice. It has more free courses, more beginner guides, and more jobs that mention it. It also pairs well with the free <a href="https://www.freecodecamp.org/learn/data-analysis-with-python/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Data Analysis with Python course from freeCodeCamp</a>, which covers NumPy, Pandas, Matplotlib, and Seaborn, with five projects. NumPy and Pandas are Python tools for working with tables of data. Matplotlib and Seaborn are tools for drawing charts. You do not need to learn all four at once. Learn Pandas first, because it handles most cleaning and summarising.</p>
<p>Choose R only if you already know you want to work in academic research or in a team that uses R already. Do not learn both at the start. Pick one, and stay with it for at least three months. Switching languages every few weeks is one of the slowest ways to learn.</p>
<p>Spend six to eight weeks on this step. Your goal is to repeat in Python what you already did in a spreadsheet: load a file, clean it, group it, and draw a chart. That is enough to start portfolio work.</p>
<h3 id="step-5-visualisation-with-tableau-public-or-power-bi">Step 5: Visualisation with Tableau Public or Power BI</h3>
<p>Visualisation means turning numbers into charts that a busy reader can understand in seconds. A good chart has a clear title, labelled axes, and no decoration that hides the data. A bad chart uses bright colours, 3D effects, and tiny labels that force the reader to work hard.</p>
<p>Tableau Public is free, and it lets you publish dashboards online, which is useful for a portfolio. Power BI has a free desktop version, and it is common in larger companies. Learn one of them, not both. Start with Tableau Public, because it is free to publish and easy to share.</p>
<p>Practise three chart types first. A bar chart compares groups, such as sales by product. A line chart shows change over time, such as sales by month. A table shows exact numbers when the reader needs them. Add a filter, such as a year or a region, so the reader can explore. Then write one sentence under each chart that states the finding in plain words.</p>
<p>Spend two to three weeks here. Your goal is one clean dashboard with three to four charts, each with a clear title and a one sentence finding. That dashboard can be the first piece in your portfolio.</p>
<h3 id="step-6-build-two-or-three-portfolio-projects">Step 6: Build two or three portfolio projects</h3>
<p>A portfolio is a small set of finished projects that shows what you can do. Employers and clients look at it to judge your thinking, not just your tools. Build two or three projects, and finish each one properly, with a question, clean data, charts, and a short written answer.</p>
<p>Here are three project ideas that use free public data. First, analyse a local shop or market data set, and answer which products sell best, and when. Second, analyse a public transport or weather data set, and answer how delays or rain relate to the time of day. Third, analyse a simple survey or sales data set, and answer which group of customers buys most often.</p>
<p>For each project, write a short page that follows the same shape: the question, where the data came from, how you cleaned it, what the charts show, what you would check next, and what the limits are. Limits matter. If the data covers only three months, say so. If some rows were missing, say how many and what you did about them. Honest limits build trust.</p>
<p>If you want to pair data work with freelance work, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-start-freelancing" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to start freelancing</a>. It explains how to present a new skill to clients without overstating what you can do.</p>
<p>Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Imagine a small online shop with 1,200 orders in three months. You clean the data and find 40 orders with missing postcodes, so you set those aside and note the gap. You group the rest by day of the week, and you find Saturday orders average 62 a week, while Tuesday orders average 31 a week. You group by product, and you find one product brings 28 percent of all sales. Your written answer is three sentences long. Sales are strongest on Saturdays. One product drives just over a quarter of all sales. The shop should check stock for that product before each weekend, and test a Tuesday offer for the slowest day. That is a complete small analysis. It uses simple tools, it answers a real question, and it states its limits.</p>

<h3 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Jumping to Python before you can use a spreadsheet well, so you learn code without learning how data behaves.</li>
<li>Collecting certificates instead of finishing projects, which leaves you with badges and no proof of skill.</li>
<li>Learning two tools at once, such as Python and R, and finishing neither.</li>
<li>Skipping data cleaning, so your charts look neat but your numbers are wrong.</li>
<li>Copying a dashboard without understanding the data behind it, so you cannot explain it when asked.</li>
<li>Hiding missing data instead of stating it, which breaks trust when someone checks.</li>
<li>Quitting after two weeks because progress feels slow, even though the first month is the hardest part.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Can I learn data analysis for free?</h4>
<p>Yes. You can learn spreadsheets, statistics, SQL, Python, and visualisation with free courses and free tools, and you can build a portfolio with free public data. A paid course can add structure, but it is not required to learn the skills or to build finished work.</p>
<h4>Do I need a degree to become a data analyst?</h4>
<p>No, not for most beginner roles or freelance work. Employers and clients look for skills and finished projects first. A degree can help in some larger companies, but a strong portfolio with clear written answers often matters more for a first role.</p>
<h4>How long does it take to learn data analysis?</h4>
<p>At about five hours a week, most beginners reach a small portfolio in about six months. The first month is the slowest, because spreadsheets and SQL feel new. Steady practice matters more than long bursts, and results vary with your time and your starting point.</p>
<h4>Python or R for data analysis?</h4>
<p>Choose Python first, unless you already know you will work in a team that uses R. Python has more free beginner courses, more guides, and more jobs that mention it. Learn one language well for three months before you think about the other.</p>
<h4>Is a certificate enough to get hired?</h4>
<p>No, not on its own. A certificate shows you finished a course, but it does not show how you think. A small portfolio with two or three finished projects, each with a clear question and a written answer, is stronger proof than a certificate alone.</p>
<h4>Is SQL hard to learn?</h4>
<p>SQL feels strange for the first few days, because it reads like a set of short commands rather than normal writing. Most beginners can write useful queries within two to three weeks of steady practice, if they practise on real questions instead of only reading about it.</p>

<p>Pick one small data set, clean it in a spreadsheet, and answer one question with a chart and a written sentence. Then follow the plan in order: statistics, SQL, Python, visualisation, and a portfolio. That is how to learn data analysis online without drowning in course lists.</p>
