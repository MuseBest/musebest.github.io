3d:T3cb8,<p>Search engine optimisation, usually called SEO, sounds technical and mysterious, because online advice contradicts itself, tools show numbers without explaining them, and results take months to appear. That confusion makes many beginners quit before learning the basics. Learning SEO is simpler than tool sellers suggest. SEO means helping search engines understand a page and helping searchers find it, and you learn it best by improving one real page, measuring what changes, and repeating that loop. This guide gives you that learning path in 90 days, using free tools only at the start. It teaches SEO itself, not blog post writing, which blogging guides cover separately.</p>
<p>In this guide, you will learn how search engines find, understand, and rank pages.
In this guide, you will learn keyword research, on-page SEO, technical SEO, and off-page SEO in order.
In this guide, you will run a practice page experiment and read Search Console correctly.
In this guide, you will learn what SEO cannot do, which shortcuts to avoid, and how long results take.</p>
<h2 id="understand-how-search-works-first">Understand how search works first</h2>
<p>Before tools, learn the three steps every search engine follows. Crawling means automated programmes visit pages by following links. Indexing means the search engine stores and organises those pages. Ranking means it orders matching pages using relevance, quality, and user experience. A page crawlers cannot reach cannot rank, however good its words are. A page that does not match search intent will rank poorly, however well it is written. Search intent is the job the searcher wants done: information, such as "what is SEO", comparison, such as "best SEO course for beginners", or action, such as "SEO course sign up". Your first SEO skill is matching a page to one intent. The official <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide" rel="noopener" target="_blank">SEO Starter Guide</a> from Google Search Central explains these basics plainly.</p>
<h2 id="learn-in-this-order-not-all-at-once">Learn in this order, not all at once</h2>
<p>Beginners often jump between tools, tactics, and trends, and learn nothing deeply. Follow this order instead, because each stage builds on the last. Stage one is search and intent: how crawling, indexing, and ranking work, plus classifying searches by intent. Stage two is keyword research: finding what people search for, grouping related searches, and choosing one main topic per page. Stage three is on-page SEO: titles, headings, structure, links, and images. Stage four is technical SEO basics: speed, mobile, sitemaps, and crawl problems. Stage five is off-page SEO: how links and reputation work, and why honest mentions beat bought links. Stage six is measurement: Search Console, what changed, and when to wait instead of changing everything again. Do not skip to links before intent and structure are sound.</p>
<h2 id="do-keyword-research-with-one-worked-example">Do keyword research with one worked example</h2>
<p>Keyword research means finding the words and questions real people type, then choosing which page should answer each group. Use free tools at the start: Search Console for your own site, search suggestions, and questions people ask in forums and comments. Work through one example. Suppose your practice page is about home vegetable gardening for beginners, starting from "grow vegetables at home". Suggestions give narrower phrases: "grow tomatoes in pots", "when to plant carrots", and "vegetable garden for small balcony". Group them by intent. "Grow tomatoes in pots" is a how to intent deserving its own page. "When to plant carrots" is a timing question fitting inside a planting calendar page. "Vegetable garden for small balcony" is a planning intent that could be a guide topic. Choose one primary phrase per page, use it naturally in the title, first paragraph, and one heading, and use related phrases where they fit. Do not chase a density percentage. Natural use and a page that fully answers the question matter more. Keep a simple keyword map, one row per page with its primary phrase, related phrases, and intent, so you do not make two pages competing for the same search.</p>

<h2 id="learn-on-page-seo-on-one-practice-page">Learn on-page SEO on one practice page</h2>
<p>On-page SEO means everything you can control on the page itself. Pick one practice page you own or can edit, such as a personal site or a free blog. Improve the page in this order. First, fix the title tag, the clickable headline in search results: keep it under about 60 characters, name the topic plainly, and match the page content. Second, write a meta description, the short summary under the title: keep it around 140 to 160 characters, describe the benefit honestly, and do not promise what the page does not deliver. Third, structure headings: one main heading stating the topic, then headings following the reader's questions in order. Fourth, improve the content: answer the main question early, cover subquestions fully, and add a table or list where it helps scanning. Fifth, add internal links to related pages, using descriptive anchor text. Sixth, check images: use descriptive file names and alt text, and compress large images so the page loads faster.</p>
<h2 id="learn-technical-basics-without-code">Learn technical basics without code</h2>
<p>Technical SEO sounds like developer work, but beginners can check the most important basics without writing code. Can search engines reach the page? Check that it is linked from somewhere, that no setting blocks it, and that the site has a sitemap listing pages you want found. Does the page load reasonably fast on a phone? Test it with <a href="https://pagespeed.web.dev/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">PageSpeed Insights</a> and fix the largest images first, the most common beginner cause of slow pages. Does the page work well on a phone? Check text size, button spacing, and scrolling on a real phone. Are there broken links? Click every link and fix errors you find. Is the page secure, using HTTPS? Most modern hosts provide this by default, but check it once. Does the page have duplicate versions under two addresses? If so, note it for a developer or host support. You need to spot problems, describe them clearly, and know which ones matter most.</p>
<h2 id="learn-off-page-seo-honestly">Learn off-page SEO honestly</h2>
<p>Off-page SEO means everything that happens away from your page, mainly links and mentions from other sites. A link acts like a vote of trust, but not all votes are equal. A link from a relevant, trusted site that mentions your page naturally is valuable. A link from an unrelated directory, a paid link farm, or a comment spam campaign can harm trust and waste money. Earn links by making pages worth linking to, then telling the right people: a useful guide shared with a relevant community, a genuine local listing, or a guest contribution that helps another site's readers. Do not buy links, join link exchange schemes, or promise links for payment without understanding the risks, because search engines treat manipulative links as a violation of their guidelines. Social shares alone are not a ranking shortcut, and a large follower count does not replace a useful page. </p>
<h2 id="measure-with-search-console-on-your-practice-page">Measure with Search Console on your practice page</h2>
<p>Measurement is where SEO learning becomes real, and Google Search Console is the free tool to start with. It shows how your site appears in Google Search, explained in <a href="https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9128668" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Search Console help</a>.</p>
<p>| Report | What it shows | How a beginner uses it |
| Performance | Impressions, clicks, position, and real queries | Compare before and after, waiting two to four weeks |
| Coverage | Indexed, problem, and excluded pages | Fix problems, starting with your practice page |
| Sitemaps | Whether your sitemap was read | Confirm it is submitted and read |
| Page experience | Phone and speed problems | Fix large images and phone layout first |</p>
<p>After your changes, wait at least two to four weeks, then compare impressions and clicks with the period before the change. Rising impressions with flat clicks often means the title or description needs work. Clicks for new queries mean the page now matches more searches. No change after two weeks does not mean failure. Change one thing at a time, note the date, and resist changing everything again after three days of flat data.</p>

<h2 id="follow-a-90-day-learning-plan">Follow a 90 day learning plan</h2>
<p>The table below turns this guide into a plan. Keep the order, and keep the practice page at the centre, so theory always ends in a measurable change.</p>
<p>| Phase | Days | What you learn | What you finish |
| Search and intent | 1 to 14 | Crawling, indexing, ranking, intent | A notes file and 20 searches classified by intent |
| Keyword research | 15 to 30 | Free tools, grouping, one topic per page | A keyword map for five pages |
| On-page SEO | 31 to 50 | Titles, headings, structure, internal links, images | One practice page improved and dated |
| Technical basics | 51 to 65 | Speed, mobile, sitemap, broken links, HTTPS | A technical checklist completed for the practice page |
| Off-page and measurement | 66 to 90 | Honest links, Search Console reading, waiting | A measurement note comparing before and after, plus one honest outreach attempt |</p>
<p>Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Suppose your practice page gets 40 impressions and 2 clicks before your changes, then 120 impressions and 8 clicks four weeks after you improve its title, headings, and structure. That is progress, not a promise. The skill is reading data calmly, fixing the weakest part, and waiting again.</p>
<h2 id="what-it-costs-and-what-to-expect">What it costs and what to expect</h2>
<p>You can learn SEO for free. Google Search Central guides, Search Console, and PageSpeed Insights are free, and free keyword research can use search suggestions and your own site data. A paid course or tool is only worth it for structured practice, feedback on a real site, or data you cannot get free. Expect to spend nothing in your first 90 days with free tools, and more only for a course with real feedback. On time, expect about three months to understand the full path and run one measured experiment, and six to twelve months before you can judge SEO work confidently across sites. On results, be honest. A new site usually takes three to six months to show meaningful search traffic, longer where competition is strong. No one can guarantee a ranking or a traffic number, and anyone who promises one should be treated with suspicion. Results vary with experience, location, demand, time, competition, expenses, and platform rules.</p>
<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h2>
<p>Many beginners chase keyword density instead of answering the question fully. Write naturally.
Many beginners change everything at once, then cannot tell what worked. Change one thing, note the date, and wait.</p>
<h2 id="risks-and-scams-to-watch-for">Risks and scams to watch for</h2>
<p>SEO attracts sellers, and some of them are dishonest. Watch for anyone who guarantees a top ranking, a fixed number of visitors, or first page results in a set number of weeks, because no one controls search rankings that way. Watch for paid link packages, private blog networks, and automated tools promising hundreds of links, because manipulative links risk penalties and wasted money. Watch for courses teaching shortcuts such as hidden text, copied content, or fake reviews, which violate search engine guidelines and can harm a site long term. Watch for audits listing only generic problems without checking your site, and for retainers with no clear work, reporting, or exit terms. If you hire help later, ask what work will be done and how it will be measured. For building a site to practise on, read <a href="/blogging/how-to-start-a-blog" rel="noopener" target="_self">How to Start a Blog in 2026</a>, and for how blog writing fits inside SEO, read <a href="/blogging/how-to-write-blog-posts-that-rank-on-google" rel="noopener" target="_self">How to Write Blog Posts That Rank on Google</a> as a companion, not a replacement. Clear copy also helps SEO, as shown in <a href="/digital-skills/how-to-learn-copywriting" rel="noopener" target="_self">How to Learn Copywriting in 2026</a>, but keep the two skills separate in your plan.</p>
<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 id="how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-seo">How long does it take to learn SEO?</h3>
<p>About three months to understand the full path and run one measured practice experiment, and six to twelve months to judge SEO work confidently across sites. Results on a new site usually take three to six months to show meaningful traffic, and some topics take longer because competition is strong.</p>
<h3 id="what-is-the-best-order-to-learn-seo">What is the best order to learn SEO?</h3>
<p>Learn search and intent first, then keyword research, on-page SEO, technical basics, off-page SEO, and finally measurement. Each stage builds on the last.</p>
<h3 id="which-free-seo-tools-should-beginners-use">Which free SEO tools should beginners use?</h3>
<p>Start with Google Search Central guides, Search Console for your own site data, and PageSpeed Insights for speed and mobile checks. Free keyword research can use search suggestions and your own Search Console queries. Paid tools can wait until you understand their numbers.</p>
<h3 id="how-do-i-practise-seo-without-a-website">How do I practise SEO without a website?</h3>
<p>Audit public pages, classify searches by intent, and build keyword maps for topics you know well. A page you can edit teaches faster, so a simple personal site or free blog is worth setting up once you understand the basics.</p>
<h3 id="how-do-i-know-if-my-seo-work-is-working">How do I know if my SEO work is working?</h3>
<p>Compare Search Console impressions, clicks, and queries before and after a change, waiting at least two to four weeks. Rising impressions with flat clicks often means the title needs work, while clicks for new queries mean the page matches more searches. Judge one change at a time, not daily noise.</p>

<p>Learning SEO is a loop: understand search, choose one topic per page, improve one practice page, check technical basics, earn honest mentions, and measure one change at a time. Do that loop for 90 days. Your next step is to pick one practice page today and improve its title and headings this week.
