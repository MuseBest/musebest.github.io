1f:Tb6b,<h2 id="the-three-questions">The three questions</h2>
<p>A skill is worth learning for earning if it passes three tests:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Do clients pay for it?</strong> Look at job listings and freelance marketplaces. If almost nobody is hiring for it, it is a hobby, not an income skill — for now.</li>
<li><strong>Can you learn it in months, not years?</strong> Some skills, like data analysis or video editing, have a usable beginner level. Others, like advanced software engineering, take longer to reach a billable standard.</li>
<li><strong>Will you enjoy the work?</strong> You will spend hundreds of hours practising. A skill you dislike is one you will abandon.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="how-to-check-demand-yourself">How to check demand yourself</h2>
<p>Do not trust a course that says a skill is "in demand". Check it:</p>
<ul>
<li>Search freelance marketplaces for the skill and count active jobs.</li>
<li>Read job listings and note the tools they require.</li>
<li>Look at the rates posted, and whether jobs are one-off or recurring.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="learning-without-wasting-money">Learning without wasting money</h2>
<p>Start with free, high-quality material: official documentation, free courses from reputable providers, and practice projects. Pay for a course only when you know what it teaches that the free material does not, and when you have already started practising.</p>
<h2 id="getting-to-your-first-paid-job">Getting to your first paid job</h2>
<p>Learn in public: publish your practice work, help in communities, and offer a small paid project to a real client as soon as your work is good enough — not perfect. The first paid job teaches you more about the market than another month of tutorials.</p>
<h2 id="reassess-every-year">Reassess every year</h2>
<p>Skills age. A tool that is essential today may be automated tomorrow. Keep one eye on how your skill is changing, and keep learning the parts that machines do badly: judgement, taste, communication, and understanding what a client actually needs.</p>
