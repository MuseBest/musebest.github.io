<h2 id="what-is-data-science-and-how-is-it-different-from-data-analysis">What is data science, and how is it different from data analysis?</h2>
<p>Data science is the practice of using statistics, programming and machine learning to find patterns in large sets of data and to make predictions from them. A data scientist collects messy data, cleans it, analyses it, builds a model and explains what the results mean for a business decision. The work sits across banking, healthcare, retail, marketing and government, and almost every large employer now keeps data of some kind.</p>
<p>Data analysis is narrower, and this is the distinction that matters most when you choose what to study. A data analyst works mainly with data that already exists. They clean it, summarise it with spreadsheets or SQL, and build charts and dashboards that describe what happened. A data scientist goes further. They use statistics and machine learning to predict what will happen next, and they build the models that make those predictions. If you want the full picture of the narrower role first, read our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/digital-skills/how-to-learn-data-analysis-online" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to learn data analysis online</a>.</p>
<p>Think of it this way. An analyst might report that sales fell by 12 percent last month and show which products drove the fall. A data scientist might build a model that predicts which customers are most likely to stop buying next month, so the business can act before it happens. The second job needs more maths and more programming, and it usually pays more, but it also takes longer to learn. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics reported a median annual salary of $120,230 for data scientists in its May 2025 report, which shows why so many learners aim for this field. Pay varies a great deal by country, experience and employer, so treat that figure as a US benchmark, not a promise.</p>
<h2 id="what-skills-do-you-need-to-learn-data-science">What skills do you need to learn data science?</h2>
<p>Data science is a mix of four skill groups. You need some of each, and you do not need to master any of them before you start the next one.</p>

SQL is the language you use to ask a database for the rows you need. You will use both in almost every data job, so learn them early.</p>
<h2 id="step-1-decide-your-goal-and-pick-a-route">Step 1: Decide your goal and pick a route</h2>
<p>Before you enrol in anything, write down what you want data science to do for you. There are three common goals, and each one changes what you should study.</p>
<p>First, a job as a data analyst or junior data scientist. This is the most common goal. You need a structured course, a certificate and three solid projects. Expect 6 to 12 months of steady study at 8 to 12 hours a week.</p>
<p>Second, a freelance income from data work. You need the same core skills, plus the ability to find clients and explain results in plain language. Our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-work-as-a-freelance-data-analyst" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to work as a freelance data analyst</a> covers the client side, and our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/ai-earning/how-to-offer-ai-data-analysis-services" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to offer AI data analysis services</a> shows how data skills fit into paid AI work.</p>
<p>Third, using data science in your current job. You may only need Python, SQL and dashboards, and you can skip the deeper machine learning work at first. This is the fastest route, and a single course plus one work project is often enough.</p>
<h2 id="step-2-learn-python-and-sql-first">Step 2: Learn Python and SQL first</h2>
<p>Python and SQL are the foundation. Do not start with machine learning. Models are built on top of these two tools, and learners who skip them get stuck on simple errors for weeks.</p>
<p>Start with Python. Learn variables, lists, loops, functions and how to read a file. Then learn pandas, the library that turns a spreadsheet-like table into something you can filter, group and summarise with a few lines of code. Kaggle Learn offers free short courses in Python and pandas that run in your browser, with exercises on real data and a free certificate for each course. You can find them on <a href="https://www.kaggle.com/learn" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Kaggle Learn</a>. Work through the Python course first, then pandas. Give this stage three to four weeks at about an hour a day.</p>
<p>Then learn SQL. Learn how to select rows, filter with a WHERE clause, sort results, join two tables and group data with COUNT and AVG. Kaggle Learn also has a free introductory SQL course, and many learners find SQL easier than Python because each query reads almost like a sentence. Give this stage two weeks.</p>
<p>Practise by writing small scripts every day rather than watching long video lectures. A useful daily habit is to load a small public dataset, ask it one question, and write the code that answers it.</p>

<h2 id="step-3-build-your-statistics-and-maths-foundations">Step 3: Build your statistics and maths foundations</h2>
<p>Statistics is the part most beginners want to skip, and it is the part that separates a data scientist from someone who only runs code. You do not need a maths degree. You need a working grasp of a short list of ideas, learned alongside real data rather than in a separate maths block.</p>
<p>Learn these ideas in this order. First, averages, medians and spread, so you can describe a dataset in one paragraph. Second, probability and distributions, so you understand what "likely" means in numbers. Third, correlation, so you can tell whether two things move together, and why that does not prove that one causes the other. Fourth, regression, which is the simplest way to predict one number from another. Fifth, the basics of linear algebra, such as vectors and matrices, because machine learning libraries are built on them. You can learn each idea in a few hours with free videos, and the best known free visual explanations come from the 3Blue1Brown channel on YouTube.</p>
<p>Learn the maths when a project makes you need it. If your project needs a regression, learn regression that week. This keeps the maths tied to something real, and it stops the common trap of spending three months on textbooks before writing a single line of code. The strongest beginner programmes start with statistical foundations before moving into coding, so that learners understand why an algorithm works rather than only how to run it.</p>
<h2 id="step-4-learn-data-cleaning-and-visualisation">Step 4: Learn data cleaning and visualisation</h2>
<p>Real data is messy. Values are missing, dates are written in three formats, and the same customer appears under two spellings of their name. Cleaning is the least glamorous part of data science and the part employers test most, because it takes up a large share of working hours.</p>
<p>Learn to handle missing values, remove duplicates, fix inconsistent labels and change text dates into real dates. Pandas covers all of this, and Kaggle Learn has a short free course on data cleaning that teaches it with exercises. Then learn visualisation. A clear chart often matters more than a clever model, because it is how a manager or a client understands your work. Learn bar charts, line charts, scatter plots and histograms first. Learn dashboards later, once you can make a single good chart.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-learn-machine-learning-basics">Step 5: Learn machine learning basics</h2>
<p>Machine learning is a set of methods that let a computer find patterns in data and make predictions from them. You do not need to learn every method. Start with the four that appear in almost every beginner job.</p>
<p>First, linear regression, which predicts a number, such as a house price, from other numbers. Second, logistic regression, which predicts a yes or no answer, such as whether a customer will leave. Third, decision trees and random forests, which make predictions by asking a series of yes or no questions about the data. Fourth, clustering, which groups similar rows together, for example grouping customers by buying habits.</p>
<p>Learn them with scikit-learn, the Python library that most beginners use. Kaggle Learn has a free introductory machine learning course that takes about three hours and builds your first models in a browser notebook. After that, learn how to test a model properly. Split your data into a training set and a test set, and check the model on data it has never seen. A model that looks perfect on training data and fails on new data is the most common beginner error, and it has a name: overfitting.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-build-three-portfolio-projects">Step 6: Build three portfolio projects</h2>
<p>Courses teach you the tools. Projects prove you can use them. Employers and clients look at projects first, so build three that show different skills, and publish them on GitHub with a short README that explains what you did and what you found.</p>
<p>Project one: an analysis project. Take a public dataset, clean it, analyse it and present the findings with charts. A good example is analysing a city's bike hire data to find the busiest hours and the effect of weather on demand. This shows data cleaning, visualisation and clear writing.</p>
<p>Project two: a prediction project. Build a model that predicts something useful, such as house prices or whether a loan will be repaid. Show how you tested the model and how accurate it was on data it had not seen. This shows machine learning and honest evaluation.</p>
<p>Project three: a dashboard project. Build a simple dashboard that updates from a dataset, using a free tool such as Streamlit or Tableau Public. This shows that you can turn analysis into something a non-technical person can use.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/digital-skills-data-science-projects.jpg" alt="A person presenting a data dashboard with bar charts and a line graph on a large screen" /></p>
<p>Write each project up in plain language. Say what question you asked, what data you used, what you found and what you would improve next time. One honest paragraph about a limitation is worth more than a page of hype. If you want ideas for the wider skill set around these projects, our guide to the <a href="https://earnquill.com/digital-skills/best-digital-skills-to-learn" rel="noopener" target="_blank">best digital skills to learn</a> places data science alongside the skills that pair well with it.</p>

<h2 id="how-to-learn-data-science-online-what-it-costs">How to learn data science online: what it costs</h2>
<p>You can learn data science for nothing. Kaggle Learn, Harvard CS50 audit mode, free YouTube explanations and public datasets cost no money at all. The only costs are your time and an internet connection. A paid route costs more but adds structure. A Coursera subscription of about $49 a month for five months comes to about $245, and a DataCamp subscription of about $25 a month for a year comes to about $300. Check current prices on each course page before you pay.</p>
<p>Expect a slow start. The first month feels hard because everything is new: the code, the maths and the tools. Most learners feel lost around week six, and that is normal. It does not mean you lack talent. It means you are learning three skills at once. By week 12, the pieces start to fit together, and by week 20 you will be able to finish a small project without following a tutorial step by step.</p>
<p>Expect no income at first. Learning is not earning, and a certificate does not guarantee a job. What it does is give you skills you can show in a portfolio, and a portfolio is what turns study into interviews. Results vary with your background, your location, the time you put in and the demand for junior roles where you live. </p>
<h2 id="common-mistakes-to-avoid">Common mistakes to avoid</h2>
<ul>
<li>Starting with machine learning before you know Python and SQL, which leaves you copying code you do not understand.</li>
<li>Watching course after course without writing code, which feels like progress but builds no skill.</li>
<li>Spending months on maths textbooks before touching a dataset, which kills motivation.</li>
<li>Collecting certificates instead of building projects, which impresses no hiring manager.</li>
<li>Using only clean tutorial datasets, which teaches you nothing about the messy data of real work.</li>
<li>Copying a project from a tutorial and calling it your own, which falls apart in an interview.</li>
<li>Comparing your week four with someone else's year two, which is a fast way to quit.</li>
<li>Ignoring communication skills, which means your best analysis never gets used.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="risks-and-scams-to-watch-for">Risks and scams to watch for</h2>
<p>Be careful with expensive bootcamps that promise a job. No course can guarantee employment, and some charge thousands of dollars for material that is free elsewhere. Check the refund policy, read independent reviews, and try the free material first. If a provider pressures you to sign up today, walk away.</p>
<p>Watch for fake job offers that ask you to pay for training, software or a background check before you start work. Real employers do not ask for payment from applicants. Protect your data and your accounts. Use a strong password on learning platforms, and never share your login details. When you publish projects on GitHub, use public datasets only, and never upload personal data or data that belongs to an employer.</p>
<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 id="can-i-learn-data-science-online">Can I learn data science online?</h3>
<p>Yes. Every core skill in data science can be learned online, from Python and SQL to statistics and machine learning. Free courses from Kaggle Learn and Harvard CS50 cover the foundations, and paid certificates add structure. What matters is steady practice and finished projects, not where you study.</p>
<h3 id="do-i-need-a-degree-to-learn-data-science">Do I need a degree to learn data science?</h3>
<p>No. Most online courses have no degree requirement, and employers increasingly hire on skills and portfolios. A degree helps for some research roles and some large employers, but it is not needed to start learning or to apply for junior roles.</p>
<h3 id="how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-data-science">How long does it take to learn data science?</h3>
<p>For a beginner studying 8 to 12 hours a week, expect 6 to 12 months to reach a junior data analyst level, and 12 to 18 months for deeper machine learning work. The exact time depends on your starting point, your weekly hours and how much you practise on real projects.</p>
<h3 id="is-data-science-hard-to-learn">Is data science hard to learn?</h3>
<p>It is demanding because it combines programming, statistics and communication, but it is learnable in small steps. Most learners find the first month the hardest. If you can study one hour a day and finish small exercises, you can learn it.</p>
<h3 id="what-is-the-difference-between-data-science-and-data-analysis">What is the difference between data science and data analysis?</h3>
<p>Data analysis describes what happened in data that already exists, using tools such as spreadsheets, SQL and dashboards. Data science goes further and uses statistics and machine learning to predict what will happen next. Data analysis is a good first step, and many data scientists start there.</p>
<h3 id="are-data-science-certificates-worth-it">Are data science certificates worth it?</h3>
<p>Certificates help when they come from a recognised provider and sit next to real projects. They show a hiring manager that you finished a structured programme. On their own they rarely land a job, so pair any certificate with three portfolio projects that prove you can do the work.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Learning how to learn data science online comes down to a simple order: Python and SQL first, statistics alongside real data, then machine learning, then three projects that prove you can do the work. Pick one course and follow the 24 week plan. Start today with a free Kaggle Learn course.</p>
