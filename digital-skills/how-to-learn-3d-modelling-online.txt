<p>To learn 3D modelling online, start with free Blender, learn to move around in 3D space, model simple objects first, and build up to a small portfolio. 3D modelling is the skill of building objects in three dimensions on a computer, for games, film, product design, and 3D printing. It looks hard from the outside, but the first steps are simple shapes and short tutorials.</p>

<h2 id="what-3d-modelling-is">What 3D modelling is</h2>
<p>3D modelling is the process of building a digital object that has length, width, and height. You build it from a mesh, which is a net of points, edges, and faces. The points are called vertices. The flat surfaces between them are called faces. By moving vertices and adding faces, you shape the mesh into a chair, a character, a building, or a machine part.</p>
<p>There are two broad kinds of 3D modelling. Artistic modelling builds objects that look good, such as game characters, film props, and product images. CAD modelling, which stands for computer aided design, builds objects with exact measurements, such as machine parts and building plans. This guide covers artistic modelling, because that is what most beginners want. CAD is a separate skill with its own tools, such as Fusion 360 and SolidWorks.</p>
<p>A 3D model is not a finished picture. After you build the shape, you add materials and colours, you add lights, and you render it, which turns the 3D scene into a flat image or video. The model, the materials, the lights, and the render are four separate skills, and you learn them one at a time.</p>

It includes modelling, sculpting, texturing, animation, and rendering in one program. You can learn the whole skill in one tool.</p>

<h2 id="step-2-learn-to-navigate-3d-space">Step 2: Learn to navigate 3D space</h2>
<p>The first skill is moving around in the 3D view. Before you model anything, spend a few hours learning to orbit, pan, and zoom. Orbit means to turn the view around the object. Pan means to slide the view sideways. Zoom means to move closer or further away.</p>
<p>Learn the number pad views too. Front, side, and top views help you check the shape of your model, because a model that looks right from the front can look wrong from the side.</p>
<p>Learn to select and move objects, to add a cube, a sphere, and a cylinder, and to switch between object mode, which moves whole objects, and edit mode, which changes the shape of one object. These basics feel slow at first, and they become fast with practice. Do not skip them.</p>

<p>Model each object on your own after you watch a tutorial. Watching is not learning. If you only follow the tutor click by click, you will forget the steps the next day. Watch once, then close the video and build the same object alone.</p>

<h2 id="step-4-learn-topology-and-modifiers">Step 4: Learn topology and modifiers</h2>
<p>Topology is the layout of the faces on your model. Good topology means the faces are mostly quads, which are four sided shapes, and they flow in clean lines around the model. Bad topology means messy triangles that pinch and crease when the model moves. For still objects, messy topology matters less. For characters that bend and move, it matters a lot.</p>
<p>Learn edge loops, which are rings of faces that follow the shape of the model. A face needs edge loops around the eyes and the mouth, for example, so it can open and close cleanly. Learn to add loops where a shape needs to bend, and remove them where it does not.</p>
<p>Modifiers are tools that change your model without destroying it. The mirror modifier copies one side of a model to the other side, so you only build half a symmetrical object. The bevel modifier rounds sharp edges, which makes models look real. The subdivision surface modifier smooths a blocky model into a curved one. Learn these three first, then add more as you need them.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-try-sculpting-and-uv-unwrapping">Step 5: Try sculpting and UV unwrapping</h2>
<p>Sculpting is pushing and pulling a digital surface as if it were clay. It suits organic shapes, such as faces, animals, and rocks. Blender has sculpting built in, and it is free. ZBrush is the specialist sculpting tool, but you do not need it as a beginner.</p>
<p>Start sculpting with a sphere. Pull it into a simple head shape, and add the brow, the nose, and the chin with the grab and smooth brushes. Do not aim for a perfect face. Aim to learn how the brushes feel. Sculpting takes months to learn well, so treat this step as an introduction.</p>
<p>UV unwrapping is the job of flattening the surface of a 3D model into a flat 2D sheet, so you can paint a texture onto it. Think of peeling an orange and laying the peel flat on a table. You cut seams where the peel should split, then you unwrap it and paint on it. Blender has a checker texture you can use to test your unwrap: if the squares look even, the unwrap is good.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-texture-light-and-render-your-models">Step 6: Texture, light, and render your models</h2>
<p>Texturing is adding colour and surface detail to a model. PBR textures, which stands for physically based rendering, are the standard way to do this. A PBR material has maps for colour, roughness, and bumpiness. Roughness controls how shiny a surface looks: a polished metal ball has low roughness, and a brick wall has high roughness. Learn roughness first, because it changes the look of a model more than colour does.</p>
<p>Lighting is the second half of a good render. A model lit by one flat light looks dull. A model lit by a key light, a fill light, and a rim light looks real. The key light is the main light, the fill light softens the shadows, and the rim light adds a bright edge that separates the model from the background. Learn this three point setup and use it for every portfolio render.</p>
<p>Rendering turns the scene into an image. Blender has two render engines: Cycles, which is slow and realistic, and Eevee, which is fast and good enough for most work. Start with Eevee, because fast renders mean you can test more ideas.</p>
<h2 id="step-7-build-a-portfolio">Step 7: Build a portfolio</h2>
<p>A portfolio is a small set of your best finished models, shown with clean renders. Aim for 6 to 10 pieces. One good chair render beats ten unfinished models.</p>
<p>Include a mix of work: two simple props, two harder objects, one character or sculpt, and one small environment. Show each model from two or three angles, and add a wireframe view for one or two pieces, because it shows your topology is clean. Write one line under each piece that says what you made and what you learned.</p>
<p>Show your portfolio on ArtStation, which is the main site for 3D artists, and on a simple personal page. Keep it updated. Remove old work when your new work is clearly better. A portfolio is a living thing, not a finished document.</p>

<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Do not start with a character or a car, because those need skills you do not have yet.</li>
<li>Do not use too many polygons early, because a dense mesh is hard to fix and slow to render.</li>
<li>Do not ignore scale, because a chair that is ten times too big will break your scene.</li>
<li>Do not follow tutorials without modelling alone afterwards, because watching is not learning.</li>
<li>Do not skip the reference image, because memory makes objects look wrong.</li>
<li>Do not model without checking the side and top views, because a model can look right from the front and wrong from the side.</li>
<li>Do not buy expensive software before you try Blender, because Blender is free and covers the basics.</li>
<li>Do not compare your first month to a professional with years of practice, because that comparison only causes frustration.</li>

<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 id="which-3d-modelling-software-should-i-start-with">Which 3D modelling software should I start with?</h3>
<p>Start with Blender. It is free, it covers modelling, sculpting, texturing, and rendering in one program, and it has the largest free tutorial community.</p>
<h3 id="is-blender-free">Is Blender free?</h3>
<p>Yes. Blender is free and open source, and it will stay free. You can download it from blender.org, use it for personal or commercial work, and never pay a licence fee. Paid add ons exist, but you do not need them as a beginner.</p>
<h3 id="how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-3d-modelling">How long does it take to learn 3D modelling?</h3>
<p>Simple props take 1 to 2 months at 4 to 6 hours a week. Characters and complex work take 6 months to 2 years. These are guide estimates, not promises, and your speed depends on how often you practise.</p>
<h3 id="do-i-need-a-drawing-tablet">Do I need a drawing tablet?</h3>
<p>No, not to start. A mouse is fine for box modelling and hard surface work. A tablet helps for sculpting, because it feels more like drawing. Buy one later, once you know you enjoy sculpting.</p>
<h3 id="can-i-learn-3d-modelling-without-a-degree">Can I learn 3D modelling without a degree?</h3>
<p>Yes. Most 3D artists are judged by their portfolio, not by a degree. Studios and clients look at your models, your renders, and your topology.</p>
<h3 id="what-computer-do-i-need-for-3d-modelling">What computer do I need for 3D modelling?</h3>
<p>Most modern laptops can run Blender for simple models. A computer with 16 gigabytes of memory and a dedicated graphics card will render faster and handle bigger scenes. Start with the computer you have.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>To learn 3D modelling online, start with free Blender, learn to move around in 3D space, model simple objects first, then learn topology, sculpting, texturing, and rendering one skill at a time. Follow the project ladder, model alone after each tutorial, and build a small portfolio of 6 to 10 finished pieces.</p>
