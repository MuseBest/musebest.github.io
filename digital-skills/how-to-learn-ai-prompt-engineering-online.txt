<h2 id="what-is-ai-prompt-engineering">What is AI prompt engineering?</h2>
<p>Prompt engineering is the skill of writing clear instructions for an AI tool so it gives you the answer you need. A prompt is the message you type into ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or any other AI chatbot. It is the practice of choosing what to put in that message: the task, the context, examples, and the format you want back.</p>
<p>You do not need to code to do it. If you can explain a task to a classmate, you can learn it. The same AI model can give a weak answer or a useful one, and the difference is often the prompt. A vague prompt gets a generic answer. A clear prompt gets something you can actually use.</p>
<p>Prompt engineering is also a real, paid skill. It shows up in job descriptions for marketing, support, product and engineering roles, and it makes everything else you do faster: writing, research, drafting and planning. For freelancers, it is a skill you can sell as part of a service. If you want to see how freelance work fits together, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-start-freelancing" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to start freelancing</a>.</p>
<h2 id="how-an-ai-model-reads-your-prompt">How an AI model reads your prompt</h2>
<p>A large language model, often called an LLM, predicts the most likely next chunk of text, over and over, based on everything it has seen so far. That is genuinely it.</p>
<p>Three facts follow from this, and they shape how you should prompt:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>It has no memory between chats unless the tool gives it one.</strong> Every conversation starts fresh.</li>
<li><strong>It can be confidently wrong.</strong> It predicts plausible text, not verified truth. Always check facts that matter, especially numbers, names and dates.</li>
<li><strong>Context is everything.</strong> It only knows what is in the conversation right now, plus its training. Leave out the audience or the purpose and it guesses.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="step-1-learn-the-five-parts-of-a-good-prompt">Step 1: Learn the five parts of a good prompt</h2>
<p>Almost every strong prompt has some combination of five parts. You will not always need all five, but knowing them tells you what to add.</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Part</th>
<th>What it does</th>
<th>Example</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody><tr>
<td>Role</td>
<td>Tells the model who to be, which shapes its vocabulary and depth</td>
<td>You are a senior editor reviewing a blog post for a beginner</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Task</td>
<td>Says exactly what you want done, with a clear action verb</td>

<td>Gives the background it cannot guess: audience, purpose, what you already tried</td>

<td>Describes the shape of the answer you want</td>

<p>You rarely write these as labelled sections. Just make sure each one is present somewhere in the message. When an answer misses the mark, ask which of the five is missing. Most bad answers are missing context, a clear task, or a format.</p>
<h2 id="step-2-master-the-core-techniques">Step 2: Master the core techniques</h2>
<p>These are the named patterns you will see everywhere. Learn the first four properly.</p>
<p><strong>Zero-shot prompting.</strong> Just ask, with no examples. This is the default way most people use AI, and it works well for common tasks such as "Classify this review as positive or negative."</p>
<p><strong>Few-shot prompting.</strong> Show two to five examples of the input and the output you want, then give your real input. Examples teach format and style far better than descriptions. Use it when you need a specific tone, a fixed layout, or consistent labels.</p>
<p><strong>Chain of thought.</strong> Ask the model to reason step by step before it answers. This improves accuracy on logic, maths and multi-step problems. Add a line such as "Think through this step by step before answering."</p>
<p><strong>Role prompting.</strong> Assign an identity and an expertise level. This changes the vocabulary, the depth, and what the model assumes you already know. Use it when you want a specific perspective, such as a hiring manager, a teacher, or a customer.</p>
<p><strong>Delimiters.</strong> Wrap different parts of your prompt in clear markers so the model knows what is instruction and what is data. Use triple quotes, XML-style tags, or markdown headings.</p>
<p><strong>Structured output.</strong> Ask for an exact structure: JSON, a markdown table, a bullet list, or numbered steps. This is essential when the output feeds into code or a spreadsheet. The <a href="https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-engineering" rel="noopener" target="_blank">OpenAI prompt engineering guide</a> explains how it works.</p>
<p><strong>Prompt chaining.</strong> Break a big task into several prompts and feed each output into the next: research, then outline, then draft, then edit. This is far more reliable than one giant prompt.</p>
<p><strong>Self-critique.</strong> Ask the model to review and improve its own answer, for example: "Now critique your answer and give an improved version." This often catches real errors on the second pass.</p>
<p><strong>RAG, or retrieval augmented generation.</strong> This means feeding the model your own documents so it answers from real sources instead of memory. It is the main cure for hallucination, and you will meet it again in Step 5.</p>
<h2 id="step-3-practise-on-real-tasks-every-week">Step 3: Practise on real tasks every week</h2>
<p>Courses teach the ideas. Practice builds the skill. Pick one task you already do and use it as your training ground, such as summarising readings, drafting emails, or planning a week.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/digital-skills-prompt-engineering-practice.jpg" alt="A person comparing two AI chatbot answers side by side on a laptop screen at a desk, with a notebook of handwritten notes" /></p>
<p>Use this weekly routine:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Pick one real task.</strong> Choose something you genuinely need, not a made-up exercise.</li>
<li><strong>Write the prompt with the five parts.</strong> Role, task, context, format, constraints.</li>
<li><strong>Compare the answer with a one-line prompt.</strong> Run the same task twice, once with a lazy prompt and once with a structured one, and note the difference.</li>
<li><strong>Save the prompts that work.</strong> Keep a simple document of your best prompts, grouped by task. This becomes your personal library.</li>
<li><strong>Refine once.</strong> Treat the first answer as a draft. If it is too long, say "Make it half as long." Good prompting is a short back-and-forth, not one perfect message.</li>
</ol>
<p>Do this three times a week and you will improve faster than someone who watches ten hours of video without practising. The <a href="https://docs.anthropic.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/overview" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Anthropic prompt engineering overview</a> makes the same point: define what success looks like first, then test your prompt against that standard.</p>
<h2 id="step-4-build-a-small-prompt-portfolio">Step 4: Build a small prompt portfolio</h2>
<p>A portfolio proves the skill better than any certificate. You do not need client work to build one. You need five to ten short examples that show a problem, the prompt, and the result.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/digital-skills-prompt-engineering-portfolio.jpg" alt="A desk with a laptop showing a neat collection of saved prompt templates in a document, with sticky notes and a checklist on paper" /></p>
<p>Build it like this:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pick five common tasks.</strong> For example: summarise an article, draft a follow-up email, turn meeting notes into action points, and explain a technical idea simply.</li>
<li><strong>Write one strong prompt for each.</strong> Use the five parts and the techniques from Step 2.</li>
<li><strong>Show the before and after.</strong> Keep the lazy prompt, the improved prompt, and a short note on what changed and why.</li>
<li><strong>Keep it honest.</strong> Label every example as your own practice work. Never present a made-up client result as real.</li>
<li><strong>Put it in one document.</strong> A simple page or PDF is enough. Link to it from your freelance profile or CV.</li>
</ul>
<p>If you plan to offer this skill to clients, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-create-a-freelance-portfolio" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to create a freelance portfolio</a> next.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-go-deeper-with-apis-rag-and-testing">Step 5: Go deeper with APIs, RAG and testing</h2>
<p>Once the basics feel natural, the next level is using AI inside real work rather than only in a chat window. It is where the skill turns into a career advantage.</p>
<p><strong>Call the API from a script.</strong> An API is a way for a program to send a prompt to an AI model and get an answer back. This turns prompting from a chat skill into a building skill. If you want to learn the coding behind it, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/digital-skills/how-to-learn-coding-for-beginners" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to learn coding for beginners</a>.</p>
<p><strong>Learn RAG properly.</strong> A simple version is pasting a document into the prompt. A fuller version uses a small database of documents. Start simple and learn the fuller version only when a real project needs it.</p>
<p><strong>Build a small evaluation set.</strong> Keep a list of ten test inputs with the answers you expect, then measure each new version of a prompt against that list. It replaces guessing with measurement, and it is what separates hobby prompting from professional prompting.</p>
<p><strong>Learn prompt injection and safety basics.</strong> Prompt injection is when hidden text in a document or a web page tries to steer the model. Treat pasted content as data, not as instructions, and never let the model act on text you have not checked.</p>

<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Asking for everything at once.</strong> One giant prompt with six unrelated requests produces six mediocre answers. Chain smaller prompts instead.</li>
<li><strong>Being polite instead of specific.</strong> "Could you please help me a bit with this?" wastes words. Clear and direct beats courteous every time.</li>
<li><strong>Trusting the output without checking.</strong> A confident tone is not accuracy. Verify anything factual, especially numbers, citations and code.</li>
<li><strong>Giving up after one try.</strong> The first prompt is a draft. Good results usually come from two or three rounds of refining.</li>
<li><strong>Saying what not to do.</strong> "Do not be boring" is weak. "Use concrete examples and short sentences" tells the model what you actually want.</li>
<li><strong>Pasting private data anywhere.</strong> Never put passwords, keys, customer data or confidential work into a public AI tool. Check your company policy first.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="risks-and-scams-to-watch-for">Risks and scams to watch for</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Courses that promise a job or a fixed income.</strong> No course can promise this. Prompt engineering is a skill, not a job guarantee. Walk away from any seller who guarantees earnings.</li>
<li><strong>"Secret prompt" sellers.</strong> There is no secret phrase that unlocks the model. Clear instructions beat clever tricks, and the best techniques are published free by the model makers.</li>
<li><strong>Fake certificates.</strong> Some sellers charge for a certificate that no employer recognises. Check whether any real job listing asks for it before you pay.</li>
<li><strong>Privacy risk.</strong> Anything you paste into a free AI tool may be used to improve the service. Never paste passwords, bank details, customer lists, medical notes or unpublished work. Read the tool's privacy page and turn off training use where the setting exists.</li>
<li><strong>Hallucination risk.</strong> The model can invent facts, quotes, links and statistics. Never publish an AI answer without checking it, and never cite a source the model gave you until you have opened it yourself.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 id="do-i-need-coding-to-learn-prompt-engineering">Do I need coding to learn prompt engineering?</h3>
<p>No. Prompts are written in plain language. Coding helps only if you build apps that send prompts to an AI model through an API, but you do not need it to write good prompts in ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini.</p>
<h3 id="how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-prompt-engineering">How long does it take to learn prompt engineering?</h3>
<p>You can learn the basics in an afternoon and see better answers right away. Getting good takes about three months of steady practice on real tasks, at five to seven hours a week.</p>
<h3 id="is-prompt-engineering-a-real-job">Is prompt engineering a real job?</h3>
<p>It is a real skill, and it appears in job descriptions, but it is rarely a standalone job title. Most people use it inside another role, such as marketing, support, writing or data work. Freelancers can also sell it as part of a service.</p>
<h3 id="will-prompt-engineering-still-matter-as-ai-improves">Will prompt engineering still matter as AI improves?</h3>
<p>Yes. Newer models understand vague requests better, but they still cannot guess facts you did not give them, such as your audience or your deadline. Clear instructions still improve the answer.</p>

<p>Learning AI prompt engineering comes down to a simple method: learn the five parts of a good prompt, master four core techniques, practise on real tasks three times a week, and save the prompts that work. Start free and build a small portfolio.</p>
