2b:T5d28,<h2 id="how-to-hire-your-first-employee-for-an-online-business-in-2026">How to Hire Your First Employee for an Online Business in 2026</h2>
<p>Learning how to hire your first employee for an online business is the step that turns a one-person business into a business that can grow. You are doing everything alone: answering emails, packing orders, posting content, and keeping the books. The work that earns money gets squeezed out by the work that keeps the business running. Hiring one person for one clear job can give you back the hours you need to grow, but only if you hire for the right job, at a cost you can afford, and with a clear plan for their first month. This guide is for solo online business owners who need help and want to hire well. This is general information, not legal, tax, or employment advice, and employment law differs by country and by US state, so check your local rules or speak to a qualified adviser before you hire.</p>

<h3 id="are-you-ready-to-hire">Are you ready to hire?</h3>
<p>Hiring too early is as costly as hiring too late, so check four things before you write a job description. First, is there work that repeats every week? A task you do for 5 hours every week, such as answering customer emails, does need help, but a one-off task does not. Second, can the business afford the true cost? Work out the full monthly cost using Step 1, and check that your revenue covers it for at least 3 months, even if sales dip. Third, will you get useful time back? If you hire someone to do 10 hours of admin a week, you must use those 10 hours for work that earns money, such as finding customers. Fourth, is there a clear job to hand over? "Help me with everything" is not a job, but "answer customer emails and process refunds" is one you can hire for, train for, and measure.</p>
<h3 id="what-to-delegate-first">What to delegate first</h3>
<p>Start with a task audit: for one week, write down everything you do, how long it takes, and whether you are the only person who can do it. Then sort the tasks into three groups: tasks you repeat often, tasks you keep postponing, and tasks you do badly and then take back from a helper. The first group is what you delegate. Five roles are common first hires for online businesses, because they take repeat work off the owner's desk.</p>

For most solo owners, that is admin or customer support, because those tasks eat hours every day but do not need your personal skill. Do not hire for a job you have never done yourself, because you will not know what good work looks like, and you will not know how to train them.</p>

A contractor gives you speed and flexibility, but you must get the classification right: the US Small Business Administration warns that misclassifying a contractor as an employee can mean back taxes and penalties and benefits, so do not label someone a contractor just to avoid payroll duties. An agency is the fastest route, and it suits owners who need help now and have no time to recruit, but its rate is the agency's pricing, not a market rate, so compare it with the cost of hiring directly before you commit. Do not treat the agency route as the only way to hire remotely: you can also hire a remote employee or contractor directly, using the same steps in this guide.</p>
<h3 id="step-1-work-out-the-true-cost">Step 1: Work out the true cost</h3>
<p>The salary is only part of the cost. A new hire also costs payroll taxes, insurance, benefits, tools, and the time you spend recruiting and training them. A commonly quoted rule of thumb says the true cost of an employee is 1.25 to 1.4 times their salary, which means a salary of 30,000 dollars a year costs about 37,500 to 42,000 dollars a year in total. Treat that figure as a rule of thumb from a secondary source, not as a verified fact, because the SBA page opened for this guide does not state that figure in the section read. Your real cost depends on your country, your state, and the benefits you offer, so work it out from your own numbers. Worked example only: these numbers are made up and are not typical results. Imagine you hire a part-time employee for 20 hours a week at 15 dollars an hour.</p>

Ask whether the business can afford 1,618 dollars a month for at least 3 months, and whether the hours you get back will earn more than that. If the answer is no, start smaller: fewer hours, or a contractor for one task, instead of an employee.</p>
<h3 id="step-2-get-the-legal-basics-ready">Step 2: Get the legal basics ready</h3>
<p>The legal steps differ by country, so this section gives the US route as an example, and a note for readers elsewhere. In the US, the first steps are to register the business if you have not already, and to get an Employer Identification Number, known as an EIN, from the IRS. An EIN is a federal tax ID number, and you need one if you have employees. The IRS says you can get one for free directly from the IRS in minutes, you can apply online, by fax, or by mail, and you can apply for only 1 EIN per responsible party per day. You can read the full detail on the IRS page for <a href="https://www.irs.gov/businesses/employer-identification-number" rel="noopener" target="_blank">employer identification number</a>. After that, the SBA lists 10 payroll setup steps, including getting an EIN, finding out about state or local tax IDs, deciding between a contractor and an employee, getting a W-4 form from the employee, choosing pay periods, and handling tax reporting. The SBA also says new employees complete a W-4 and Form I-9, and that the IRS requires employment tax records to be kept for at least four years. Check whether your state needs workers' compensation insurance, and whether your state has new hire reporting rules. If you are not in the US, the steps are different, but the idea is the same: register the business, get a tax ID for employment, find out about payroll tax and insurance in your country, and keep records. Check your local tax authority or a qualified adviser, and do not rely on US guides for rules that apply only in the US.</p>
<h3 id="step-3-write-a-clear-job-description">Step 3: Write a clear job description</h3>
<p>A clear job description saves you from hiring the wrong person. It needs seven parts: the job title, the main responsibilities in order of importance, the skills the person needs on day one, the skills you will train them in, the hours and the time zone, the pay range, and whether the job is remote or in person. Write the responsibilities as actions, not as vague qualities: "Answer customer emails within one working day" is a responsibility, while "Be a team player" is not. List 5 to 7 responsibilities, not 20, because a list of 20 tells the candidate you do not know what the job is. Say what success looks like in the first month, such as "The inbox is clear each evening, and no customer waits more than one working day for a reply." Be honest about the pay range: a range that is too wide, such as "10 to 30 dollars an hour", tells candidates you have not worked out the budget, while a clear range, such as "14 to 16 dollars an hour", shows you know what the job is worth. For the basics of writing a fair contract for the role, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/freelance-contracts-explained" rel="noopener" target="_blank">freelance contracts explained</a>, which covers written agreements that also apply to hiring.</p>
<h3 id="step-4-find-candidates">Step 4: Find candidates</h3>
<p>Start where your audience already is. Post the job on your own site and your social media accounts, because people who already follow your business understand what it does. Then use job boards and LinkedIn, which reach people who are actively looking. Referrals work well too: ask people you know, and ask friends who run online businesses, whether they know someone good. One older guide recommends asking in Facebook groups for online business owners, and that method still works, but the groups change over time, so check that a group is active and well run before you post there, and do not rely on a list of groups from 2017, because groups close and rename. Write the job post the same way you wrote the job description: clear title, clear responsibilities, clear hours, and clear pay. Do not ask for a CV only. Ask for a short note on why they want the job, and one example of relevant work. Do not rush this stage: a week spent finding good candidates saves a month spent fixing a bad hire.</p>
<h3 id="step-5-interview-for-the-work-not-the-cv">Step 5: Interview for the work, not the CV</h3>
<p>A CV tells you what someone has done, not how well they will do your job. Interview for the work itself. Ask the same core questions of every candidate, so you can compare their answers fairly, and take notes during each interview. Use these five questions, and listen for what each one reveals:</p>
<ol>
<li>"Tell me about a time you handled a difficult customer." Listen for calm, clear steps, not blame.</li>
<li>"What would you do in your first week in this job?" Listen for a plan, not a vague promise.</li>
<li>"How do you organise your work when you have five tasks at once?" Listen for a real method, not just "I work hard".</li>
<li>"What is a task in this job description that you have never done before, and how would you learn it?" Listen for honesty and a learning plan, not a claim to know everything.</li>
<li>"Why do you want this job, and not another one like it?" Listen for a real reason, not a scripted answer.</li>
</ol>
<p>Then give the best two or three candidates a paid test project, with clear terms. The project should take 1 to 2 hours, use the real tools they would use, and test the actual job, such as answering three sample customer emails. Pay them for their time, and say so in advance. Check references, including one person the candidate did not list, if you can. Ask the reference what the candidate did well, what they struggled with, and whether they would hire them again. For more on spotting bad actors on either side of a hiring process, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-jobs/how-to-spot-online-job-scams" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to spot online job scams</a>.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/first-employee-job-description.jpg" alt="A person writing a job description on a laptop with a notebook and coffee on the desk" /></p>
<h3 id="step-6-make-the-offer">Step 6: Make the offer</h3>
<p>When you have chosen a candidate, make a written offer. An offer letter should state the pay, the hours, the start date, the main responsibilities, the location or remote arrangement, how performance will be reviewed, and any trial period. Keep the trial period honest: it is a time for both sides to check the fit, not a way to pay less or to avoid the legal duties of employment. Note that trial period rules differ by country and by US state, so check your local rules before you set one. Call the candidate first, then send the offer in writing. A call shows respect, and it lets you answer questions fast. Give them a few days to decide, and do not pressure them. A candidate who feels rushed into the job is more likely to leave in the first month. If the candidate asks for more pay than you offered, check your budget before you agree. A small rise may be worth it for the right person, but do not agree to a salary you cannot afford.</p>
<h3 id="step-7-paperwork-and-onboarding">Step 7: Paperwork and onboarding</h3>
<p>Paperwork matters, and it must be done before the first day of work, not after. In the US, new employees complete a W-4 form and Form I-9, and the employer must report the new hire to the state. Keep employment tax records for at least four years, as the IRS requires. If you are not in the US, check your local rules for the equivalent forms and reporting. Onboarding is the first 4 weeks, and it decides whether your hire stays. Give them one point of contact, which is you, and a clear first-week plan. Day 1 should cover access, tools, and a short tour of the business. Week 1 should cover the main task, with you checking the work each day. Week 2 should add a second task, with checks every other day. Week 3 and week 4 should build independence, with a review at the end of each week. Set expectations in writing on day 1: what good work looks like, how fast replies should be, when you are available, and how to ask for help. Most new hire problems come from unclear expectations, not from laziness.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/first-employee-onboarding.jpg" alt="A new employee working at a desk with a laptop while a colleague points at the screen" /></p>
<h3 id="security-and-access-for-remote-hires">Security and access for remote hires</h3>
<p>A remote hire needs access to your tools, but they do not need access to everything. Give them separate accounts for each tool they use, with only the access they need to do their job. A customer support hire needs access to the inbox and the order system, not to your bank account or your domain registrar. A marketing assistant needs access to the social media scheduler, not to the payment processor. Use strong passwords and two-factor authentication on every account, and do not share your own login. If a tool lets you invite a team member with limited access, use that option. Keep a list of what each person can access, and remove access at once when someone leaves. Do not share passwords during interviews, and do not ask a candidate for access to their own accounts.</p>
<h3 id="when-the-first-hire-does-not-work-out">When the first hire does not work out</h3>
<p>Some first hires do not work out, and that is normal. Watch for early warning signs: missed deadlines, no questions asked when the work is unclear, slow replies, or work that needs constant correction after the first month. Do not ignore these signs and hope they fix themselves. They rarely do. Talk to the hire early, and be specific. Say what you observed, what you expected, and what needs to change, with a clear date to check again. Give them a fair chance to improve, and write down what was agreed. If the work does not improve, end the arrangement professionally. Follow the notice rules in your country and in the offer letter, pay what is owed, remove access promptly, and keep records of the process. A clean ending protects you and is fair to the hire. For more on the freelance side of working with a first client or contractor, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/freelancing/how-to-start-freelancing" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to start freelancing</a> and <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-jobs/how-to-work-as-a-virtual-assistant" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to work as a virtual assistant</a>.</p>
<h3 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Hiring before checking the budget, then cutting the hire's hours within weeks.</li>
<li>Writing a vague job description, so the wrong people apply.</li>
<li>Skipping the paperwork, which creates tax and legal problems later.</li>
<li>Choosing too quickly, because you are desperate for help.</li>
<li>Ignoring onboarding, so the new hire guesses at what good work looks like.</li>
<li>Hiring for everything at once, instead of one clear job.</li>
<li>Giving a new hire access to every account on day one.</li>
<li>Waiting too long to talk about poor work, then ending the hire suddenly.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>How do I hire my first employee?</h4>
<p>Check that you are ready, decide what to delegate, work out the true cost, get the legal basics ready, write a clear job description, find candidates, interview for the work with a paid test project, make a written offer, and onboard them with a four-week plan. Start with one clear job, not everything at once.</p>
<h4>How much does it cost to hire your first employee?</h4>
<p>It depends on the pay, the hours, and your country. In the example in this guide, a part-time employee working 20 hours a week at 15 dollars an hour costs about 1,618 dollars a month, including payroll taxes, tools, and recruitment costs. Work out your own cost from your local rates, and check that your revenue covers it for at least 3 months.</p>
<h4>Do I need an EIN to hire an employee?</h4>
<p>In the US, yes. An EIN is a federal tax ID number, and the IRS says you need one if you have employees. You can get one for free directly from the IRS in minutes, and you can apply online. Outside the US, the rules differ, so check your local tax authority for the equivalent ID.</p>
<h4>Should I hire a full-time or part-time employee first?</h4>
<p>Most solo owners should start part-time, with 10 to 20 hours a week for one clear job. Part-time costs less, it is easier to afford, and it lets you test the role before you commit to full-time. Hire full-time when the job needs 30 or more hours a week, and the revenue covers the full cost.</p>
<h4>Where can I find employees for my small business?</h4>
<p>Post on your own site and social media accounts first, then use job boards and LinkedIn, and ask people you know for referrals. Online business owner groups can also help, but check that a group is active and well run before you post there. Do not rush this stage, because a week spent finding good candidates saves a month spent fixing a bad hire.</p>
<h4>What do I do if my first hire does not work out?</h4>
<p>Talk to them early, be specific about what needs to change, and give them a fair chance to improve, with a clear date to check again. If the work does not improve, end the arrangement professionally, following the notice rules in your country and in the offer letter, paying what is owed, and removing access promptly.</p>
<h3 id="conclusion-hire-for-one-clear-job-and-onboard-well">Conclusion: hire for one clear job, and onboard well</h3>
<p>Hiring your first employee for an online business comes down to four moves: check that you are ready and that the business can afford the true cost, pick one clear job to delegate, choose the route that fits your control and your budget, and onboard the new hire with a four-week plan and clear expectations. Do not hire on hope, do not skip the paperwork, and do not give a new hire access to everything on day one. Your next step is to do the one-week task audit from this guide, and to write down the one job you would delegate first. When you are ready to plan the wider business, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-business/how-to-automate-online-business" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to automate an online business</a>, study <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-business/how-to-scale-small-online-business" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to scale a small online business</a>, and learn <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-business/how-to-get-first-customers-online-business" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to get your first customers for an online business</a>. You can also browse all the guides in the <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-business" rel="noopener" target="_blank">online business category</a>.</p>
