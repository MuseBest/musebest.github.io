30:T4d56,<h1>How to Start an Online Course Business in 2026</h1>
<p>To start an online course business, you need a topic people will pay to learn, a course that gets them a clear result, and a way to reach buyers. Many beginners record a long course first and then wonder why nobody buys it, but the order should be the other way round: validate the idea, pre-sell it, then record it. This guide walks you through topic choice, validation, planning, recording, platforms, pricing and launch, so you build a course people actually want.</p>

<h2 id="step-1-pick-a-topic-people-will-pay-to-learn">Step 1: Pick a topic people will pay to learn</h2>
<p>A course business starts with a transformation, which is the clear change a student gets by the end of the course. People do not pay for information, because information is free. They pay for a result, such as passing an exam, getting a first client, cooking a meal or finishing a project. Write your topic as one sentence that names the result, such as "Learn to build a simple budget and stick to it for three months."</p>
<p>Pick a topic you know well enough to teach, and that you can talk about for hours without notes. Your best topic sits where three things meet: a skill you have, a problem people ask you about, and a group of people who can pay. If you have taught the skill before, even informally, that is a strong sign. If nobody has ever asked you about it, test the idea before you build anything.</p>
<h2 id="step-2-validate-before-you-record">Step 2: Validate before you record</h2>
<p>Validation means checking that people will pay before you spend weeks recording. The simplest validation is a pre-sale, which means offering the course for sale before it is finished, at a lower early price, and seeing whether anyone buys. If ten people buy, you have proof. If nobody buys, you have saved weeks of work and you can change the topic or the promise.</p>

Add a worksheet or a checklist to each module, because students who do the work get results, and students who get results leave good reviews and recommend the course.</p>
<h2 id="choose-a-format-that-fits-your-audience">Choose a format that fits your audience</h2>
<p>The format decides how students learn and how much work you do. A self paced course is recorded once and sold many times, which suits a teacher who wants to earn while they sleep, but it needs strong marketing, because there is no live event to push sales. A cohort course runs live with a group on fixed dates, which suits a teacher who likes live teaching and wants fast feedback, but it takes more of your time each time you run it.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Self paced:</strong> students watch recorded lessons when they like, which suits teachers who want to sell the same course many times.</li>
<li><strong>Cohort based:</strong> a group starts together and meets live each week, which suits teachers who want community and fast feedback.</li>
<li><strong>Mini course:</strong> a short course of 1 to 2 hours on one narrow skill, which suits a first course or a low price entry product.</li>
<li><strong>Live workshop:</strong> a single live session, often 60 to 90 minutes, which suits testing a topic before building a full course.</li>
</ul>

Check the current prices before you choose, because platform pricing changes often.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-record-the-course-properly">Step 5: Record the course properly</h2>
<p>Good audio matters more than good video. Students will forgive a plain background, but they will quit a course with echo, hiss or a quiet voice. Buy a decent USB microphone, record in a quiet room with soft furnishings, and test the sound with a short recording before you film the whole course. Speak slowly, pause between ideas and smile when you talk, because it shows in your voice.</p>
<p>Add captions to every lesson. Captions are a text version of the spoken audio, and they help students who are deaf, students who watch without sound and students who learn better by reading. The World Wide Web Consortium explains <a href="https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how captions work and why they matter</a>, and most course platforms can generate a first draft of captions that you should check and correct, because automatic captions often get words wrong. A transcript, which is the full written text of a lesson, is also worth adding, because it helps students search the course and it helps search engines understand what you teach.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-set-up-the-business-taxes-and-refunds">Step 6: Set up the business, taxes and refunds</h2>
<p>Register the business in the same way as any other online business, and speak to an accountant about how course income is taxed in your country. In the United States, the Internal Revenue Service explains in its <a href="https://www.irs.gov/businesses/gig-economy-tax-center" rel="noopener" target="_blank">gig economy tax center</a> that income earned from online work, including selling courses, must be reported on a tax return, even if no tax form is sent to you. Rules differ by country, so check your local tax authority. See also our <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-business/how-to-start-an-online-tutoring-business" rel="noopener" target="_blank">tutoring guide</a> for a live teaching business.</p>
<p>Tax on digital goods is a real issue, because some countries charge sales tax or VAT on online courses, and the rules depend on where the student lives, not where you live. Many course platforms handle this tax for you, but check whether yours does, and keep records of every sale, including the student's country, so you can prove what you charged and why.</p>
<p>Write a clear refund policy before you launch, and put it on the sales page. A common policy is a 14 or 30 day refund for students who have watched less than a set share of the course. A fair refund policy builds trust and cuts complaints, and it protects you from students who buy, download everything and then ask for their money back. Also write short terms that explain what students may do with the course materials, because piracy, which is copying and sharing your course without permission, is a real risk. Watermark your videos where the platform allows it, and do not send downloadable video files if you can avoid it.</p>
<h2 id="step-7-price-the-course">Step 7: Price the course</h2>
<p>Pricing a course is a guess until you have sales, so start with a simple method and test it. Look at what similar courses charge, then price yours in the same range, not far above or far below. A course that is much cheaper than rivals looks low quality, and a course that is much dearer needs strong proof to justify it.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>27 to 49 dollars:</strong> a mini course or a first course, with 1 to 2 hours of lessons and worksheets.</li>
<li><strong>99 to 199 dollars:</strong> a full course on one clear skill, with 3 to 6 hours of lessons, worksheets and support.</li>
<li><strong>299 to 499 dollars:</strong> a deep course with live help, including the full course, live calls, community and feedback.</li>
<li><strong>500 dollars and above:</strong> a cohort course or a professional skill, with live teaching, coaching and a certificate.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="step-8-launch-in-the-first-60-days">Step 8: Launch in the first 60 days</h2>
<p>A realistic first 60 days looks like this. Days 1 to 14: validate the topic, run a workshop and build a waiting list. Days 15 to 30: write the outline, record the first half of the lessons and set up the platform. Days 31 to 45: finish recording, add captions and worksheets, and test the whole course as a student. Days 46 to 60: run the beta launch, fix problems, then open public sales with an early price for one week.</p>

<h2 id="market-the-course-and-keep-students-finishing">Market the course and keep students finishing</h2>
<p>A course that nobody knows about will not sell, so marketing is part of the business, not an afterthought. The most reliable channel is email. Build an email list by offering a free lesson or a free checklist in exchange for an email address, and send one useful email a week that teaches something small and links to the course. If you want to learn list building, read our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/blogging/how-to-build-email-list-for-a-blog" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to build an email list for a blog</a>.</p>
<h2 id="worked-example-what-a-small-course-business-could-earn">Worked example: what a small course business could earn</h2>
<p>Example only: these numbers are not typical results, and they are shown only to explain how the maths works.</p>
<p>Imagine a teacher who sells a budgeting course for 99 dollars. In a month, 40 students buy the course. The revenue is 40 multiplied by 99, which is 3,960 dollars. The platform takes no transaction fee on the plan she uses, but the payment provider takes 3 percent, which is 119 dollars, so the revenue after payment fees is 3,841 dollars.</p>
<p>Her monthly costs are 89 dollars for the course platform, 30 dollars for email software, 20 dollars for the website and 100 dollars for ads. That is 239 dollars in total. The profit before tax is 3,841 minus 239, which is 3,602 dollars.</p>
<p>That example assumes 40 sales a month, which takes many months to build. In the first months, the same teacher might sell 4 courses a month, which is 396 dollars of revenue against the same 239 dollars of costs. The lesson is that a course business earns little at first and grows as the email list and the reviews build up, so plan your finances for a slow start.</p>

<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Recording a long course before checking whether anyone will pay for it.</li>
<li>Picking a topic that is too broad, which makes the course hard to sell and hard to finish.</li>
<li>Recording lessons that are too long, which makes students quit halfway through.</li>
<li>Skipping captions and transcripts, which shuts out students who need them.</li>
<li>Pricing far below rival courses, which makes the course look low quality.</li>
<li>Launching without an email list, which leaves you with nobody to sell to on launch day.</li>
<li>Ignoring tax rules on digital goods, which can cause problems later.</li>
<li>Never updating the course, which lets it go stale while rivals improve theirs.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="risks-and-scams-to-watch-for">Risks and scams to watch for</h2>
<p>Watch for companies that promise to build and sell your course for a large upfront fee, or that guarantee a fixed number of sales. Legitimate platforms charge a monthly fee or a share of sales, and they do not guarantee results. Read every contract before you sign, and never pay a large sum before you see how the service works.</p>
<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 id="how-do-i-start-an-online-course-business">How do I start an online course business?</h3>
<p>Pick a topic people will pay to learn, validate it with a workshop or a pre-sale, plan a short outline with a clear result, record it with good audio, choose a platform such as Teachable, then launch to an email list with an early price. Do not record the full course before you have proof that people want it.</p>
<h3 id="how-much-money-can-you-make-selling-online-courses">How much money can you make selling online courses?</h3>
<p>It varies widely, because it depends on your topic, your price, your audience size and how many students finish and recommend the course. Most new course businesses earn little in the first months and grow slowly as the email list and reviews build up, so plan for a slow start and do not count on fast income.</p>
<h3 id="do-i-need-to-be-an-expert-to-sell-a-course">Do I need to be an expert to sell a course?</h3>
<p>You need to know the topic well enough to teach it clearly and to answer student questions, but you do not need a degree or a formal title. Many successful course teachers are practitioners who learned by doing, and students care more about a clear result than about credentials.</p>
<h3 id="how-long-should-an-online-course-be">How long should an online course be?</h3>
<p>Long enough to get the student to the promised result, and no longer. Most good first courses run for 2 to 6 hours of lessons, split into short lessons of 5 to 10 minutes each, with a worksheet or a task after each module.</p>
<h3 id="should-i-sell-my-course-on-udemy-or-host-it-myself">Should I sell my course on Udemy or host it myself?</h3>
<p>Udemy brings students without marketing, but it controls your price and takes a large share of each sale. Hosting it yourself on a platform such as Teachable gives you control over price and branding, but you must bring your own students. Many teachers start on a standalone platform and add a marketplace later.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>To start an online course business, pick a narrow topic with a clear result, validate it with a workshop or a pre-sale, plan a short outline with a task after each module, record it with good audio and captions, then launch to an email list with an early price and keep students finishing with support and a community. Marketing and updates are part of the business, not an afterthought.</p>
