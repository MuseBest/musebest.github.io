How to Start an App Development Agency Online in 2026

To start an app development agency online, you need a niche clients will pay for, proof that you can build good apps, and a clear way to price and deliver projects. Many beginners build a website first and then wonder why nobody calls, but the order should be the other way round: pick a niche, build proof, then find clients through warm outreach and partnerships. This guide walks you through niche choice, proof, costs, contracts, delivery, pricing and a realistic first year, so you can launch with work to show, not just a website.

Step 1: Pick a niche that pays

An app development agency is a business that builds mobile apps for clients, usually for a fixed project fee plus ongoing maintenance. The agency does not own the app. The client does, and the agency is paid to design, build, test and launch it. The niche you choose decides who your clients are, how much they can pay and how hard it is to win them.

Niche Who the client is What the app usually does Why it pays
Restaurants and cafes Owners with one to five sites Ordering, loyalty, menus Clear return from more orders
Gyms and studios Owners with regular members Booking, payments, class timetables Members pay monthly, so the app earns its cost
Clinics and health Practice managers Appointments, reminders, patient forms Saves staff time every day
Small business tools Owners with a manual process Scheduling, job tracking, quotes Replaces spreadsheets and phone calls
Startup MVPs Founders with funding A first version to test with users Funded founders can pay for speed

Pick one niche and learn its language. A restaurant owner cares about orders and table turnover, not about code. A clinic manager cares about missed appointments, not about frameworks. Write your website for that one client, and say clearly who the agency is for and who it is not for.

Step 2: Choose your technical approach

Your technical approach decides how you build apps, how fast you deliver and how much each project costs. The two main choices are native and cross platform. A native app is built separately for each system, using Swift for iPhone and iPad, and Kotlin for Android, which gives the best performance and the most access to phone features, but it costs more because you build the app twice. A cross platform app is built once and runs on both systems, using a framework such as React Native or Flutter, which is faster and cheaper, but it can struggle with very complex features.

Flutter is a framework from Google that lets you build apps for mobile, web and desktop from one codebase, and it is a strong choice for a new agency because one team can serve both iPhone and Android clients. React Native is a similar framework from Meta, and it is also widely used.

Step 3: Get proof before you pitch

Proof is the work you can show a client, and it matters more than a website. Clients buy apps from agencies they trust, and trust comes from seeing real work, not from reading claims. Before you pitch to a paying client, build two or three proof apps that show what you can do.

Step 4: Register the business, contracts and insurance

Register the business in the same way as any other online business. A limited company is a common choice for an agency because it separates your personal money from the business, and clients and partners take a registered company more seriously than a sole trader. Check the registration rules in your own country, and speak to an accountant about how project income and retainers are taxed, because the rules can differ for work that spans several months.

Contracts are essential in agency work, and a good contract protects both you and the client. Your contract should cover the scope, which is a clear list of what the app will and will not do, the price and the payment schedule, who owns the code and the design, how changes are handled, what happens if the client delays, and how support works after launch. Get a qualified lawyer to review your contract template before you use it, because a weak contract is the fastest route to an unpaid invoice or a dispute. See our SEO agency guide for the same launch steps. For a plain language guide to contracts, read our freelance contracts explained guide.

Professional indemnity insurance, often called errors and omissions insurance, pays if a client claims your work caused them a loss, such as a broken app that lost them sales. Get quotes from two or three insurers who cover software agencies, and check the cover limit, the excess and whether the policy covers the type of apps you build. Do not start a project until the policy is in place.

Step 5: Set up delivery, tools and testing

Delivery is the process you follow to build an app, from first meeting to launch, and a clear process is what separates a professional agency from a freelancer who wings it. Write your process down in five stages, and share it with every client before you start. See our hosting guide for a service business that also depends on reliable delivery.

First, discovery, which is a paid stage where you learn what the client needs, who the users are and what the app must do. Charge for discovery, because it is real work, and it stops clients from asking for free plans. Second, design, where you make wireframes and screens that show how the app will look and work. Third, build, where you write the code in short cycles and show the client progress each week. Fourth, testing, where you check the app on real phones and fix bugs. Fifth, launch, where you submit the app to the stores and help the client through review.

Step 6: Price projects properly

Pricing is where many new agencies fail, because they charge by the hour and then work for less than minimum wage once they count the admin time. Price by value, which means charging for the result the app gives the client, not for the hours you spend. An app that saves a clinic ten hours of staff time a week is worth far more than the hours it took to build.

Project size What it usually includes Price guide
Small app A simple app with a few screens and no payments Lower project fee
Medium app Booking, payments, user accounts and admin tools Mid project fee
Large app Custom features, integrations and a web dashboard Higher project fee
Ongoing retainer Monthly updates, fixes and small improvements Monthly fee after launch

Use a milestone payment structure, which means the client pays in parts as the work progresses. A common split is 30 percent at the start, 20 percent when the design is approved, 30 percent when the app is built and tested, and 20 percent at launch. This structure protects your cash flow and keeps the client engaged, because they pay as they see progress. Do not start work without a deposit, and do not hand over the final app or the store accounts until the final payment is made.

Add a maintenance retainer to every project, which is a monthly fee for updates, bug fixes and small improvements after launch. A retainer turns a one off project into recurring income, and it keeps the app working as phones and store rules change. Offer the retainer in the proposal, not as an afterthought, and explain what it covers.

Step 7: Build your website and sales materials

Your website has one job: to turn a visitor from your niche into a booked call. The home page should say who you build apps for, show your proof apps with screenshots, and link to a booking page. A services page should explain your process, your pricing approach and what is included in a project. A case study page should tell the story of each proof app in plain language, with screenshots and a quote from the client.

Step 8: Find your first clients

Your first clients will come from warm outreach and partnerships, not from search alone, because a new agency site takes months to rank. Warm outreach means contacting people you already know, or people who know someone you know, with a short, specific message about the app you can build for them. Do not send a generic message to hundreds of strangers. Send a short message to ten people a week, each one written for that person, and mention a specific problem their business has that an app could solve.

Handle app store submission and rejection

Every app you build must pass a review before it appears in the Apple App Store or Google Play, and rejections are normal, especially for a first submission. Apple publishes its App Store Review Guidelines, which explain the rules on safety, performance, business, design and legal issues, and Google publishes its Developer Policy Center, which covers restricted content, privacy, impersonation and spam. Read both before you build, because an app that breaks the rules will be rejected and the fix can take weeks.

Grow with retainers and your first hires

Once you have three or four clients, growth comes from retainers and referrals, not from more cold outreach. A retainer is a monthly fee for updates, fixes and small improvements, and it turns a one off project into steady income. Offer every client a retainer at launch, and explain what it covers, such as a set number of hours, priority fixes and a monthly report. Clients who pay a retainer stay longer, and they recommend you more often, because you are part of their team.

Worked example: what a small app agency could earn

Example only: these numbers are not typical results, and they are shown only to explain how the maths works.

Imagine a small agency that completes one medium app project every two months, priced at 12,000 dollars. The project is paid in milestones: 30 percent at the start, which is 3,600 dollars, 20 percent at design approval, which is 2,400 dollars, 30 percent at build completion, which is 3,600 dollars, and 20 percent at launch, which is 2,400 dollars. The total is 12,000 dollars per project.

The agency also has three clients on a maintenance retainer of 500 dollars a month each, which is 1,500 dollars a month. In a month when a project launches, the revenue is 2,400 dollars from the final milestone plus 1,500 dollars from retainers, which is 3,900 dollars.

Monthly costs are 300 dollars for software and tools, 150 dollars for insurance spread across the month, 50 dollars for the website and email, and 500 dollars for a part time tester. That is 1,000 dollars in total. The profit before tax in that month is 3,900 minus 1,000, which is 2,900 dollars.

That example assumes a steady flow of projects and retainers, which takes a year or more to build. In the first months, the same agency might have one project and no retainers, which is far less revenue against the same fixed costs. The lesson is that agency income is lumpy, because projects pay in milestones, so a retainer base is what smooths the income between projects.

How to start an app development agency online: what it costs and what to expect

A lean launch is realistic, because you can start alone with a laptop and the tools you already use. The table below shows typical ranges to check before you budget. Prices vary by provider and by country, so confirm each one before you spend.

Item Typical range Notes
Business registration 50 to 500 dollars Depends on country and structure
Professional indemnity insurance 500 to 2,000 dollars per year Get quotes before you take a client
Apple Developer and Google Play accounts 100 to 150 dollars per year Usually paid by the client
Design and project tools 30 to 100 dollars per month Figma, project tool and code hosting
Website, domain and email 100 to 400 dollars per year Keep it simple at the start
Testing devices 0 to 500 dollars Use phones you already own first
Marketing 0 to 200 dollars per month Warm outreach first, ads later

Expect the first year to be slow. A realistic plan is to spend the first two months on proof apps and registration, the next three months on outreach and your first paying client, and the rest of the year on delivery, retainers and referrals. Keep another income source until the agency can support you.

Common mistakes

  • Building a website before you have proof apps to show.
  • Taking every project, which leaves you with no niche and no referrals.
  • Charging by the hour, which punishes you for working fast and well.
  • Starting work without a deposit, which leaves you unpaid when a client goes quiet.
  • Skipping testing on real phones, which leads to bugs and bad reviews.
  • Letting the client change the scope for free, which is called scope creep and eats your profit.
  • Hiding the app store review rules from the client, which makes a rejection feel like your fault.
  • Hiring a full team before you have steady work to pay for it.

Risks and scams to watch for

The biggest risk is scope creep, which is when a client keeps adding features without paying for them. A clear contract with a written scope and a change process is your main protection. When a client asks for a change, write down what it costs and how long it takes, and get their agreement in writing before you build it.

Frequently asked questions

How do I start an app development agency?

Pick one niche, choose one technical approach, build two or three proof apps, register the business, write a contract template and get insurance, then find clients through warm outreach, LinkedIn and partnerships. Do not build a website before you have proof to show.

How much do app development agencies charge?

It varies widely, because it depends on the app size, the features and the client. Small apps often cost a few thousand dollars, medium apps often cost ten to thirty thousand dollars, and large apps can cost far more, but these are broad ranges, not quotes. Always give a written quote based on the client's exact scope.

Do I need to know how to code to start an app agency?

You need to understand how apps are built well enough to price, plan and check the work, but you do not need to write every line of code yourself. Many agency founders hire developers and focus on sales, clients and delivery, but a founder who cannot read code at all will struggle to judge quality and time.

How do app agencies get clients?

The first clients usually come from warm outreach, referrals, LinkedIn and partnerships with web designers and marketing agencies, not from search. Proof apps, case studies and a clear niche matter more than ads at the start, because clients buy trust before they buy code.

How profitable is an app development agency?

It can be profitable once you have steady projects and retainers, but income is lumpy, because projects pay in milestones and gaps between projects are common. Retainers smooth the income, and a narrow niche lets you charge more and deliver faster, but most new agencies take a year or more to reach a steady profit.

Conclusion

To start an app development agency online, pick one niche that pays, choose one technical approach, build two or three proof apps before you pitch, then register the business, write a clear contract and get insurance. Price by value with milestone payments, follow a five stage delivery process with real device testing, and let warm outreach, LinkedIn and partnerships bring your first clients while retainers smooth your income.
