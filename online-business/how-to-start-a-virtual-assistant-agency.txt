<h2 id="what-a-virtual-assistant-agency-is-and-what-it-is-not">What a virtual assistant agency is (and what it is not)</h2>
<p>A virtual assistant agency is a small firm that sells remote admin support to other businesses. Clients pay the agency a monthly fee, and the agency assigns one or more assistants to do the work. Common tasks include inbox management, calendar and travel booking, data entry, customer support, social media scheduling, and basic bookkeeping support.</p>
<p>This is different from working as a solo virtual assistant. A solo assistant sells their own hours. An agency owner sells a service that other people deliver, and the owner spends their time on sales, quality checks, and running the team. If you enjoy doing the admin work yourself and do not want to manage people, the solo route suits you better. Our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-jobs/how-to-work-as-a-virtual-assistant" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to work as a virtual assistant</a> covers that path in detail.</p>
<h2 id="step-1-choose-a-niche-you-can-serve-well">Step 1: Choose a niche you can serve well</h2>
<p>A niche is one type of client plus one clear problem you solve. "We do admin for anyone" is hard to sell, because no buyer feels it is meant for them. "We handle inbox, calendar, and travel for real estate agents" is easy to understand, easy to buy, and easy to staff, because every client needs similar work.</p>
<p>Good first niches include real estate agents, podcasters, e-commerce store owners, coaches and consultants, and small law or accounting firms. Pick one where you already understand the daily work, or where you can learn it fast. Ask yourself three questions before you commit:</p>
<ul>
<li>Can I name 30 businesses in this niche that I could contact this month?</li>
<li>Do these businesses already pay for help, or would I have to convince them the problem exists?</li>
<li>Can I explain their most common tasks without guessing?</li>
</ul>
<p>If you cannot name 30 buyers, the niche is too small or too hard to reach. If buyers do not already pay for help, you will spend your time educating the market instead of selling. A narrow niche also makes hiring easier, because one set of standard procedures covers most clients.</p>
<h2 id="step-2-define-your-services-and-packages">Step 2: Define your services and packages</h2>
<p>Clients buy packages more easily than open-ended hours. A package states what is included, how much it costs, and what happens when the work runs over. Start with two or three packages and add more only when clients ask for them.</p>

<p>Keep the list of services short at first. Inbox and calendar work, travel booking, data entry, basic customer support, and social media scheduling cover most early clients. Avoid promising specialist work you cannot staff, such as legal admin or medical admin, until you have an assistant who is trained for it. A clear scope also protects your margin, because extra tasks outside the package are charged separately instead of being absorbed.</p>

<h2 id="step-3-write-a-one-page-business-plan">Step 3: Write a one page business plan</h2>
<p>You do not need a 40 page plan. One page forces you to decide before you spend money. Write these six lines and keep the page where you can see it:</p>
<ol>
<li>Niche: the audience and the service, in one sentence.</li>
<li>Offer: your two or three packages, with prices.</li>
<li>Costs: your monthly software, insurance, and admin costs.</li>
<li>Break-even: how many retainer clients you need to cover those costs.</li>
<li>First clients: the 30 businesses you will contact first, and how.</li>
<li>First hire: the point at which you bring in your first assistant, such as when a second client signs.</li>
</ol>
<p>That one page is the core of how to start a virtual assistant agency without guessing at prices or hiring too early. The plan also needs a simple cash flow note. Clients often pay monthly in arrears, while you pay assistants weekly or monthly, so keep at least one month of assistant pay in reserve before you take on a second client. If the numbers do not work on paper, they will not work in practice, so fix the prices before you sell.</p>
<h2 id="step-4-set-up-the-business-legally">Step 4: Set up the business legally</h2>
<p>Set up the business before your first client pays you. The exact steps differ by country, so use this list as a checklist to take to a local professional, not as legal advice.</p>
<p>Choose a business name and check that the domain and the social handles are free. Pick a name that sounds like a firm rather than a single freelancer, because larger clients look for capacity. Then choose a business structure. In the United States, the common choices are a sole proprietorship or a limited liability company, known as an LLC. The US Small Business Administration explains <a href="https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/choose-business-structure" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to choose a business structure</a> and the trade-offs of each option. An LLC separates your personal assets from the business, which matters once other people do client work under your name.</p>
<p>Register the business name if your local rules require it, and check whether you need a general business licence from your city or county. If you are in the United States and you form an LLC, you can apply for an employer identification number, known as an EIN, free from the IRS. The IRS explains <a href="https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/apply-for-an-employer-identification-number-ein-online" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to apply for an EIN online</a> and warns that you never have to pay a fee for one. Open a separate business bank account on day one, and keep client money and your own money apart. Get professional liability insurance and ask the insurer specifically about work done by contractors on your behalf.</p>
<p>Write three documents before your first client: a service agreement that states the scope, the price, the payment terms, and how either side can end the work; a contractor agreement for each assistant, covering pay, confidentiality, who owns the work, and data handling; and an invoice template. A local accountant can tell you which tax registrations apply where you live.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-price-your-services-so-the-margin-works">Step 5: Price your services so the margin works</h2>
<p>An agency earns the gap between what the client pays and what the assistant costs, after software and admin. If that gap is too thin, every new client adds stress instead of profit. Work out the margin before you quote, not after.</p>
<p><strong>Example only: these numbers are not typical results.</strong> Suppose you sell a 20 hour monthly retainer for 800 US dollars. You pay the assistant 20 US dollars per hour, which is 400 dollars for the month. Software for that client costs about 25 dollars a month. Your gross profit is 800 minus 400 minus 25, which is 375 dollars, or about 47 percent of the price. Your own time for sales, quality checks, and client calls still comes out of that 375 dollars, so the real margin is thinner than it looks.</p>
<p>Run the same maths for every package. If the margin falls below about 30 percent after software, raise the price, narrow the scope, or find a cheaper way to deliver. Retainers beat hourly billing for agencies, because the income is predictable and you can book assistant hours in advance. Avoid charging per hour for ongoing work, because efficient assistants then earn you less, which is the wrong incentive. Review prices every six months, and raise them when your costs rise or your waiting list grows.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-hire-and-vet-your-first-virtual-assistants">Step 6: Hire and vet your first virtual assistants</h2>
<p>Do not hire a team before you have clients. Hire one assistant when the work is there, then a second when the first is full. A bench of vetted freelancers you can call on is better than employees you cannot keep busy.</p>
<p>Look for assistants in niche communities, on VA job boards, on LinkedIn, and through referrals from assistants you already trust. Skill matters, but reliability matters more: clients forgive a slow reply, they do not forgive a missed deadline or a lost email. Vet every assistant with a short paid trial that matches real client work, with a brief, a deadline, and your quality checklist. Judge accuracy, how well they followed the brief, communication speed, and how they took feedback.</p>
<p>Agree the terms in writing before any client work starts: the hourly or monthly rate, how hours are tracked, payment timing, confidentiality, data handling rules, and who owns the finished work. Decide whether your assistants are contractors or employees, because the tax and legal rules differ, and ask a local professional if you are unsure. Our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-business/how-to-hire-your-first-employee-for-an-online-business" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to hire your first employee for an online business</a> explains the first hire decision in more detail.</p>
<p>Plan for turnover from the start. Assistants leave, get sick, or take other work, so keep a backup assistant briefed on each client and keep the procedures written down. If only one person knows how a client works, your agency has a single point of failure.</p>
<h2 id="step-7-build-your-tools-and-standard-procedures">Step 7: Build your tools and standard procedures</h2>
<p>An agency runs on systems, not memory. Choose simple tools that cover five jobs: communication, task tracking, time tracking, file sharing, and passwords. A common starter stack is Slack or Microsoft Teams for chat, Asana or Trello for tasks, Toggl or Clockify for time tracking, Google Drive for files, and 1Password or LastPass for sharing client logins safely. Never share a client password in plain text in an email or chat.</p>
<p>Write your standard procedures early, even if the first version is one page per task. At minimum, document:</p>
<ul>
<li>How an inbox is triaged, and what counts as urgent.</li>
<li>How a calendar booking is confirmed and what to do about clashes.</li>
<li>How a client is onboarded, including logins, contacts, and preferences.</li>
<li>How work is checked before it goes to the client.</li>
<li>What happens when an assistant is sick or leaves.</li>
</ul>
<p>A short quality checklist for every piece of client work keeps standards steady from assistant to assistant. Review the procedures each month and fix the one that caused the most mistakes. That is how a small agency gets more reliable instead of just busier.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/virtual-assistant-agency-onboarding.jpg" alt="Two people on a friendly video call, with a welcome pack and folder on the desk" /></p>
<h2 id="step-8-find-your-first-clients">Step 8: Find your first clients</h2>
<p>Your first clients usually come from people you already know and from direct outreach, not from waiting for search traffic. A practical first 30 days looks like this:</p>
<ul>
<li>Week 1: list 50 businesses in your niche, tell your network exactly who you serve, and ask for introductions.</li>
<li>Week 2: send short, personal emails to the first 25. Mention one specific admin pain you noticed, such as a slow reply to enquiries, and offer a short call.</li>
<li>Week 3: offer a small paid trial, such as one week of inbox management, or a free audit of how a business handles enquiries. This lowers the risk for a hesitant buyer.</li>
<li>Week 4: follow up with everyone who replied, contact the remaining 25, and ask trial clients whether they want to move to a retainer.</li>
</ul>
<p>LinkedIn is the strongest channel for this kind of agency, because your buyers are there and you can reach them by name. Post short, useful notes about delegation and admin problems in your niche, and comment helpfully on posts by your target clients before you pitch them. Referral partnerships also work well: business coaches, accountants, and web designers meet founders who need admin help, and a referral fee for the first three months of a contract keeps the relationship warm.</p>
<p>On your website, show the packages, the prices or at least starting prices, and a simple way to book a call. A short case note about a real result, with the client permission, beats a page of adjectives. For more on winning early buyers, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/online-business/how-to-get-first-customers-online-business" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to get your first customers for an online business</a>.</p>

<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Hiring a team before you have clients, then paying people with no work to give them.</li>
<li>Pricing from the assistant rate alone and forgetting software, admin, and your own time.</li>
<li>Taking any client in any niche, which makes every job different and nothing repeatable.</li>
<li>Skipping written contracts, then arguing about scope when a client asks for extra work.</li>
<li>Letting one assistant hold all the knowledge about a client, with no backup and no written procedure.</li>
<li>Doing all the quality checks yourself forever, so the agency stops growing when your week fills up.</li>
<li>Promising a client a result you cannot control, such as a reply rate or a sales number.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="risks-and-scams-to-watch-for">Risks and scams to watch for</h2>
<p>You handle client inboxes, passwords, and customer data, so security is part of the product. Use a password manager, give each assistant access only to what they need, and remove access the day someone leaves. Tell clients how you store and protect their data, and put it in the service agreement.</p>
<p>Watch for clients who ask your assistants to do work that breaks platform rules, such as sending bulk spam or writing fake reviews. Refuse those jobs, because the risk lands on your agency. Watch for payment risk too: take a deposit or payment in advance for the first month, and stop work politely if an invoice goes unpaid. Check the local rules on data protection where your clients live, because rules differ by country, and ask a qualified professional when you are unsure.</p>
<p>Be wary of anyone selling a "done for you agency" package that promises clients on autopilot for a large upfront fee. No course can guarantee clients, and the honest agencies grow through outreach, referrals, and steady delivery.</p>
<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 id="how-much-does-it-cost-to-start-a-virtual-assistant-agency">How much does it cost to start a virtual assistant agency?</h3>
<p>A lean start usually costs under 2,000 US dollars if you already own a computer. The main costs are business registration, a website, software, contract templates, and insurance. Assistants are paid only when client work exists, so they are a delivery cost rather than a startup cost.</p>
<h3 id="do-i-need-experience-as-a-virtual-assistant-first">Do I need experience as a virtual assistant first?</h3>
<p>It helps a lot, because you need to judge good work and write procedures for it. If you have never done admin work for a client, start by taking on two or three clients yourself, learn the tasks properly, and then hire your first assistant once you can train someone else to do them.</p>
<h3 id="how-do-i-find-my-first-virtual-assistant-agency-clients">How do I find my first virtual assistant agency clients?</h3>
<p>Start with your own network and direct outreach to 50 businesses in one niche. Offer a small paid trial or a free audit of how they handle enquiries, because that shows your value before asking for a retainer. LinkedIn and referral partners such as business coaches are the strongest channels for most new agencies.</p>
<h3 id="should-my-assistants-be-employees-or-contractors">Should my assistants be employees or contractors?</h3>
<p>It depends on your country and on how much control you have over their hours and methods. Contractors are the usual starting point for small agencies, because you pay for the work delivered. Employees bring more control but also payroll, tax, and legal duties. Ask a local accountant before you decide, because getting this wrong is expensive to fix.</p>
<h3 id="how-many-clients-do-i-need-to-make-a-virtual-assistant-agency-work">How many clients do I need to make a virtual assistant agency work?</h3>
<p>That depends on your prices and your costs. As a rough guide, three retainer clients at 800 US dollars a month bring in 2,400 dollars of monthly revenue, and after assistant pay and software you may keep around 1,000 to 1,200 dollars before your own time and tax. Work out your own break-even number in your one page plan, and do not hire ahead of it.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Learning how to start a virtual assistant agency comes down to a few decisions made in order. Pick one niche, package two or three services, check the margin before you quote, set the business up properly, and hire one assistant at a time as the work arrives. Keep quality steady with written procedures and a backup for every client, and find your first clients through your network and direct outreach.</p>
