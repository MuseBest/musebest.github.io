Learn how to start a web design business online step by step: pick a niche, build a portfolio, price your work, and win your first clients online.

Learning how to start a web design business online is a practical first business for someone who enjoys making websites and helping small businesses look professional. You do not need a degree, and you do not need to code everything from scratch. You need a niche, a portfolio, a clear price, and a way to find clients. This guide shows you each step, with honest costs, honest timelines, and no income promises.

What a web design business does

A web design business plans, designs, and builds websites for other businesses. Clients pay for more than a pretty page. They pay for a site that explains their offer clearly, loads fast, works on a phone, and turns visitors into enquiries or sales. That means your work covers layout, colours, fonts, images, words, forms, and the basic search settings that help people find the site.

Clients also pay for the parts they cannot see. A good designer asks what the business needs the site to do, organises the pages in a clear order, writes headings a visitor can scan, and sets up contact forms, maps, and booking links that work. The design is the visible part. The thinking behind it is the valuable part.

Your clients will mostly be small businesses: cafes, clinics, coaches, photographers, tradespeople, and online shops. They hire a designer because they lack the time or the skill to build a site themselves, and because a weak site costs them customers. Your job is to deliver a site that works, on time, with clear communication.

Step 1: Choose a niche

A niche is a type of client you serve again and again. "Web design for anyone" is not a niche. "Websites for photographers" is a niche. "Websites for dental clinics" is a niche. "Online stores for skincare brands" is a niche. A niche makes your work faster, because each project looks like the last one, and it makes your marketing clearer, because your message speaks to one kind of buyer.

Use a short niche pick test. Answer these four questions honestly:

  1. Do I genuinely enjoy this type of business, enough to build 10 sites for it?
  2. Do the owners have money to spend on a website?
  3. Do their current websites look weak, so my work would clearly help?
  4. Can I reach them, locally or online, by name?

A niche that scores yes on all four is a good start. Common first niches are photographers, coaches, wellness businesses, restaurants, and local trades. Each has clear needs and visible websites you can study before you approach anyone.

Write your niche in one sentence: "I design websites for [audience] that [result]." For example: "I design websites for photographers that turn visitors into booking enquiries." Keep that sentence on your own website, in your social profiles, and in every outreach message.

Step 2: Learn the core skills

You need four core skills, and none of them needs a degree.

First, design principles. Learn how layout, spacing, colour, and type work together, and how a visitor reads a page from top to bottom. Free guides and short courses cover this well, and studying good sites teaches it faster than theory alone.

Second, WordPress basics. WordPress is the most common platform for client sites, and most guides agree that its basics cover most of the technical knowledge a beginner designer needs. Learn how to install it, choose a theme, edit pages, add plugins, and keep it updated. You can read about the platform on WordPress.org, which describes it as the open source publishing platform used by millions of sites.

Third, enough HTML and CSS to tweak a design. HTML is the structure of a page. CSS is the styling, such as colours and spacing. You do not need to master either one. You need enough to fix a heading size, change a colour, or adjust spacing when a theme does not do what you want.

Fourth, clear communication with clients. Asking good questions, writing clear updates, and handling feedback calmly matter as much as design skill. A designer who communicates well keeps clients. A designer who goes silent loses them.

AI tools can help you work faster. They can draft text, explain an error, suggest CSS, and build a first draft of a page. Use them as a helper, not a replacement. AI can produce a page quickly, but it cannot ask a client the right questions, judge whether a design fits a brand, or take responsibility for a finished site. That judgement is your job. For the full skills path, read how to learn web design for beginners and how to learn UX design for beginners.

Step 3: Choose your platform and toolkit

Do not let one platform choose you. Choosing a platform is a practical part of how to start a web design business online, because the platform shapes what you can build and what you charge. Each platform suits different clients, and a good designer can explain the trade offs honestly. Compare them before you commit to one.

Squarespace and Showit are faster to learn and easier to hand over to a client. Wix and Webflow sit between them. There is no single best platform, only the best fit for each client.

Step 4: Build a portfolio with no clients yet

A portfolio is proof you can do the work. Without clients, build demo sites, and label them honestly as demo work. Never present a demo as a paid client project.

Build two or three demo sites for real local business types in your niche. Pick a real cafe, a real photographer, or a real clinic in your area, and redesign their site as a demo. Use your own words and stock photos, not their real photos or their real text, and add a clear note on the demo that it is a concept project, not a live client site.

Each demo should show a different strength. One can show a clean homepage with a booking form. One can show a simple online store with three products. One can show a service page with clear pricing and a contact form. Share your process as well as the finished screens: a short note on what was wrong with the old site, what you changed, and why. Clients hire designers who think, not just designers who decorate.

Put the demos on your own website, with a short case note under each one. Your own website is your most important portfolio piece. If your own site looks weak, no client will trust you with theirs.

Step 5: Set up the business

Set up the business before your first paying client. The exact steps differ by country, so use this list as a checklist to take to a local professional, not as legal advice.

  • Choose a business name and check that the domain and the social handles are free.
  • Choose a business structure. In the United States, the common choices are a sole proprietorship or a limited liability company, known as an LLC. The US Small Business Administration website explains business structures for US founders. Other countries have their own entity types and rules.
  • Open a separate business bank account, so client money and personal money never mix.
  • Write a standard contract before your first project. It should state the scope, the price, the payment schedule, the number of revision rounds, who owns the site and the content, and how either side can end the work. Use a lawyer or a trusted contract template for your country, and do not start work without a signed contract and a deposit.
  • Set up invoicing, and agree payment terms in writing. A common rule is 50 percent before work starts and 50 percent before launch.
  • Check your local tax rules, including whether you must register for VAT or a local sales tax, and set aside 25 to 30 percent of your income for tax, as many guides advise. Ask a local accountant if you are unsure.

You can start as a side hustle, in evenings and weekends, while you keep a job. Many designers start that way. Move to full time only when client income covers your costs with room to spare.

Step 6: Price your services

There are four common pricing models. Most designers start with packages, because they are easiest for a client to understand.

Model How it works Best for Risk
Hourly A rate per hour, tracked and billed Small fixes and unclear work Clients worry about the final bill
Project A fixed fee for a defined site Most first projects Scope creep if the job is not defined
Package A fixed price for a set bundle, such as a 5 page site Standard sites for one niche You must define exactly what is included
Retainer A monthly fee for care, updates, and small changes Ongoing income after launch Clients expect fast replies every month

List your prices as "starting at" figures, so a larger site costs more than a small one. A small template customisation may start at about 1,200 to 2,200 US dollars, and a custom site may start at about 2,000 US dollars, according to guides read for this article. Those are starting points from other guides, not prices you must charge. Your price should reflect your skill, your costs, your niche, and your country.

Worked example: pricing a package honestly

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. They show how the maths works.

Imagine a 5 page website package priced at 1,800 US dollars. You estimate 30 hours of work, including discovery, design, build, revisions, and launch. That gives an implied rate of 60 US dollars an hour, because 1,800 divided by 30 equals 60. Now check your costs: hosting and tools for the project at 50 US dollars, and a deposit of 50 percent, which is 900 US dollars, paid before you start.

Now check the risk. If the client asks for 10 extra pages and 3 extra revision rounds, and your contract does not limit revisions, the same 1,800 US dollars may cover 50 hours instead of 30. That drops the implied rate to 36 US dollars an hour, because 1,800 divided by 50 equals 36. This is why the contract must state the number of pages, the number of revision rounds, and the price of extra work. Price the package, then protect it with a clear scope.

Do not lower your prices just because AI makes the work faster. AI may cut the time a task takes, but the value to the client is the finished site, not the hours you spent. Price on value, on the result the client gets, and on the care you put into the work.

Step 7: Find your first clients

Finding clients is the hardest part of how to start a web design business online for most beginners, so pick one channel and work it well, instead of trying five channels badly. The channels that work for new designers are: people you know, local businesses, cold outreach, and referrals.

Start with people you know. Tell friends, family, and former colleagues what you do, in your niche sentence, and ask who they know in that niche. Your first client is often someone you already know, or one introduction away.

Go local next. Visit cafes, clinics, and shops in your area, look at their websites, and note what is weak. Then send a short message: 3 to 5 sentences, no links, naming one real problem you found and offering a 15 minute call. The goal of the first message is a call, not a sale. Follow up once after a few days if there is no reply, then move on.

Offer template customisation as a low barrier first sale. Some clients cannot afford a custom site, but they can afford a template set up with their words and photos. It is honest work, it builds your reviews, and it often leads to a full custom site later.

Set realistic expectations. One guide reports a cold outreach reply rate of about 2 percent, based on that author's own experience, which means about 2 to 3 replies per 100 emails. That figure comes from one author's experience, not from a verified study, and your rate will differ by niche, message, and timing. What matters is steady effort: a set number of real, personal messages each week, tracked in a simple list, with a follow up for each one.

Step 8: Run the business

Good delivery is what turns one client into referrals. Use a client questionnaire before any project starts, and a scope checklist that both sides agree.

Client questionnaire

Ask these questions before you quote a price:

  • What should the site do for the business? For example: take bookings, sell products, or win enquiries.
  • Who is the visitor, and what should they do first?
  • What pages are needed, and what words and photos will the client provide?
  • What sites does the client like, and what do they dislike about their current site?
  • What is the deadline, and what is the budget range?
  • Who will approve the design, and how fast can they reply?

Scope checklist

Agree these in writing before work starts:

  • Number of pages, and what each page contains.
  • Number of revision rounds included, and the price of extra rounds.
  • Who writes the words and who provides the photos.
  • Launch date, and what happens if the client is late with content.
  • What is included after launch, such as 30 days of small fixes, and what costs extra.

During the project, send short updates at agreed points: after the first design, after the build, and before launch. Show the work early, ask for feedback in writing, and keep a record of every change the client approves. Clear communication prevents most disputes.

After launch, offer a care plan. A monthly retainer for updates, backups, security checks, and small changes gives you steady income and gives the client peace of mind. Price it clearly, such as a set monthly fee for a set number of small changes, and state what counts as a small change. Some clients will stay on a care plan for years, and that recurring income is the steadiest part of a web design business.

Keep your admin simple. Send invoices on time, track expenses, set aside money for tax, and keep a backup of every client site. A spreadsheet is enough for your first five clients. A paid invoicing tool becomes worth it when invoicing takes more than an hour a month.

What it costs and what to expect

A new web design business can start for little money if you already own a computer. The main costs are your tools, your domain, and your time.

Cost First year guide
Computer 0 US dollars if you already own one
Domain and hosting About 50 to 150 US dollars for the year
Theme or builder 0 to 299 US dollars, only when a project needs it
Design and AI tools 0 to 140 US dollars for the year
Invoicing tool 0 US dollars to start, with a free tier or template
Business registration Varies by country, often 0 to a few hundred US dollars
First year total About 150 to 500 US dollars, plus your time

Expect the first paying client to take weeks or months, not days. Learning the skills takes weeks if you are new to design. Building a portfolio takes a few more weeks. Finding the first client takes longer still, and depends on how many real conversations you start each week. A side hustle pace is normal at first. Full time income, if it comes, usually takes a year or more of steady work.

Do not repeat income claims from other guides as facts. Some guides describe six figure designers, a first project at 100 US dollars an hour, or clients worth 20,000 US dollars over their lifetime. Those are single author stories without verification, not typical results, and this guide does not repeat them as facts. Your results will depend on your skill, your niche, your prices, and how many clients you win.

Common mistakes and pitfalls

  • Starting without a niche, so every project is different and nothing repeats.
  • Building a portfolio of copied designs instead of honest demo work.
  • Quoting a price before asking the client questionnaire, then finding the scope is twice the size.
  • Working without a contract or a deposit.
  • Allowing unlimited revisions, so a fixed price project never ends.
  • Scope creep: adding pages, features, and meetings that were never agreed, without charging for them.
  • Promising search rankings, which no honest designer can guarantee.
  • Lowering prices because AI is faster, instead of pricing on value.
  • Going silent during a project instead of sending short updates.
  • Forgetting backups, tax records, and invoices until something goes wrong.

Scope creep deserves special care. Every extra request should get one of three answers: yes, it is inside the agreed scope; yes, and here is the extra cost; or no, and here is what we can do instead. Saying yes to everything feels helpful and quietly destroys your profit.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to start a web design business?

If you already own a computer, a first year total of about 150 to 500 US dollars covers a domain, hosting, and basic tools, plus your time. Courses, paid themes, and paid tools add to that only when a project needs them. Check each platform's live pricing page before you buy, because prices change.

Is web design a good business to start?

It can be, if you enjoy design and client work, and if you pick a niche with businesses that have money to spend. It suits a side hustle start, because costs are low and you can take one client at a time. It does not suit someone who dislikes talking to clients or meeting deadlines.

Do you need to know how to code to be a web designer?

No. Page builders and platforms such as Squarespace, Showit, and WordPress with a theme let you build professional sites without writing code. Enough HTML and CSS to tweak a design helps, and it is worth learning, but it is not a requirement to start.

Do I need a degree to be a web designer?

No. Clients hire on portfolio and results, not on a degree. A strong portfolio of honest demo work, a clear website, and good communication matter more than a certificate.

Can I start a web design business as a side hustle?

Yes. Most designers start in evenings and weekends while they keep a job. Take one client at a time, be honest about your availability, and move to full time only when client income covers your costs with room to spare.

How long does it take to learn web design?

A beginner can learn the basics and build a simple site in a few weeks of focused practice, and a usable portfolio in a few months. Reaching a professional standard, where clients pay full prices, usually takes longer, and learning never fully stops, because platforms and design trends change.

What is the best platform to start a web design business?

There is no single best platform. WordPress suits most client sites and gives the most freedom. Squarespace and Showit suit simple sites and creative niches, and they are faster to learn. Wix and Webflow suit other cases. Choose the platform that fits your niche and the client in front of you, not the one a single guide promotes.

How do you get your first web design client?

Start with people you know, then approach local businesses in your niche with a short, personal message that names one real problem on their site. Offer a small first job, such as a template customisation, and ask happy clients for referrals. Expect slow replies at first, and keep a steady weekly number of real conversations going.

Conclusion: how to start a web design business online in 2026

That is how to start a web design business online: pick one niche, learn the core skills, choose a platform that fits your clients, build a portfolio of honest demo work, set up the business with a contract and a deposit, price in packages with a clear scope, and find your first clients through people you know and local businesses. Protect your prices with a written scope, keep clients with clear updates, and add a monthly care plan for steady income.
