How to Start an Online Tax Preparation Business in 2026

Learning how to start an online tax preparation business is a practical way to turn tax knowledge into a service people need every year. Most people find tax returns stressful, and many small business owners would rather pay a professional than do the paperwork themselves. An online tax preparation business lets you serve those clients from home, with video calls and secure document uploads instead of a shopfront. This guide shows you how to get qualified, register with the tax authority, choose software, set prices and find clients, with honest costs and a realistic timeline.

Is an online tax preparation business worth starting in 2026?

Yes, if you enjoy detail work and you are willing to study tax rules every year. Tax preparation is a repeat service. A client who trusts you with one return usually comes back the next year, and happy clients refer friends and family. That repeat base is the main strength of the business. The work is also seasonal, which suits some people and not others. Most returns cluster into a few busy months, and the rest of the year is quieter, which leaves time for tax planning work, bookkeeping or a second income stream.

Be honest about the learning curve. You handle other people's money and their legal duty to file correctly, so accuracy matters more than speed. If you like careful, rule-based work and you can explain a confusing form in plain words, this business fits you. If you want fast money with little study, it does not.

Step 1: Learn the tax skills you need

You do not need a degree to prepare tax returns, but you do need real knowledge. Start by learning how individual tax returns work in the country you will serve. In the United States, that means the individual return, Form 1040, plus the common schedules for income, deductions and credits. If you plan to serve small businesses, add business returns and self-employment tax to your study list.

There are three common routes to build the skill: a tax course from a recognised provider, working for an established tax firm for one season, or volunteering at a free tax help programme. Working for a firm is the strongest route, because you learn the workflow and the mistakes to avoid before your own name is on a return.

Consider a credential once you have the basics. An Enrolled Agent, known as an EA, is a federally licensed tax practitioner in the United States who can represent taxpayers before the IRS, the Internal Revenue Service. A Certified Public Accountant, known as a CPA, is a state licensed accountant with wider training. Neither is required to start, but both raise trust and let you handle harder returns. The IRS also runs an Annual Filing Season Program for preparers without a credential, which records continuing education and lists participants in a public directory. Check the requirements for your own country, because tax rules and credentials differ widely outside the United States.

Step 2: Get your PTIN

In the United States, anyone who prepares federal tax returns for pay must have a Preparer Tax Identification Number, known as a PTIN. It is the number you sign returns with instead of your own Social Security number, and it is the first registration to complete.

Apply online through the IRS PTIN system. Most first-time applicants can get a PTIN in about 15 minutes online, and the fee is 18.75 US dollars, as shown on the IRS PTIN requirements page, checked on 2026-11-24. The fee is non-refundable. The paper option, Form W-12, takes about six weeks to process, so apply online if you can. Renew your PTIN every year before you prepare returns for the new season. Note that some guides still quote an older fee of 19.75 dollars, which is out of date as of November 2026, so always check the IRS page itself for the current figure.

Step 3: Register to e-file and get your EFIN

Clients expect electronic filing, known as e-filing, so you need to become an authorised e-file provider. In the United States, that means applying through the IRS e-services system and receiving an Electronic Filing Identification Number, known as an EFIN.

The application has three stages: access the e-file application, submit it, and pass a suitability check. The IRS checks your credit, your tax compliance and your criminal background as part of that check. The IRS says approval can take up to 45 days from submission, so apply well before tax season starts, as explained on the IRS page for becoming an authorised e-file provider, checked on 2026-11-24. If you are not a credentialed professional such as a CPA or an Enrolled Agent, the IRS will usually ask you to be fingerprinted through its authorised vendor.

Choose the Electronic Return Originator option on the application if you will prepare and file returns for clients. Keep your EFIN details safe, and never share your e-services login with anyone.

Step 4: Choose your business structure and register

Pick a legal structure before you take clients. In the United States, a sole proprietorship is the simplest, but it does not separate your personal assets from the business. A limited liability company, known as an LLC, gives you that separation and is the common choice for a small tax practice. The right choice depends on your country and your plans, so a short talk with a local accountant is money well spent.

Register the business name with your state or local authority, and open a separate business bank account. Mixing business and personal money makes your own tax return harder and looks unprofessional if a client ever asks. Get an Employer Identification Number, known as an EIN, from the IRS if you form an LLC or plan to hire help. It is free to apply for online.

Buy professional liability insurance, also called errors and omissions insurance, before your first client. Tax work carries real liability. If a mistake on a return costs a client a penalty, the insurance covers the claim. Check whether your state requires a separate tax preparer licence or registration. A few US states, such as California, Oregon and New York, have their own rules, and the requirements change, so check your state regulator rather than trusting a general guide.

Step 5: Choose tax software and a secure client system

Professional tax software is the heart of the business. It checks the maths, flags missing forms, and files returns electronically. Popular professional options in the United States include Drake Tax, Intuit ProConnect Tax Online, Lacerte and TaxAct Professional. Prices vary by the number of returns and the features you need, so compare the per-return cost rather than the headline price. Most providers offer a demo or a trial, so test the workflow with a practice return before you pay.

Your client system matters as much as the software. You need a secure way for clients to send you tax documents, because email is not safe for Social Security numbers and bank details. Use a client portal with encryption, such as TaxDome, SmartVault or Canopy, or the portal built into your tax software. Add video calls for client meetings, an online scheduler such as Calendly, and e-signatures for engagement letters and returns. Keep a simple checklist for every return: documents received, return prepared, return reviewed, client signed, return filed, and copy stored. That checklist is your quality control.

System What it does What to check before you pay
Professional tax software Prepares and e-files returns, and checks the maths Per-return price, state forms included, e-file support
Client portal Secure document upload and messages Encryption, storage limits, ease of use for clients
E-signature tool Signs engagement letters and returns Legal validity in your country, price per document
Video calls and scheduler Client meetings and bookings Reliability, time zone handling, reminder emails

Security is not optional in this business. Use strong, unique passwords, turn on two-factor authentication everywhere, encrypt your devices, and back up client files to an encrypted location. The IRS requires tax preparers to have a written data security plan, so write one down and follow it. Never store client documents on an unprotected personal drive.

Step 6: Set your services and prices

Start narrow. Individual returns are the simplest service, and they are the best place to begin. Add small business returns, tax planning and amended returns only when you are confident with the basics. A clear service list helps you price honestly and helps clients understand what they are buying.

Service What it covers Typical price range in the US
Simple individual return One income source, standard deduction 100 to 250 dollars
Itemised individual return Deductions, credits, investment income 250 to 500 dollars
Self-employed return Business income, expenses, quarterly estimates 400 to 800 dollars
Small business return Partnership or company return 600 to 1,500 dollars
Tax planning session Year-round advice and estimates 150 to 300 dollars per hour

These ranges come from the competitor guides reviewed for this article, and prices differ by city, experience and complexity. Price by the return, not by the hour, so clients know the cost before they commit. Never base your fee on the size of a client's refund.

Step 7: Find your first clients

Your first clients usually come from people who already know you. Tell friends, family and local small business owners that you prepare tax returns online. Offer a free 15 minute call to explain how the process works, because many people are nervous about sending tax documents to someone they have only met online.

Then build a simple online presence. A one page website with your services, your starting prices, your credentials and a secure upload link is enough to start. Add a Google Business Profile so local searches can find you, and write short, helpful posts about common tax questions, such as which documents to gather or how estimated tax works for freelancers. How to run the tax season without burning out

Tax season is a sprint, and it rewards preparation. Before it starts, test your software, update your document checklist, set your prices, and write your standard emails for missing documents and completed returns. Book client slots in advance, and cap the number of returns you accept each week so you have time to review each one properly.

Worked example: what a first season could look like

Imagine a new preparer who charges an average of 200 dollars per individual return and completes 60 returns in the first tax season. The gross income is 60 multiplied by 200, which is 12,000 dollars. The costs for that season are about 1,200 dollars for professional software, 500 dollars for insurance, 300 dollars for the client portal and website, and 200 dollars for marketing, which adds up to 2,200 dollars. The income before tax is 12,000 minus 2,200, which is 9,800 dollars. That is a realistic shape for a first part-time season: modest income, low costs, and most of the value in the repeat clients you keep for next year. Results vary with your prices, your city, how many returns you can review carefully, and how early you start marketing.

How to start an online tax preparation business: what it costs and what to expect

You can start for very little if you already have a computer. The main costs are software, insurance and a client portal. A realistic first-year budget is 2,000 to 5,000 dollars, with the software as the biggest single cost. Add a training course if you are new to tax, which can cost a few hundred dollars more.

A realistic timeline is three months of setup before your first tax season: one month to learn and get your PTIN, one month to register for e-filing and set up your systems, and one month to market and test your process with practice returns. Apply for your EFIN early, because the IRS approval can take up to 45 days. Expect the first season to be slow, with most clients arriving through people you know. Income grows in the second and third seasons, when past clients return and refer others. Plan for the quiet months too, and keep a second income source until the practice is steady.

Common mistakes

  • Taking on complex business returns before you are ready for them, which leads to errors and unhappy clients.
  • Applying for your EFIN too late, and missing the start of tax season while you wait for approval.
  • Pricing by the hour, which makes clients nervous about the final bill.
  • Using email to collect tax documents, which puts client data at risk.
  • Skipping the review step, so simple errors reach the tax authority.
  • Basing your fee on the size of a refund, which creates a conflict of interest.
  • Accepting more returns than you can review properly, which hurts quality and your reputation.
  • Forgetting to renew your PTIN each year, which stops you from signing returns.

Risks and scams to watch for

The biggest risk is an error on a return. A wrong figure can cost a client a penalty and damage your reputation, so review every return and keep professional liability insurance current. Data security is the second risk. Tax files contain names, addresses, Social Security numbers and bank details, so a breach is serious. Use encryption, two-factor authentication and a written security plan, and never email sensitive documents.

Watch out for scams aimed at new preparers. A common one is a "software reseller" that sells cheap professional tax software that turns out to be pirated or fake, which can lock you out in the middle of the season. Buy software only from the provider or an authorised reseller. Another is a client who asks you to inflate a refund or hide income. Refuse, and end the engagement, because filing a false return can cost you your PTIN and lead to penalties. If a client pressures you, that is a sign to walk away. Tax rules differ by country, so check your local tax authority or a qualified accountant before you advise a client on anything outside your training.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to start an online tax preparation business?

A realistic first-year budget is 2,000 to 5,000 dollars, covering professional software, liability insurance, a secure client portal and a simple website. The PTIN fee is 18.75 dollars as of November 2026, and an EIN is free from the IRS. Costs vary by country and by the software you choose.

How long does it take to get a PTIN and an EFIN?

A PTIN usually takes about 15 minutes to get online. An EFIN takes longer, because the IRS runs a suitability check, and approval can take up to 45 days from submission. Apply for the EFIN at least two months before tax season starts.

Can I prepare tax returns from home?

Yes. Most online tax preparers work from home, using a secure portal for documents and video calls for meetings. You need a quiet workspace, a reliable computer, encrypted storage and a written data security plan. Check your local rules, because a few places have extra requirements for home-based tax work.

What is the difference between a PTIN and an EFIN?

A PTIN identifies you as a paid preparer, and you sign each return with it. An EFIN identifies your firm as an authorised e-file provider, and it lets you file returns electronically with the IRS. You usually need both to run an online tax preparation business in the United States.

Conclusion

Learning how to start an online tax preparation business in 2026 comes down to learning the tax rules, getting your PTIN and EFIN early, choosing secure software, pricing by the return, and finding your first clients through people you know. Start with simple individual returns, review every return before you file it, and protect client data as if it were your own. The practice grows slowly at first, then compounds as clients return each year and refer others.
