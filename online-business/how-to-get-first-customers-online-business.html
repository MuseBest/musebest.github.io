How to Get Your First Customers for a New Online Business in 2026

Win your first ten customers with conversations before ads: clear offers, short outreach templates, honest reviews, referrals, and a simple tracker.

A new online business with no customers faces a strange problem: buyers want proof before they buy, but proof comes from buyers. The way out is smaller than most guides suggest. You do not need an audience, a viral post, or an advertising budget to get your first customers. You need a clear offer, a list of likely buyers, and a series of helpful conversations that lead, naturally, to a sale. This guide is built around the first ten customers, because ten paying customers teach you more about pricing, delivery, and language than a thousand views ever will. The first ten principle is simple: conversations before ads. Paid advertising amplifies an offer that already works; it rarely fixes an offer nobody understands or trusts.

Before you chase customers: is your offer clear in one sentence?

Before any outreach, test your offer with one sentence: who it helps, what outcome it delivers, and what the next step is. For example: "I write four menu graphics a month for small food businesses, delivered every Monday, for one monthly price, starting with a paid trial week." A stranger should understand the buyer, the outcome, and the action without asking a follow up question. If your sentence needs a paragraph of explanation, the offer is still too broad, and outreach will produce confusion instead of sales. Run an offer clarity checklist before you contact anyone. Can a stranger name the buyer from your sentence? Can they name the outcome? Can they see what is included and what is not? Can they see the price or the next step?

The first ten principle: conversations before ads

Your first ten customers will almost certainly come from conversations, not from campaigns. That means your daily work in the early stage is not designing posts or studying analytics, but talking to likely buyers, answering their questions, and inviting a clear next step. Set a simple activity goal, such as ten useful conversations a week, and judge the week by that number plus replies, not by followers or views. A week with ten conversations and two replies teaches you something. A week with no conversations teaches you nothing, however busy it felt. Conversations also build the language your business will use later. Buyers will describe their problem in their own words, ask questions you had not considered, and object in ways that reveal price, trust, or timing concerns.

Path A: services, freelance marketplaces and direct outreach

For services, the fastest first customer route is usually a combination of marketplaces and direct outreach. On a freelance marketplace, your profile is your shop window: one clear service, a few strong examples, a realistic starting price, and a description written in the buyer's language. Apply only to jobs that fit your package closely, and write proposals that refer to the buyer's actual problem, not a generic pitch. A short proposal that shows you understood the job beats a long proposal that lists every skill you own. For a fuller freelance starting path, see How to Find Freelance Clients and How to Create a Freelance Portfolio. Direct outreach complements marketplaces, because it reaches buyers who have not posted a job. List twenty to fifty likely buyers, research each one briefly, and send a short personal message (templates are below).

Path B: digital products, marketplaces and searchable content

Digital products rarely sell on outreach alone, because buyers usually search for a solution when they need it. Your first customers therefore come from being findable where buyers search. List your product on a marketplace that already has traffic for your category, with a clear title, an honest description, a preview, and a price that covers fees. Use the words buyers actually type when they search, not clever wording that only you understand. A single product, well listed, beats ten thin listings that confuse search and split reviews. Searchable content is the second route: short articles, videos, or posts that answer the exact question your product solves, with a clear link to the product at the end.

Path C: physical products, early reviewers, communities, and micro creators

Physical products need trust before purchase, because buyers cannot touch the product and worry about quality, delivery, and returns. Early reviewers solve part of that problem, if handled honestly. Offer a small number of early buyers a fair introductory price or a sample in exchange for an honest review, with no condition that the review must be positive. Never buy fake reviews, never write your own reviews, and never offer payment for positive ratings, because that destroys trust and can breach platform rules. A handful of honest reviews, including a critical one you respond to well, is more persuasive than a wall of identical praise. Communities are the second route for physical products: groups where buyers discuss the hobby, problem, or lifestyle your product serves.

Outreach that gets replies: three short templates (and what not to send)

Good outreach is short, personal, and specific. It shows you understood the buyer, offers one clear outcome, and asks for one simple reply. Bad outreach is long, generic, and self focused: a list of your skills, a vague offer, and pressure to reply. Never send the same message to fifty people with only the name changed and expect replies, because buyers can tell. Never claim a connection you do not have, never invent a result, and never follow up aggressively. One polite follow up is enough.

Template 1, for services: "Hello [name], I noticed [specific detail about their business or post]. I help [buyer type] with [outcome], usually through [one package].

Turn buyers into proof: honest reviews and testimonials, done properly

After a sale, proof is the next asset to build, and it must be honest to be useful. Ask for a review at the right moment: after delivery, once the buyer has had a chance to use the product or see the result, and when satisfaction is fresh. Make it easy: send a direct link, suggest two or three prompts, such as what problem they had, what changed, and what they would tell a friend, and thank them whatever they write. Use their exact words where permission is given, and never edit a testimonial to change its meaning. If a review is critical, respond calmly, fix what you can, and treat the exchange as public evidence of good service. Testimonials need the same care.

Referrals without awkwardness, and your first email list

Referrals feel awkward when asked too early, too vaguely, or with pressure. They feel natural when asked after a good delivery, with a specific request. The right moment is after a buyer is happy: they have thanked you, used the product, or told you the result helped. At that point, a simple ask works: "I am glad that helped. If you know one person with the same problem, would you be happy to pass my name on, or introduce us?" That ask is specific (one person), low pressure (would you be happy to), and tied to a real result. Make referrals easy to give: provide a short description the buyer can forward, a link to your offer, and a clear next step for the new person.

Track it simply: reach, replies, interest, sales, and source

A simple tracker turns outreach from guesswork into a system. Record five numbers each week, by source: reach (how many likely buyers saw your offer or message), replies (how many responded), interest (how many asked about price, timing, or details), sales (how many paid), and source (marketplace, community, outreach, content, referral, or email). A spreadsheet with one row per week and one column per number is enough. The pattern between the numbers tells you where to work next, and it prevents the common mistake of changing everything at once. Use the tracker to set realistic activity goals. If your reply rate is low, improve your message or your buyer list, not your website. If replies are high but interest is low, improve offer clarity.

If nobody responds: diagnose the weak link before quitting

A dry spell is painful, but it is also information, if you diagnose it in order. Work through four possible weak links, one at a time. First, wrong people: are you contacting buyers who actually have the problem and can pay, or a broad audience that merely finds the topic interesting? Second, unclear offer: can a stranger name the buyer, outcome, and next step from your message in seconds? Third, weak trust: do you have any proof, examples, reviews, or a clear presence that a stranger can check, or are you asking for payment on the strength of a new profile alone? Fourth, too little traffic: have you actually contacted enough of the right people, or have you sent ten messages and concluded the market is dead? Test each link with a small change and watch the tracker.

After ten customers, and risks

After ten paying customers, your job shifts from finding any buyer to building a repeatable system. Systematise what already works: a standard package, a delivery checklist, a welcome message, a review request, and a referral ask after each good delivery. Write down the steps you repeat, so delivery stays consistent when volume grows. Improve your best channel first: if outreach brings customers, refine your list and templates; if a marketplace brings them, improve listings and reviews; if content brings them, publish the next helpful piece on the same topic. Do not add three new channels at ten customers. Depth in one proven channel beats breadth across unproven ones.

FAQs

How do I get my first customer with no audience?

Start with conversations where buyers already gather: a marketplace, a community, or direct outreach to likely buyers. Offer one clear package or product, show examples or samples, and ask for a specific next step, such as a trial, a booking, or a purchase. An audience helps later, but your first customers usually come from personal outreach and helpful answers, not from a large following.

Should I run ads to get my first customers?

Usually not. Ads amplify an offer that already sells, but they rarely fix unclear offers, weak trust, or a wrong audience, and they spend money before you know your numbers. Get your first customers through free conversations, marketplaces, and communities first, learn what converts, and consider a small, strictly limited ad test only after the offer sells reliably without ads.

How do I ask for referrals?

Ask after a good delivery, when the buyer is happy, and keep it specific and low pressure: ask whether they know one person with the same problem and whether they would be happy to pass your name on. Provide a short description and a link they can forward. Do not ask vaguely, do not pressure, and do not offer misleading incentives. One warm referral often converts better than many cold messages.

How many customers do I need before paid ads make sense?

There is no fixed number, but ten paying customers through free routes is a sensible minimum, because by then you know your offer, price, conversion pattern, and value per sale after fees. With those numbers, you can judge whether an ad test is affordable. If you do not yet know what a sale is worth or how many enquiries become sales, ads are still a guess, not a test.

What is the cheapest way to get customers?

The cheapest routes are usually marketplace search, helpful answers in communities, direct outreach, and referrals from happy buyers, because they cost time rather than money. The cheapest route for you depends on your business type: services suit outreach and marketplaces, digital products suit marketplaces and searchable content, and physical products suit communities, early reviewers, and micro creators. Track each route and spend more time on the one that actually brings sales.

Conclusion: your next step

Your first customers will not arrive because your business exists. They will arrive because a clear offer reached the right people, through helpful conversations, and earned their trust one sale at a time. Check your offer in one sentence, choose the path that fits your business type, use short honest outreach, turn each buyer into proof with permission, ask for referrals after good deliveries, start a tiny email list, and track reach, replies, interest, sales, and source every week. When silence comes, diagnose the weak link before quitting, and change one thing at a time. Your next step is to list twenty likely buyers today, write your one sentence offer, and send the first three outreach messages using the template that fits your type. Do not wait for an audience, a perfect website, or an advertising budget. Ten conversations this week, followed by a review of what happened, will teach you more than another month of preparation, and those conversations are where your first ten customers are waiting to be found.
