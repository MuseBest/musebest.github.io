How to Start an Ecommerce Agency in 2026

Learning how to start an ecommerce agency is a practical way to turn store-building or marketing skill into a business that serves online sellers. Millions of online stores need help with design, ads, email and conversion. This guide shows you how to choose a service lane, package one clear offer, price it, win clients and deliver results, with honest costs and a realistic timeline.

What an ecommerce agency actually does

An ecommerce agency is a service business that helps online stores grow. That sounds simple, but it can mean very different things, and the first job is to decide which one you are. This guide is about running an agency that serves online stores. It is not about running your own store, which is a different business. If you want to run your own store instead, read our guide to how to start an ecommerce store with Shopify first. It is also not a general digital marketing agency, which serves all kinds of businesses. If you want the general agency route, read our guide to how to start a digital marketing agency.

Agency lane What you sell Who buys it
Store setup Build or redesign a store on Shopify or WooCommerce New brands and stores that need a relaunch
Growth and ads Paid social, search ads and landing pages Stores with traffic that want more sales
Retention and email Email flows, SMS, loyalty and repeat purchase work Stores with buyers who never come back
Conversion optimisation Product pages, checkout flow and site speed Stores with traffic that does not buy enough
Maintenance and support Updates, fixes, apps and small improvements Busy stores that need reliable help

Each lane can work. The best one is usually the one that matches your real skill. Pick one lane and build the agency around it. You can add a second lane later, once the first one is working. Trying to be full service on day one usually means doing five things at a mediocre level.

Shopify is the most common platform for this work. Its partner programme lets agencies build stores and list in a partner directory where merchants look for help. Shopify reports more than 100,000 partners, and 1.3 billion dollars paid to partners in 2025, according to the Shopify Partner Programme page, checked on 2026-11-24.

Step 1: Choose one service lane

Start with the lane that fits what you can already do well. If you have built stores before, start with store setup. If you have run email campaigns, start with retention. If you have run ads, start with growth. Your first clients pay for skill they can see, not for a long list of services.

Write down what your lane includes and what it does not. A retention offer might include a welcome flow, an abandoned cart flow and a monthly campaign calendar, but not paid ads or redesigns. Clear boundaries protect you from scope creep, which is when a client keeps adding work without paying for it.

Step 2: Pick a niche you can win

A niche makes you easier to trust, easier to refer and easier to remember. A store owner is far more likely to hire "the agency that does email for beauty brands on Shopify" than "a digital agency for ecommerce". Pick your niche by product type, by platform, by problem or by revenue stage, and combine two of them for a sharper position.

Niche angle Example Why it works
Product type Skincare, fashion, pet products, supplements You learn the buyer and the language fast
Platform Shopify brands, WooCommerce brands You master one set of tools deeply
Problem Low conversion, poor retention, slow checkout You sell a fix, not a vague service
Revenue stage New brands, stores at 10,000 dollars a month, scaling brands You match your price to their budget

Validate the niche before you commit. List 30 brands in the niche, review their stores and ads, and note the problems that keep appearing. If those brands are spending on growth and you can explain their problem better than other agencies, the niche can work.

Step 3: Package one clear offer

A weak offer sounds like this: "We do ecommerce marketing, design, SEO, funnels, email, social and consulting." A strong offer sounds like this: "We help small Shopify skincare brands increase repeat purchases with automated email flows, a campaign calendar and lifecycle segmentation." The second one is specific, and a buyer can understand it in seconds.

Your first offer should answer four questions: who it is for, what problem it solves, what is included, and what result it is designed to improve. Use this frame: "We help [type of ecommerce brand] improve [specific business goal] through [service or process]." Then list the deliverables plainly, so a buyer knows exactly what they get. A retention offer might list a welcome flow, an abandoned cart flow, a post-purchase flow, a win-back flow, a monthly campaign calendar and a performance review. Keep the list short and honest.

Add a short promise, but never a guarantee. Good promises sound like 'a store that is easier to buy from'. Bad promises sound like 'double your revenue in 30 days'. Serious clients are sceptical of hype.

Step 4: Set up the business properly

Set up the basics before you take client work, because a messy foundation makes everything harder later. Register the business, open a separate business bank account, and set up invoicing. The US Small Business Administration's guide to planning your business, checked on 2026-11-24, explains the two common plan formats, traditional and lean startup. In most countries, a limited company or an LLC is the common choice for a small agency. Check whether your city or county needs a general business licence, and whether your state charges sales tax on digital services. Rules differ by country, so check your local authority or a qualified adviser.

Your website needs only a few pages to start: home, services, about, examples of your work, and contact. The home page should explain who you help and what you do in plain words. The examples page can show teardown-style work until you have real client case studies. Keep the site simple and honest.

Protect yourself with a contract for every client, covering scope, payment terms, revision limits, timeline and termination clauses. A contract feels optional until a client delays payment or changes the scope.

Step 5: Build a portfolio without clients

You do not need paying clients before you can look credible. You need proof of thinking. Build a demonstration portfolio by reviewing real stores in your niche and showing what you would improve. A store teardown audit, a product page critique, an email flow mockup, or a conversion review all show skill without a client.

For each piece, explain the business context, the problem, your recommendation, the logic behind it, and the expected business impact. That structure matters more than screenshots. A screenshot shows what a store looks like. Your reasoning shows why a change would help, and that is what a store owner pays for. Pick three real brands in your niche, write a short teardown for each, and publish them on your website and on LinkedIn. That content also brings inbound enquiries, because store owners search for exactly this kind of help.

Step 6: Price your services

Pricing is where many smart people undercut themselves, because they are afraid nobody will say yes. Hourly pricing feels safer, but it punishes efficiency: the better you get, the less you earn for the same outcome. A smarter model is fixed-fee pricing for defined work, then monthly retainers for ongoing services.

Pricing model Best for Watch out for
One-time project Store setup, audits, migrations, flow builds Income can be lumpy
Monthly retainer Email, ads, conversion work, ongoing strategy Needs a clear scope
Hybrid Setup fee plus monthly management Can confuse buyers if packaged poorly
Performance add-on Bonus tied to growth milestones Hard to track without clean data

One guide reports that Shopify agency builds range from 3,000 to 25,000 dollars, with monthly retainers of 1,000 to 5,000 dollars. Treat those as reported benchmarks, not as a promise. A setup fee plus a monthly retainer often works well for a new agency.

Test your price with a simple question: if the client says yes, can you realistically do great work without resenting the scope? If not, the price is too low. Low prices do not just reduce your income. They often attract the most difficult clients, who expect too much, delay feedback and question every invoice.

Step 7: Win your first clients

Your first clients usually come from direct action, not from perfect branding. Start with warm outreach: former colleagues, founders in your network, and store owners in online communities. Make a short list of businesses you can help, review each store quickly, and send a short message that names one concrete improvement. That shows you looked at their store, which is far more persuasive than a generic agency pitch.

Use targeted cold outreach the same way: pick a narrow niche, build a prospect list, review each brand, find one concrete issue, and send a short message with a practical observation. Offer a call, a short video review, or a mini audit as a next step. A short personalised video can work well, because it shows effort without a full proposal upfront. Follow up a few times, and keep the message short each time.

Create one lead magnet that matches your offer, such as a ten-point product page audit. It gives you a reason to start conversations. Publish one teardown a week on LinkedIn, and track every lead and follow-up.

Step 8: Deliver work that keeps clients

Winning a client is exciting. Keeping one is what builds an agency. Start with a clear onboarding process: a welcome email, an access checklist, a kickoff questionnaire, a kickoff call, and a 30-day roadmap. Answer five questions in week one: what happens first, what access you need, what timeline to expect, how often you will communicate, and how success will be measured.

Agree success metrics before you start the work. If success is unclear, the client will invent their own standard later, and that usually ends badly. The right metrics depend on your service: conversion rate, average order value, email-attributed revenue, repeat purchase rate, return on ad spend, cost per acquisition, or cart recovery rate. If the tracking setup is weak, fix that early, because you need clean data to defend your work.

Communicate as part of the service. A weekly update and a monthly review keep clients calm. Explain what changed and what happens next in plain words, and document your process as you go, so a new hire can follow it.

Worked example: what a first client could earn

Example only: these numbers are not typical results, and they are shown only to explain how the maths works.

Imagine a new agency that wins a small Shopify skincare brand as a retention client. The agency charges a setup fee of 1,500 dollars for the email flows, plus a monthly retainer of 800 dollars for campaigns and testing. In the first three months, the income is 1,500 plus 3 multiplied by 800, which is 3,900 dollars. The costs for those three months are about 150 dollars a month for tools, 150 dollars a month for the website and invoicing, and 50 dollars a month for a scheduler and video calls, which adds up to about 1,050 dollars. The income before tax for those three months is 3,900 minus 1,050, which is 2,850 dollars. That is a realistic shape for a first client: modest income, low costs, and most of the value in the retainer continuing past month three. Results vary with your price, your niche, and whether the client stays.

How to start an ecommerce agency: what it costs and what to expect

You can start an ecommerce agency for 1,000 to 3,000 dollars, covering business registration, a domain and hosting, basic tools, and a small marketing budget. The main costs are software subscriptions, legal formation and your website, not stock or an office. A computer you already own is enough to start.

A realistic timeline is 90 days to your first paying client: the first month to pick a niche, package your offer and build two or three portfolio pieces; the second month to prospect, send outreach and close a first project; the third month to deliver the work, track the results and turn it into a case study. Plan for three to six months before income becomes steady, and keep a second income source until retainers cover your costs. Hiring help makes sense only after your process is documented, because a new hire cannot follow a process that exists only in your head.

Common mistakes

  • Selling too broadly too early, which makes you forgettable and hard to refer.
  • Chasing tactics before you have a clear position, such as tweaking outreach templates while your offer is still vague.
  • Underpricing to get a fast yes, which creates low-quality work and difficult clients.
  • Operating without systems, so missed tasks and unclear timelines create churn after the first few clients.
  • Skipping a contract, so scope, payment and revision limits are unclear.
  • Promising results you cannot control, such as guaranteed revenue growth or a fixed return on ad spend.
  • Hiring before your process is documented, which creates inconsistency and rework.
  • Spending on a fancy office or brand before you have a client.

Risks and scams to watch for

The biggest risk is scope creep. A client asks for one more page, one more campaign, one more revision, and the project that should have taken two weeks takes two months. Protect yourself with a written scope, a revision limit, and a clear price for extra work. The second risk is client concentration. If one client pays most of your income and they leave, your revenue drops sharply. Aim for at least three retainer clients before you feel safe.

Watch out for scams aimed at new agencies. A common one is a "client" who asks you to pay for access to their store, or a tool vendor that locks you into a long contract for software you do not need. Real clients pay you, not the other way round. Another is a store owner who asks for a full audit or a full strategy for free, then uses your work without hiring you. Give a useful sample, but keep the full plan for paying clients. Check the rules for advertising and data protection in your country, because email and ad work touches customer data, and the rules differ by place.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to start an ecommerce agency?

A realistic starting budget is 1,000 to 3,000 dollars, covering business registration, a domain and hosting, basic tools and a small marketing budget. The biggest cost is your time in the first months, before retainers start. Costs vary by country and by the tools you choose.

How long does it take to get the first client?

A realistic timeline is one to three months from starting outreach to signing a first project, if you prospect consistently and your offer is clear. The first client usually comes from your network or from a store teardown you published, not from a cold ad. Plan for three to six months before income becomes steady.

Can I run an ecommerce agency on my own?

Yes. Most agencies start as a solo founder with a laptop and a few tools. The difference between a freelancer and an agency is the system: a defined offer, a repeatable delivery process, standard onboarding and clear reporting. Build those first, and hire only when the process is documented and the work is more than one person can handle well.

Conclusion

Learning how to start an ecommerce agency in 2026 comes down to choosing one service lane, picking a niche you can win, packaging one clear offer, pricing it to protect your time, and winning clients with specific, useful outreach. Build a portfolio from real store teardowns, deliver with a clear process, and keep clients with honest reporting. The agency grows one retainer at a time, and each happy store owner becomes your best source of the next client.
