A written buy box is the first step in how to buy an online business without falling in love with the first listing you see. Your buy box keeps you disciplined.</p>

<h2 id="step-3-shortlist-fast-with-the-30-minute-screen">Step 3: Shortlist fast with the 30 minute screen</h2>
<p>Before you spend days on due diligence, screen each listing in about 30 minutes. Ask six questions, and walk away if any answer is weak.</p>
<ol>
<li>What makes the money? You should be able to explain the revenue in one sentence, such as "It sells a 49 US dollar a month tool to dental clinics." If you cannot, the model is too complex or too vague.</li>
<li>How much does the owner work? A business that needs the owner 40 hours a week is a job, not a business, and its profit is lower than it looks once you cost that time fairly.</li>
<li>Is revenue recurring or one off? Subscriptions and repeat orders are steadier than single sales.</li>
<li>How old are the customers? A business with customers who have stayed for years is sturdier than one with a flood of new buyers and no repeat buyers.</li>
<li>Where does traffic come from, by channel and by month? If more than half comes from one source, such as one search engine update or one social account, the business is fragile.</li>
<li>Why is it for sale? Good reasons include retirement, a new venture, or a move. Vague reasons, such as "lost interest", deserve a closer look.</li>
</ol>
<p>Score each answer simply: strong, unclear, or weak. Two weak answers means you move on. There are always more listings, and discipline at this stage saves weeks later.</p>
<h2 id="step-4-value-the-business-with-sde">Step 4: Value the business with SDE</h2>
<p>When you learn how to buy an online business, valuation is the skill that saves you the most money. Most small online businesses are valued on profit, not revenue. The measure most sellers use is SDE, which stands for seller discretionary earnings. SDE is the profit left after adding back the owner's pay and personal costs, because a new owner will run the business differently.</p>
<p>The formula is: SDE equals earnings before tax, plus owner pay, plus depreciation and amortisation, plus discretionary expenses, plus non recurring items. Depreciation is the accounting cost of assets wearing out. Amortisation is the same idea for non physical assets. Discretionary expenses are personal costs the owner ran through the business, such as a personal car. Non recurring items are one off costs that will not repeat, such as a single legal bill.</p>
<h3 id="worked-example-valuing-a-business-with-sde">Worked example: valuing a business with SDE</h3>
<p>Example only: these numbers are not typical results. They show how the maths works.</p>
<p>Imagine a content site for sale. Its figures for the last year are:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Item</th>
<th>Amount</th>
</tr>

<tr>

</tr>
<tr>
<td>Depreciation</td>

<td>6,000 US dollars</td>

<p>The price is SDE times a multiple. The multiple depends on the business type, its growth, its risks, and how much of the profit depends on the owner. <p>At a 3x multiple, the example business would be priced at 396,000 US dollars, because 132,000 times 3 equals 396,000. At 2.5x it would be 330,000 US dollars. At 4x it would be 528,000 US dollars. The range is wide, which is why the multiple matters as much as the profit.</p>
<p>Apply two warnings before you accept any multiple. First, normalise the profit. If the owner works 30 hours a week for free, subtract the market cost of hiring someone to do that work. A business that looks profitable because the owner works for nothing is not profitable. Second, discount for single channel risk. If more than half of traffic or revenue comes from one source, reduce the multiple by about 20 to 40 percent, because one change at that source can halve the business. A store with 500,000 US dollars of revenue and an 8 percent margin is a 40,000 US dollar profit business, not a half million dollar business. Value the profit, not the revenue.</p>

<h2 id="step-6-agree-terms">Step 6: Agree terms</h2>
<p>Terms matter as much as price. A fair price with bad terms is a bad deal. The main terms to agree are:</p>
<ul>
<li>Price and payment: full payment at close, or part now and part later.</li>
<li>Asset sale or share sale: most small deals are asset sales, where you buy the assets and not the company itself. Share sales, where you buy the company, carry more history and more risk. Tax differs by country, so get advice.</li>
<li>Letter of intent, known as an LOI: a short document that sets out the price, the assets included, the due diligence period, transition support, non compete terms, and exclusivity, usually 30 to 45 days. It is usually not binding on price, but it focuses both sides.</li>
<li>Earn out: part of the price is paid later, only if the business hits agreed targets. This shares risk with the seller.</li>
<li>Seller financing: the seller lends you part of the price, often 20 to 50 percent, repaid over 12 to 24 months. This can help if a bank will not lend.</li>
<li>Holdback: 10 to 20 percent of the price is held back for 30 to 90 days after the sale, and released if nothing hidden appears.</li>
<li>Non compete: the seller agrees not to start a rival business for a set period.</li>
<li>Transition support: the seller helps for 30 to 60 days after the sale, answering questions and introducing you to suppliers and customers.</li>
</ul>
<p>Negotiate from the numbers, not from emotion. If due diligence finds a problem, use it to adjust the price or the terms, or to walk away. A seller who rushes you, or who refuses a holdback on a large deal, is telling you something.</p>

<h2 id="step-8-close-through-escrow-and-take-over-in-order">Step 8: Close through escrow and take over in order</h2>
<p>Never pay a seller directly. Use an escrow service, which holds your money and releases it only when the agreed assets have transferred. Marketplaces such as Flippa offer escrow as part of the sale, and independent escrow services exist for direct deals. If a seller asks you to pay outside escrow, stop. That is the most common scam pattern in this market.</p>
<h3 id="handover-checklist-in-order">Handover checklist, in order</h3>
<ol>
<li>Sign the asset purchase agreement, with the help of your lawyer.</li>
<li>Transfer the domain to your account, and check it resolves to your hosting.</li>
<li>Transfer hosting, code, and databases, and take a fresh backup.</li>
<li>Transfer payment accounts, such as Stripe or PayPal, and check payouts reach your bank.</li>
<li>Transfer marketplace and app store accounts, if any.</li>
<li>Transfer the email list and the email tool account.</li>
<li>Transfer social media accounts and ad accounts.</li>
<li>Transfer supplier and contractor agreements, and introduce yourself to each one.</li>
<li>Change every password and every two factor authentication setting, and remove the seller's access.</li>
<li>Confirm the seller's transition support start date and how to reach them.</li>
</ol>

<h3 id="scam-warning">Scam warning</h3>
<p>Scams in this market follow a pattern. A listing shows strong profit with screenshots only, the seller refuses live access to records, and the deal must close quickly, outside escrow, often with a reason that sounds urgent. Real sellers accept escrow, give live access, and allow time for checks. If a deal feels rushed or secret, it is not a bargain. It is a warning.</p>

<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 id="how-much-money-do-i-need-to-buy-an-online-business">How much money do I need to buy an online business?</h3>
<p>Small sites start from about 10,000 US dollars, and a business with meaningful cash flow usually costs 100,000 to 300,000 US dollars. Keep a reserve of 10 to 20 percent of the price for running costs after the sale. The exact figure depends on the business type, its profit, and its multiple.</p>
<h3 id="is-it-safer-to-buy-than-to-build">Is it safer to buy than to build?</h3>
<p>It can be, because you buy existing customers and income, but it is not safe by itself. A bought business can lose its traffic or its customers after the sale. Safety comes from careful due diligence, a fair price, escrow, and a business you understand, not from buying alone.</p>
<h3 id="how-long-does-buying-an-online-business-take">How long does buying an online business take?</h3>
<p>A small asset purchase usually takes 6 to 12 weeks from first contact to close. Larger deals take longer, because due diligence, legal checks, and loan approval all need time. Do not rush the timetable to meet a seller's deadline.</p>
<h3 id="do-i-need-a-lawyer-to-buy-an-online-business">Do I need a lawyer to buy an online business?</h3>
<p>Yes, for any deal above roughly 25,000 US dollars, and it is wise even for smaller deals. A lawyer checks the sale agreement, the contracts, and whether the assets truly transfer. An accountant should check the finances at the same time.</p>
<h3 id="can-i-buy-a-business-with-no-experience">Can I buy a business with no experience?</h3>
<p>You can, but buy a business type you understand, or buy small and learn with a survivable amount of money. A content site suits a writer. A store suits someone who understands stock and customers. Do not buy a complex SaaS business as your first purchase if you cannot read its code or run its systems.</p>
<h3 id="what-should-i-do-if-the-seller-will-not-give-analytics-or-payment-access">What should I do if the seller will not give analytics or payment access?</h3>
<p>Walk away. Read only access to analytics and payment records is a normal, reasonable request, and honest sellers expect it. Screenshots can be edited, so they are not proof. A refusal is itself the most useful fact the seller has given you.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion-how-to-buy-an-online-business-in-2026">Conclusion: how to buy an online business in 2026</h2>
<p>That is how to buy an online business safely: write your buy box first, study listings before you bid, screen fast, value the business on SDE with a realistic multiple, check every claim against live records, agree fair terms with a holdback and transition support, and close through escrow. Then change nothing for a month while you learn how the business really runs.</p>
