How to Start a Bookkeeping Business Online in 2026

Learning how to start a bookkeeping business online is one of the most accessible routes into running your own firm. Every business needs someone to keep its books in order, the startup costs are low, and you can work from home with a laptop and cloud software. This guide is about running the business, not just doing the job: the skills, the certification, the packages, the prices, and how to find clients who stay.

What a bookkeeping business does

A bookkeeping business handles recurring financial recordkeeping for other businesses. That includes categorising transactions, reconciling bank and credit card accounts, managing accounts payable and accounts receivable, which means tracking who the business owes and who owes the business, and producing monthly reports such as profit and loss statements and balance sheets.

Clients connect their bank accounts and credit cards to cloud software such as QuickBooks Online or Xero. Transactions flow in automatically. You log in weekly or every two weeks, categorise everything, reconcile accounts, and flag anything unusual. At month-end, you close the books and deliver reports. Most virtual bookkeepers work with 10 to 25 clients at once, and each client needs 2 to 6 hours a month depending on transaction volume.

This is different from working as a remote bookkeeper for one employer. A business owner sells a service to many clients, sets the prices, and can hire help as the client list grows. If you want the employed route first, read how to work as a remote bookkeeper.

Bookkeeper vs accountant: the line you must not cross

Bookkeeping is recording what happened. Accounting is interpreting it, giving tax advice, and filing tax returns. In most countries, bookkeeping is not a licensed profession, so you do not legally need a degree or certification to start. Preparing or filing tax returns for clients is the exception and often requires a separate credential, so do not offer it unless you hold the right qualification for your country.

Stay on the right side of the line. You can categorise transactions, reconcile accounts, chase invoices, and produce reports. You should not give tax advice, sign tax returns, or tell a client how much tax they owe, unless you are qualified to do so. When a client needs tax help, refer them to an accountant or a certified public accountant, known as a CPA. That referral relationship works both ways, because accountants often send bookkeeping work to bookkeepers they trust.

Step 1: Learn the fundamentals and get certified

You do not need a degree, but you do need competence. Start with free resources to understand double-entry bookkeeping, the accounting equation, and financial statement basics. Double-entry bookkeeping simply means every transaction is recorded in two places, so the books always balance, and it is the foundation of the whole job.

Then get certified in the software your clients use. The most useful free certifications are:

  • QuickBooks ProAdvisor certification: free through the Intuit ProAdvisor programme, which offers training and certification for QuickBooks Online. It also lists you in the ProAdvisor directory, where small business owners actively search for help.
  • Xero Advisor certification: free through Xero partner programme, and essential in the UK, Australia, and New Zealand markets where Xero is widely used.
  • Certified Bookkeeper designation: from the American Institute of Professional Bookkeepers in the US, which requires passing a four-part exam and at least two years of full-time bookkeeping experience, or 3,000 hours of part-time experience.
  • Certified Public Bookkeeper licence: from the National Association of Certified Public Bookkeepers in the US, which requires passing two exams and verifying bookkeeping experience.

If you are starting from scratch, an online bookkeeping course plus QuickBooks Online or Xero certification is the fastest path to credibility. Most can be completed in 2 to 4 months of part-time study. Practice with real transaction scenarios, not just theory, because clients care about accuracy and reliability, not diplomas.

Step 2: Choose your niche and services

General bookkeeping for "small businesses" is a crowded market. Specialising makes you referable and allows premium pricing, because you understand one industry quirks and can charge for that knowledge. Strong niches include e-commerce sellers, real estate investors, restaurants and food trucks, construction contractors, medical and dental practices, and creative agencies and freelancers.

Pick an industry where you have existing knowledge, interest, or connections. Understanding industry-specific expenses, common software integrations, and typical financial patterns makes you far more valuable. E-commerce involves multichannel sales and inventory purchases. Real estate involves rental property accounting and commission tracking. Restaurants involve daily cash reconciliation and tip allocation. Non-profits involve fund accounting and grant reporting.

Define your core services first, then add extras once you are established:

Core services, start here Add-on services, once established
Monthly bookkeeping, categorisation, reconciliation, and reporting Payroll processing
Accounts receivable and invoicing management Sales tax or VAT preparation
Accounts payable and bill payment Cash flow management
Bank and credit card reconciliation Cleanup and catch-up bookkeeping
Monthly financial reports Receipt management and expense tracking

Step 3: Set up the business legally

Set up the business before your first client pays you. The exact steps differ by country, so use this list as a checklist to take to a local professional, not as legal advice.

Choose a business name and check that the domain is free. Choose a business structure. In the United States, the common choices are a sole proprietorship or a limited liability company, known as an LLC. The US Small Business Administration explains how to choose a business structure and the trade-offs of each option. A sole proprietorship works to start, and many bookkeepers move to an LLC once they are established, for liability protection. If you are in the United States and you form an LLC, you can apply for an employer identification number, known as an EIN, free from the IRS. The IRS explains how to apply for an EIN online.

Register your business name and get any required business licences. Open a separate business bank account immediately, and never mix personal and business finances. Get professional liability insurance, also called errors and omissions insurance, which protects you if a client claims your bookkeeping caused them financial harm. For a solo bookkeeper it typically costs 300 to 800 US dollars a year. Set up a dedicated workspace with a reliable computer, high-speed internet, a quiet place for client calls, and a lockable place to store any physical documents clients give you. A second monitor is strongly recommended, because it makes bookkeeping work much faster.

Step 4: Choose your software and tools

Learn QuickBooks Online or Xero, because most clients use one of these. Get certified in both through their free partner programmes, which also give you discounts and free subscriptions. You will need your own QuickBooks Online Accountant subscription, which is free for ProAdvisors, to manage multiple client files from one dashboard.

You also need a system to track your clients, jobs, and deadlines. A practice management tool covers job management, a client portal for document sharing, invoicing, and e-signatures, so you do not need separate tools for each job. For receipt and document capture, tools such as Dext or Hubdoc automate the work: clients photograph receipts, the tool extracts the data, and it feeds into QuickBooks Online or Xero. That saves hours per client per month.

Step 5: Price your services and build packages

Avoid hourly billing for ongoing work. It punishes efficiency and creates unpredictable income. Instead, charge a fixed monthly fee based on transaction volume and complexity. Use hourly rates only for cleanup and catch-up work, where the time needed is unknown.

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Suppose you price by client size. A sole trader with under 50 transactions a month might pay 250 to 400 US dollars a month. A small business with 50 to 150 transactions might pay 400 to 800 dollars. A growing business with 150 to 500 transactions might pay 800 to 1,500 dollars. A mid-size business with over 500 transactions might pay 1,500 to 3,000 dollars or more. Add-ons such as payroll at 100 to 300 dollars a month, or cleanup work at 75 to 150 dollars an hour, are charged separately.

Start new clients on an hourly rate for the first month so you can measure how long their books take, then move them to a retainer once you have real data on the time commitment. That first month on hourly rates is the safest way to learn how to start a bookkeeping business online without guessing at retainer prices. Guessing at a retainer price up front could mean making very little per hour on a flat-fee client whose books turn out messier than expected. Review prices every 6 to 12 months and increase them where the work has grown.

Step 6: Find your first clients

Your first clients almost always come from people you know. Tell everyone, friends, family, former colleagues, and social media, that you have started a bookkeeping business, and be specific about who you help. Offer a founding client rate, a modest discount in exchange for a testimonial later, and join local business groups such as chambers of commerce and networking groups.

Then build a system, because referrals alone will not fill a client list:

  • Warm outreach first: contact every business owner you know personally and ask if they know anyone struggling with their books. Referrals convert faster than cold leads.
  • Accountant partnerships: accountants and CPAs are excellent referral partners, because they need bookkeepers to clean up client files before tax season. Introduce yourself and explain your niche.
  • Google Business Profile: set it up, optimise it, and collect reviews from your first clients, because local business owners often search for a bookkeeper near them, even for a service that is entirely remote.
  • LinkedIn: share bookkeeping tips, tax deadline reminders, and business insights regularly, especially in your niche language, which filters for the right leads.
  • Targeted outreach: direct outreach to businesses in your chosen niche works better than broad cold outreach, because you can address their specific challenges from the first message.
  • A lead magnet: a checklist such as "10 tax deductions small businesses miss" captures emails from owners who are not ready to hire yet but will be eventually.

Most new bookkeepers land their first client within 4 to 8 weeks with active marketing, and your timeline depends on your outreach volume, your niche, and your local market. For more on winning early buyers, read how to get your first customers for an online business.

Step 7: Onboard clients properly

A smooth onboarding process sets the tone and prevents most disputes. For each new client:

  1. Send an engagement letter, which is a contract outlining services and terms, and get it signed.
  2. Collect their business details, accounting software access, and bank account information.
  3. Connect their accounts to QuickBooks Online or Xero.
  4. Do a cleanup of any backlogged transactions, and charge separately for this.
  5. Schedule a kickoff call to understand their business.
  6. Begin regular bookkeeping services, and send a welcome email with expectations about what you need, when, and how to send it.

Retention often comes down to onboarding. Prepare prior financial statements, bank access, and a written scope of work before you touch a single transaction. Clients who feel surprised by what is included or excluded in their monthly fee are the ones who tend to leave after just a few months.

Step 8: Systemise and grow

Even as a solo bookkeeper, document your processes from the start. Write a standard bookkeeping workflow with step-by-step instructions for each client monthly work, a client onboarding checklist, a document request checklist per service type, and a month-end closing checklist. These processes become critical when you hire your first employee or contractor, and they are what let you scale without drowning.

Raise prices when you are consistently working more hours than your pricing allows, and review pricing every 6 to 12 months. Hire when you are consistently turning away work or working 50 or more hours a week. Your first hire is typically a part-time bookkeeper who takes over production work so you can focus on client relationships and growth. Our guide on how to hire your first employee for an online business explains the first hire decision in more detail.

As the client list grows, keep learning. Tax laws change, software updates, and best practices evolve, so budget 2 to 4 hours a month for professional development. Free peer communities, such as bookkeeping groups on Reddit and the QuickBooks Online Accountant community, are good for quick questions and referrals. In the US, the SCORE network offers free mentoring for any small business, including bookkeeping firms.

You can be registered and ready for a first client in a few weeks. Building to a full-time income usually takes six to eighteen months, depending on your niche, your network, and how consistently you market. A realistic timeline is 1 to 2 clients in months 3 to 4, 4 to 6 steady clients by month 6, and 8 to 12 clients by month 12. Results vary with your niche, your prices, and your effort.

Common mistakes

  • Underpricing to get clients, which attracts difficult clients and makes the business unsustainable.
  • Taking every client regardless of fit, including clients with chaotic records and unrealistic expectations.
  • Neglecting written agreements, so scope creep destroys profitability.
  • Ignoring continuing education, because tax laws change and software updates.
  • Failing to systemise, so doing everything manually and differently for each client creates chaos as you grow.
  • Starting hourly with every client and never moving them to a retainer.
  • Doing tax work you are not qualified for, instead of referring it to an accountant.

Risks and scams to watch for

The main risk in bookkeeping is an error that costs a client money: a miscategorised transaction, a missed bill, or a wrong report. Professional liability insurance covers the claim, but prevention is better: reconcile every account every month, double-check the month-end close, and never rush a client file. Keep a clear audit trail in the software, so any number can be traced back to its source.

Watch for clients who ask you to hide income, inflate expenses, or prepare false records. Refuse those jobs immediately, because the legal risk lands on you. Watch for payment risk too: take payment in advance each month, and pause work politely if an invoice goes unpaid.

Be wary of anyone selling a bookkeeping course that promises a full client list for a large upfront fee. No course can guarantee clients, and the honest path is certification, outreach, and steady delivery. Also be wary of "free" software trials that ask for bank details before you have tested the product, and of clients who want you to work in their personal bank account rather than through the accounting software.

Frequently asked questions

Do you need a degree to start a bookkeeping business?

No. Bookkeeping is an unregulated profession in most countries. You need skills, not credentials. Many successful virtual bookkeepers are self-taught or completed short certification programmes. Clients care about accuracy and reliability, not diplomas, but a QuickBooks ProAdvisor or Xero certification builds trust quickly.

How much does it cost to start a bookkeeping business?

You can start for under 2,000 US dollars if you already have a computer. The main costs are business registration at 50 to 300 dollars, professional liability insurance at 300 to 800 dollars a year, and software at 50 to 200 dollars a month. You can learn the skills with free training, so software and insurance are usually the only fixed costs.

How do I get my first bookkeeping client?

Most first clients come from your existing network: former colleagues, local small businesses, and referrals from accountants who do not offer bookkeeping. Pick a niche, tell everyone exactly who you help, and offer a founding-client rate or a paid cleanup to get started.

Is a bookkeeping business profitable?

Yes, because costs are low and the work is recurring. A part-time bookkeeper with 5 to 10 clients can earn 15,000 to 50,000 US dollars a year, and a full-time solo bookkeeper with 15 to 30 clients can earn 60,000 to 150,000 dollars a year, before tax and expenses.

How long does it take to start a bookkeeping business?

You can be registered and ready for a first client in a few weeks. Building to a full-time income usually takes six to eighteen months, depending on your niche, your network, and how consistently you market. The setup is fast; the client base is what takes time.

What is the difference between a bookkeeper and an accountant?

A bookkeeper records what happened: categorising transactions, reconciling accounts, and producing reports. An accountant interprets the numbers, gives tax advice, and files tax returns. Preparing or filing tax returns for clients often requires a separate credential, so refer tax work to a qualified accountant.

Conclusion

Learning how to start a bookkeeping business online comes down to a clear sequence. Learn the fundamentals and get certified for free, choose a niche and a service model, set the business up properly with insurance and a separate bank account, and price monthly packages by transaction volume. Then find your first clients through your network and accountant referrals, onboard them with a written agreement and a clear scope, and systemise the monthly work so you can grow without drowning.
