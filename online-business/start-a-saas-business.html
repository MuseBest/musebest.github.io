How to Start a SaaS Business in 2026

Learn how to start a SaaS business step by step: find a painful problem, validate it, price it, build a small MVP, and win your first paying customers.

Learning how to start a SaaS business is less about coding and more about order. Most new founders build first and look for customers later. That order fails. The founders who last find a painful problem, check that people will pay to solve it, and only then build a small product. This guide shows you that order, step by step, for a beginner founder, mainly one who is bootstrapping alone or with a small team.

What a SaaS business is, in plain words

SaaS stands for software as a service. It means customers pay a regular fee, usually monthly or yearly, to use software that runs in a web browser or an app. They do not buy the software once and own it. They rent access to it, and they can cancel when they stop finding it useful.

Think of tools you may already use. A team pays a monthly fee for project software. A small shop pays a monthly fee for an online booking tool. A freelancer pays a monthly fee for invoicing software. In each case, the business earns money every month from the same customers, as long as those customers stay.

That repeat income is the main attraction. It is also the main risk. If customers leave faster than new ones join, the business shrinks even while sales look healthy. The key measure is monthly recurring revenue, usually shortened to MRR, which is the total of all active monthly subscriptions. The second key measure is churn, which is the share of customers who cancel in a given month. A SaaS business lives or dies on the balance between the two.

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology defines cloud computing as on demand access to shared computing resources, and SaaS is one way software is delivered through that model. You can read the definition on the NIST Computer Security Resource Center. You do not need the technical detail to start, but it helps to know that SaaS is a delivery model, not a type of product. Almost any repeated task can become a SaaS product if people will pay monthly to have it done well.

Is a SaaS business right for you

A SaaS business suits a founder who enjoys solving one problem deeply and serving the same customers for years. It does not suit someone who wants fast income or who dislikes talking to customers. The honest timeline is months, not weeks, from idea to first paying customers, and longer still to steady income.

Point The honest version
Time to first paying customer Often 3 to 6 months of part time work, longer if you are learning to build as you go
Money needed to start Low if you build it yourself with no code tools, higher if you hire a developer
Skills needed Talking to customers, clear writing, basic product thinking, and either coding or no code building
Best part Repeat monthly income from customers who stay
Hardest part Finding customers. Distribution is harder than building
Risk Churn can erase growth, and a bigger rival can copy a simple idea

Ask yourself three questions before you go further. First, can you name one group of people with one painful, repeated problem? Second, can you reach at least 10 of those people to talk to them? Third, are you willing to spend months on this before it pays? If any answer is no, a SaaS business may not be the right first business. A service business, where you sell your time and skill directly, usually pays faster. Read how to start an online business as a beginner if you want to compare the two paths first.

Step 1: Find a painful problem for one named audience

Start with a problem, not an idea. An idea is a guess about a product. A problem is a pain someone already feels and already pays, in money or in time, to ease. The guides that rank for this topic all agree on this point, and they are right.

Name your audience in one sentence. The sentence has this shape: a specific group of people who struggle with a specific repeated task. For example: "Independent gym owners who spend hours each week chasing late membership payments." That sentence names the audience, the task, and the pain. Compare it with a weak version: "Software for small businesses." That names no one and no pain, so it cannot guide a single decision.

Pick an audience you can reach and understand. That focus is the first real answer to how to start a SaaS business, because every later choice follows from it. Your own work history is a good place to look. If you worked in a dental clinic, you know what frustrates dental receptionists. If you ran a small shop, you know what eats a shop owner's evenings. That knowledge is worth more than a clever idea in a market you have never entered.

Write the problem in the customer's words, not yours. A customer says, "I lose two hours every Friday copying bookings into a spreadsheet." They do not say, "I need a cloud based scheduling platform." Your job is to hear the first sentence and build for it.

Step 2: Validate before you build

Validation means checking that real people will pay for the fix, before you spend weeks building it. It is the step most beginners skip, and the guides that rank best all treat it as the most important step. Aim for at least 10 interviews with people in your named audience before you write any code.

Ask about their current process, not about your idea. Good questions are: "Walk me through the last time this happened." "What do you do now to deal with it?" "What does it cost you when it goes wrong?" "Have you paid for anything to fix it?" Bad questions are: "Would you use an app that did this?" People say yes to be polite, and politeness is not a sale.

Then run a payment test. Build a simple landing page that describes the product, the price, and the result, and ask visitors to join a waitlist or pay a deposit for early access. A waitlist of 50 names is a weak signal. Five people paying a small deposit is a strong one. Money is the only honest vote. For the full method, read how to validate a business idea.

Watch for three warning signs. If people will not give you 20 minutes to talk, they do not feel the pain. If they love the idea but will not pay a deposit, the pain is not worth money to them. If every interview points to a different problem, your audience is too broad. In each case, narrow the audience or pick a sharper problem before you build.

Step 3: Choose your model and price before the MVP

Choose your pricing model before you build, because the model shapes what you build. A product that charges per seat needs team features. A product that charges by usage needs metering. A free tier needs a clear upgrade path. Decide first, then build only what the model needs.

These are the common models. A flat monthly fee is simplest: one price, every feature included. Tiered pricing offers two or three plans, usually named Starter, Growth, and Business, with more features at each step. Per seat pricing charges for each user, which suits team tools. Usage based pricing charges for what the customer consumes, such as messages sent or reports run, which suits tools with clear volume. Freemium gives a free plan with limits and charges for more, which can grow fast but needs many free users to pay off. A free trial, usually 7 to 14 days, lets people test the paid plan before they pay.

Most beginner SaaS products should start with simple tiered pricing and a free trial. It is easy to explain, easy to buy, and easy to change later. Do not copy a large rival's pricing. Their tiers fit their scale, not yours.

Worked example: how three tiers turn into monthly revenue

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. They show how the maths works.

Imagine a tool for independent gym owners, with three tiers. Starter costs 19 US dollars a month. Growth costs 49 US dollars a month. Business costs 99 US dollars a month. Your target is 5,000 US dollars of monthly recurring revenue, which is the total of all active monthly subscriptions.

Now suppose you reach 122 paying customers, split like this:

Tier Price per month Customers Monthly revenue
Starter 19 US dollars 50 950 US dollars
Growth 49 US dollars 60 2,940 US dollars
Business 99 US dollars 12 1,188 US dollars
Total 122 5,078 US dollars

Recheck the maths. 50 times 19 equals 950. 60 times 49 equals 2,940. 12 times 99 equals 1,188. Add them: 950 plus 2,940 equals 3,890, plus 1,188 equals 5,078 US dollars a month. So 122 customers at these prices pass the 5,000 US dollar target.

The lesson is not the total. It is the mix. Most revenue comes from the middle tier, so that tier must hold the features your best customers want. It is also a warning: if churn takes 10 customers a month and you add only 10, revenue stays flat. Growth comes from adding more customers than you lose, every month. For a deeper pricing method, read how to price digital products.

Step 4: Choose how to build it

There are four common build routes, and the right one depends on your skills, your budget, and your timeline. There is no best route, only the route that gets a working product in front of paying customers soonest.

Build route Who it suits Cost guide Main risk
Code it yourself You already code, or you are willing to learn slowly Low money cost, high time cost Months pass before any customer sees it
No code tools You want to launch in weeks and the product is simple About 25 to 100 US dollars a month for tools The tool may limit you as the product grows
Hire a freelancer You have a budget and a clear, small scope Often 2,000 to 10,000 US dollars for a small MVP You pay for changes when the scope moves
Find a technical co founder You can sell and your partner can build Shared ownership, low cash cost A bad partnership is slow and painful to fix

If you cannot code, start with no code tools and a narrow scope. Tools such as Bubble, Softr, and Glide can build a working product for many simple ideas, and AI coding assistants can help a beginner build faster, but AI written code still needs careful review before real customers rely on it. Do not hire a developer to build a large product before validation. You will pay to build the wrong thing, carefully.

Pick a starter tech stack and stop there. A common starter stack is: a no code builder or a simple web framework for the product, a payment provider such as Stripe or Paddle for billing, an email tool with a free tier for onboarding messages, and a simple analytics tool to see where users stop. Do not over engineer. One database, one payment provider, and one way to talk to users is enough to start. You can change tools later. You cannot get back months spent choosing tools.

Step 5: Build an MVP that does one job

An MVP is a minimum viable product. It is the smallest version that solves one problem completely for one audience. The word "completely" matters. A half finished tool that does five things badly helps no one. A small tool that does one job well earns its first payments.

Scope the MVP with a checklist, and cut everything that is not on it:

  • One audience, named in a sentence.
  • One core workflow, done end to end.
  • Sign up, payment, and the core job itself.
  • A way for users to contact you when it breaks.
  • Basic security, such as passwords and data backups.

Leave out the rest for now: team features, integrations, a mobile app, advanced settings, and a beautiful marketing site. Each can wait until paying customers ask for it.

Build in small loops. Show a working version to your interview list every one or two weeks, watch them use it, and fix what confuses them. A feature users ignore is a feature you should remove. A step users stumble on is the next thing to fix. This loop is the real work of the MVP stage, and it matters more than the code.

Set a hard deadline. A solo founder working part time should aim to have a testable MVP in 4 to 8 weeks. If the scope needs longer, the scope is too big. Cut it in half and launch the smaller version.

Step 6: Set up the business basics and billing

Set up the business in parallel with building, not after launch. The exact steps differ by country, so treat this section as a checklist to take to a local professional, not as legal advice.

  • Choose a business structure. In the United States, the common choices are a sole proprietorship or a limited liability company, known as an LLC. The US Small Business Administration explains business structures on its site, and you can read the guide on the US Small Business Administration website. Other countries have their own entity types, and the names and rules differ.
  • Register the business name and get any local tax numbers your country requires.
  • Open a separate business bank account, so business money and personal money never mix.
  • Set up accounting from day one, even if it is a simple spreadsheet at first.
  • Write basic terms of service and a privacy policy. These describe what the product does, what data you collect, and what happens when a customer cancels.
  • Get contracts for anything you outsource, and keep records of every payment.

Billing deserves special care, because it is where SaaS businesses quietly lose money. Choose a payment provider that supports recurring billing in your country and your customers' countries. Set up automatic retries for failed payments, because cards expire and banks decline payments, and a polite retry often saves the customer. Give customers a self service page where they can update their card, change plan, or cancel. Make cancelling easy. A customer who cancels cleanly may return. A customer who feels trapped will warn others away.

Taxes on digital sales differ by country and by where your customers live. Some places charge sales tax or VAT on software subscriptions. Your payment provider may handle some of this, but the responsibility is yours. Ask a local accountant before your first sale, not after your first tax bill.

Step 7: Launch in stages and get your first 10 paying customers by hand

Launch in stages, not with one big announcement. Start with a private test for your interview list. Fix the problems they find. Then open to a small beta group, perhaps 20 to 50 people, who pay a reduced price in exchange for honest feedback. Then launch in public, once real customers are already paying and the product works for them.

Your first 10 paying customers should be won by hand. That means personal emails, calls, and messages to the people you interviewed, one by one. Paul Graham, a founder of the startup fund Y Combinator, calls this doing things that do not scale, and his essay explains why manual early work is normal for successful startups. You can read it on Paul Graham's website. It is slow, and it works, because the first customers teach you what the product must do.

For each of the first 10, do the work yourself. Set up their account for them. Import their data for them. Watch them use the product and ask what confused them. Write down every question, because each question is a missing feature, a missing help page, or a missing onboarding step.

After the first 10, add one marketing channel at a time. Common first channels are: helpful content that answers your audience's real questions, a short demo video, partnerships with tools your audience already uses, and posts in communities where your audience already gathers. Pick the channel where your audience already spends time. Do not try all of them at once.

What it costs, and your first 90 days

Costs vary widely by country and by build route, so treat these figures as planning ranges for a solo founder, not as quotes. Anyone learning how to start a SaaS business should count the time cost first, because time is the largest cost. The point is that a simple SaaS product can start for little money if you build it yourself.

Cost Planning range Notes
Domain name About 10 to 20 US dollars a year Buy only the name you will use
Hosting and product tools About 10 to 50 US dollars a month No code tools sit at the higher end of this range
Payment provider No monthly fee, a percentage of each sale Check the provider's fee page for your country
Email tool Free tier to about 20 US dollars a month Free tiers cover small lists
Business registration Varies by country, often 0 to a few hundred US dollars Check your local rules
Your time The largest cost, often 10 to 20 hours a week for months Count it, even though no invoice arrives
First month total if you build it yourself About 50 to 150 US dollars, plus your time Before any developer fees

If you hire a freelancer to build the MVP, add 2,000 to 10,000 US dollars to that total. If you hire an agency, the cost is higher still. That is why validation comes first: it protects the largest spend.

Your first 90 days

Period Focus What done looks like
Days 1 to 30 Problem and validation 10 interviews done, problem written in customer words, landing page live, 5 deposits or pre payments
Days 31 to 60 MVP build One core workflow works end to end, billing works, 5 test users can complete the core job
Days 61 to 90 Launch and first customers Beta group using the product, first 10 paying customers won by hand, first churn and activation numbers tracked

Track only three numbers at first. Activation is the share of new signups who complete the core job. MRR is the total of active monthly subscriptions. Churn is the share of customers who cancel each month. If activation is low, the product confuses people. If churn is high, the product does not hold its value. Fix those before you spend money on marketing.

Bootstrap or raise funding

Bootstrapping means funding the business from your own money and from customer payments. Raising funding means taking money from investors in exchange for a share of the company. Most solo founders should bootstrap first, and the table shows why.

Choice What changes Best when
Bootstrap You keep full control, growth is slower, and you must earn money early The product can reach paying customers cheaply and you can work part time
Raise funding You grow faster but give up a share and answer to investors The market is large, the build is costly, and speed matters more than control
Pre sell or paid pilot Customers fund the build through deposits or annual payments You have a named audience that will pay before the product is finished

Funding does not fix a weak product. It speeds up whatever exists, good or bad. A funded startup with no customers runs out of money faster than a bootstrapped one. Prove that people will pay first, then decide whether outside money would help.

Common mistakes

  • Building for months before talking to a single customer.
  • Choosing a broad audience, such as "small businesses", instead of one named group.
  • Adding features before the core job works well.
  • Pricing by copying a large rival instead of testing your own tiers.
  • Hiding the price until the sales call, which wastes everyone's time.
  • Ignoring churn while celebrating new signups.
  • Spending on ads before the product keeps its first customers.
  • Quitting a job before the product has paying customers and a cash reserve.

Risks and scams to watch for

Churn is the biggest risk. If 10 percent of customers cancel each month, you must replace a tenth of your customer base every month just to stay still. That is why onboarding matters: the first days decide whether a customer stays. Watch activation closely, ask cancelling customers why they leave, and fix the top reason before adding features.

Distribution is the second risk. A good product with no audience earns nothing. If you have no way to reach your audience, the business will stall no matter how good the product is. Build the audience alongside the product, through helpful content and direct conversations, not after launch.

Ignore hype. No course can guarantee monthly recurring revenue, and no paid template can guarantee product market fit. Product market fit means enough customers value the product enough to pay for it and stay. Anyone who promises it for a fee is selling hope, not a method. Be wary of "gurus" who show income screenshots without proof, and never pay for a "starter kit" that promises a ready made SaaS business.

Frequently asked questions

How much money do I need to start a SaaS business?

A solo founder who builds with no code tools can start for about 50 to 150 US dollars in the first month, plus their time. Hiring a freelancer for the MVP usually costs 2,000 to 10,000 US dollars. The honest answer depends on your build route, your country, and how much of the work you do yourself.

Do I need to know how to code to start a SaaS business?

No. No code tools can build many simple SaaS products, and you can hire a developer or find a technical co founder. Coding helps, because you can fix small problems yourself, but it is not a requirement. Customer understanding matters more than code at the start.

How long does it take to start a SaaS business?

Most solo founders take 3 to 6 months of part time work to reach their first paying customers. Validation takes 2 to 4 weeks, and a small MVP takes 4 to 8 weeks. Reaching steady income usually takes much longer, often a year or more, and many products never reach it.

Should I quit my job to start a SaaS business?

No, not at first. Keep your job while you validate the idea and build the MVP. Quit only when paying customers and a cash reserve make the risk manageable. A steady income lets you make calm decisions instead of desperate ones.

Do I need funding to start a SaaS business?

No. Most SaaS businesses start bootstrapped, funded by the founder's savings and early customer payments. Funding can speed growth later, but it is not needed to start, and it does not fix a product that customers do not want.

What is the hardest part of starting a SaaS business?

Finding and keeping customers is harder than building the product. Most founders enjoy building and avoid selling, so distribution becomes the bottleneck. If you solve distribution, through direct conversations, helpful content, and partnerships, the rest becomes easier.

Conclusion: how to start a SaaS business in 2026

That is how to start a SaaS business: find one painful problem for one named audience, check that people will pay to solve it, price the fix before you build it, build a small MVP that does one job well, and win your first 10 customers by hand. Track activation, monthly recurring revenue, and churn, and fix the weakest number before you add features. Expect months of work before steady income, and do not believe anyone who promises fast guaranteed revenue.
