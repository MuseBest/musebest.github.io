How to Sell a Website or Online Business in 2026

Learning how to sell a website or online business starts with one honest question: what is it really worth? You built something over months or years, and it is easy to value it by the work you put in. Buyers do not pay for your effort. They pay for profit, for traffic they can keep, and for a business they can run without you. This guide shows you how to value it honestly, prepare clean records, choose the right way to sell, and close safely without being scammed. It is for owners of content sites, ecommerce stores, newsletters, and small online businesses who want to sell.

Should you sell now?

Before you value the business, ask whether now is the right time to sell. A business sells for more when its profit and traffic are stable or growing, not when they are falling. If revenue has dropped for three months in a row, buyers will ask why, and they will pay less. If you can fix the problem first, the sale price may be higher than the cost of the fix. Selling at a peak can also be a mistake, because a peak month is not a normal month, and buyers value the business on 12 months of profit, not on the best month. If your traffic spiked because of one viral post, wait until you have a few normal months to show, or be ready to explain the spike honestly. Give yourself 30 to 90 days to prepare. Use that time to clean up your records, fix the obvious risks, and gather the proof buyers will ask for. A rushed listing with messy records sells for less and takes longer, because buyers cannot trust what they cannot check.

Step 1: Work out what it is worth

Buyers value a business on profit, not on revenue. A site that earns 10,000 dollars a month in revenue but spends 9,500 dollars to run it is worth far less than a site that earns 5,000 dollars a month and spends 1,000 dollars. The number buyers use is called seller discretionary earnings, or SDE. SDE is the profit left after you add back the costs that belong to you, not to the business. To work out SDE, start with net profit for the last 12 months. Then add back your own salary or owner's draw, personal expenses that were run through the business, and one-off costs that will not repeat, such as a website redesign. Be honest with the add-backs, because a buyer will check them, and a made-up add-back destroys trust.

The valuation formula used by Empire Flippers is 12 months of total net profit times a multiple, typically 1.7 to 5 plus. Empire Flippers says SDE is the most common method for small businesses up to around 5 million dollars in annual revenue, and that a valuation is not necessarily the listing price or the sale price. You can read the detail on their online business valuation calculator. The multiple depends on the business type, its age, its growth, and its risks. Worked example only: these numbers are made up and are not typical results. Imagine a content site with 24,000 dollars of net profit in the last 12 months. The owner paid themselves 6,000 dollars in that year, and spent 2,000 dollars on a one-off redesign. Add those back: 24,000 plus 6,000 plus 2,000 equals 32,000 dollars of SDE. At a multiple of 3, the value is 32,000 times 3, which equals 96,000 dollars. At a multiple of 2, the value is 64,000 dollars. At a multiple of 4, the value is 128,000 dollars. The range is wide, and that is the point: the multiple is a judgement, not a fact, and a valuation tool gives an estimate, not a guaranteed price. Do not value the business on a best month, on revenue, or on a multiple you read in a guide that does not explain its maths. One competitor guide gives a multiple table and then a worked example that contradicts its own table, so its figures cannot be trusted.

Step 2: Prepare clean records

Buyers trust records, not claims. Before you list, gather 12 to 24 months of proof. A buyer who cannot verify your numbers will either walk away or offer far less.

Record What it proves Where to get it
Profit and loss statements Real income and real costs, month by month Your accounting tool or your bank statements
Revenue by source Where the money comes from, such as ads, affiliates, or sales Ad network, affiliate, and payment dashboards
Traffic data How many visitors come, and from where Analytics and Search Console
Expense list What the business costs to run each month Bank statements and receipts
Ownership proof That you own the domain, content, and accounts Domain registrar and account settings

Separate personal and business finances before you list. If your personal spending is mixed into the business accounts, the buyer cannot tell what the business really earns, and your add-backs will look like guesses. Open a separate account if you have not already, and keep it clean for at least a few months before you sell. For the basics of keeping business money separate, read freelance taxes for beginners. Keep the records in one folder, with file names a buyer can understand. When a buyer asks for proof, send it fast, because a seller who takes a week to find a traffic report looks disorganised, and a disorganised seller gets lower offers.

Step 3: Fix the value gaps buyers discount

Buyers pay less for risk, and five risks come up in almost every sale. Fix what you can before you list, and be honest about what you cannot fix.

Risk Why buyers discount it What to do
Single traffic source One Google update or one platform change can cut traffic Build a second source, such as email or social media
Single customer or advertiser Losing one buyer can halve the income Win more customers, or show the income is spread out
Owner does everything The business stops if you leave Write simple guides for the main tasks, and train a helper
Declining revenue Buyers expect the fall to continue Find the cause, fix it, and show a few stable months
Messy code or unclear content ownership The buyer cannot tell what they own or what will break Clean up the code, and confirm you own the content

You cannot fix every risk in 90 days, and that is fine. A buyer will accept a known risk if the price reflects it. What buyers will not accept is a hidden risk they discover during due diligence, because that breaks trust and often ends the sale. List the risks in your own notes, and be ready to talk about them openly.

Step 4: Decide what is included in the sale

Write down exactly what the buyer gets, before you list. A vague listing leads to arguments later, when the buyer expects something you never meant to sell. Most sales include the domain, the website content, the code, the hosting account or the files to move it, the social media accounts, the email list, the analytics and Search Console access, the ad network and affiliate accounts, and any contracts with writers, suppliers, or customers. Say clearly what does not transfer. A personal social media account, a licence you cannot transfer, or a contract that ends when you leave should be named early, not discovered after the buyer has paid. Check each account's rules on transfer. Some ad networks and affiliate programmes do not allow an account to change owner, so the buyer may need to apply for their own account. Tell the buyer this in the listing, so they can plan for it.

Step 5: Choose how to sell

There are three main routes, and the right one depends on the size of the business and how much work you want to do.

Route How it works Strengths Drawbacks
Open marketplace You list the business, buyers bid or make offers Low cost to list, many buyers see it You handle questions and due diligence yourself
Curated marketplace or broker A broker values, lists, and manages the sale for you Professional help, vetted buyers, higher trust Fees are higher, and the process is slower
Direct sale You sell to a buyer you already know, such as a competitor No listing fees, and a fast close is possible Fewer buyers, so the price may be lower

Fees change, so check them before you list. Brokers typically charge a commission of 10 to 15 percent of the sale price, according to figures quoted by brokers themselves. Treat that as a typical range quoted by brokers, not as a verified market fact, and confirm the exact fee in writing before you agree. Marketplace fees vary by platform and by sale price, so read the fee page for the marketplace you choose. Escrow fees also vary, and they were not verified in this guide, so check the escrow provider's fee page before you agree terms. Do not agree a sale without knowing what you will pay and what you will keep. Small sites often suit an open marketplace, because the fees are low and the buyer pool is large. Larger businesses often suit a broker, because the broker handles the valuation, the buyer checks, and the negotiation.

Step 6: Write a listing that answers buyer questions

A good listing answers the questions a buyer will ask, so you get fewer time-wasting enquiries. Include the business model in one paragraph, the age of the business, the profit for the last 12 months, the traffic sources, the owner hours per week, what is included, and why you are selling. Buyers distrust a seller who hides the reason for selling, so give a real one, such as wanting to focus on another project, or needing the money for a new venture. Keep the site address private until a buyer is qualified. A public listing that names the site lets competitors, advertisers, and strangers see your income and your traffic, and it can attract fake buyers. Most marketplaces let you hide the domain and share it only with buyers who have signed a non-disclosure agreement and shown proof of funds. Use that option. Use screenshots of your dashboards only when the buyer asks for them, and never send a screenshot as the only proof, because a screenshot can be faked. Offer read-only access to analytics and to your ad or affiliate dashboards instead, after the buyer is qualified.

Step 7: Qualify buyers and handle due diligence

Due diligence is the buyer checking that your claims are true. It is normal, and it is not a sign of distrust. The buyer will ask for financial records, traffic data, and access to the accounts that earn money. They may ask for a video call to walk through the business. Answer clearly and fast, because slow answers make buyers nervous. Qualify buyers before you share sensitive details. Ask three questions: have they bought a business like this before, do they have the funds or a loan approved, and what is their plan for running it? A buyer who cannot answer these is not ready, and sharing your records with them wastes your time. Ask serious buyers to sign a non-disclosure agreement before you share the domain name, the full financial records, or the login details. Share information in stages: first the public summary, then after a non-disclosure agreement the financial records and the domain name, then after a serious offer read-only access to analytics and revenue accounts. Do not give anyone full login access before the sale is agreed and the escrow is open, because that access can be misused.

Step 8: Negotiate terms, not just price

The price is only one part of the deal. The terms decide how much you actually receive, and when. A higher price paid in instalments over two years may be worth less than a lower price paid in full on the day of the sale, because of the risk that the buyer stops paying.

Term What it means What to watch for
Upfront payment Money paid when the sale closes The safest option, and the one most sellers prefer
Instalments The price is paid in parts over months Ask what happens if the buyer misses a payment
Earn-out Extra money is paid if the business hits a target after the sale Agree the target in writing, and who measures it
Transition support You help the buyer for a set time after the sale Agree the hours and the length, such as 30 days
Non-compete You agree not to start a competing business for a set time Agree the time, the topic, and the area it covers

Work out the expected value of each offer, not just the headline price. An offer of 100,000 dollars paid in full is worth more than an offer of 120,000 dollars paid over two years with an earn-out that may never be paid. Ask the buyer to put the terms in writing, and get advice before you sign, especially if the deal includes instalments or an earn-out.

Step 9: Close safely through escrow

Never accept payment outside escrow. Escrow protects both sides: the buyer pays the escrow provider, the seller transfers the business, the buyer checks it works, and only then does the escrow provider release the money to the seller. Escrow.com explains the five steps as: buyer and seller agree terms, buyer pays Escrow.com, seller transfers the business, buyer inspects and accepts it, and Escrow.com releases the funds to the seller. Money is not released until the buyer is satisfied. You can read the full detail on how escrow works. Before the transfer, work through a checklist with the buyer: transfer the domain, move the hosting or hand over the files, move the content management system and the code, hand over analytics and Search Console access, transfer the email list and the newsletter account, move the payment processor and the ad network accounts, and hand over the social accounts and the vendor contracts. Then check that everything works under the buyer's control: the site loads, the emails send, the payments arrive in the buyer's account, and the logins work. Keep a copy of the final records and the signed agreement for at least a year after the sale.

Selling a website that makes no money

A website that makes little or no money can still be worth something, but not by the profit formula. Its value comes from its assets: the domain name, the traffic, the content, and the email list. A domain that is short and easy to remember has value on its own. A site with steady traffic has value, because the buyer can monetise it. A large email list has value, because the buyer can sell to it. Explain the value honestly. Show the traffic, the content, and the list, and say clearly that the site makes no profit yet. Do not force an earnings multiple onto a site with no earnings, because the maths makes no sense and buyers will see through it. Price it on what the assets are worth to the buyer, and be ready for a lower price than a profitable site would fetch.

Scams aimed at sellers

Sellers are targets for scams, because buyers know you want to close the deal. Watch for these signs. Fake buyers ask for free access to your analytics, your code, or your ad accounts before they have shown any proof of funds. They may use that access to copy your content or to learn your methods. Only share read-only access after a non-disclosure agreement and a serious offer. Payment outside escrow is the most common scam. A buyer says escrow is too slow or too expensive, and asks you to accept a bank transfer, a crypto payment, or a payment app instead. A real buyer who is serious about a business will use escrow. If a buyer refuses escrow, walk away. Phishing is a third risk. A buyer sends a link that looks like a marketplace or an escrow login page, and asks you to sign in. The link steals your password. Only sign in through the official site, typed into your browser yourself, and turn on two-factor authentication on every account that holds money or traffic. If a buyer pressures you to decide fast, or asks you to transfer the domain before payment, stop. A real sale takes days or weeks, and the transfer happens after the money is in escrow, not before.

After the sale

The sale is not finished when the money arrives. Agree a transition support period, usually 30 days, in which you answer the buyer's questions and help them run the business. Write down how many hours you will give, and how the buyer should contact you. After that period, your help ends, unless you agree a paid consulting arrangement. Tell your customers and staff about the change, if the business has any. A short, honest message works best: the business has a new owner, the service continues, and the new owner will be in touch. Get tax advice before you agree terms, not after the sale. Tax on selling a business depends on where you live and how the business is owned, and the rules differ by country. Common mistakes

  • Pricing the business on its best month or on revenue, instead of on 12 months of profit.
  • Listing before the records are ready, so buyers cannot verify your claims.
  • Hiding risks that the buyer will find during due diligence anyway.
  • Accepting the first offer without checking the terms, especially instalments and earn-outs.
  • Agreeing to payment outside escrow, which leaves you with no protection.
  • Changing the business during the sale, such as raising prices or cutting content, which confuses buyers.
  • Sharing full login access before the sale is agreed and escrow is open.
  • Forgetting tax advice until after the deal is signed.

Frequently asked questions

How do I sell my website?

Value it from 12 months of profit, prepare clean records for 12 to 24 months, fix the risks buyers discount, choose a marketplace, a broker, or a direct sale, write a listing that answers buyer questions, and close through escrow. Do not transfer anything until the buyer's money is in escrow.

How much is my website worth?

It depends on profit, not revenue. Use seller discretionary earnings for the last 12 months, times a multiple that is typically 1.7 to 5, according to Empire Flippers. A valuation tool gives an estimate, not a guaranteed price, and the final price depends on the buyer and the terms.

Where can I sell my online business?

Open marketplaces suit smaller sites and cost less to list. Curated marketplaces and brokers suit larger businesses and give you professional help, but they charge more. A direct sale to a buyer you know can be fast, but you may get fewer offers. Check the fees for each route before you list.

How long does it take to sell a website?

It varies widely. Small sites can sell in a few weeks, and larger businesses can take a few months, including preparation, listing, due diligence, and transfer. Give yourself 30 to 90 days to prepare before you list, and do not rush the due diligence stage.

Do I need a broker to sell my website?

No. You can sell through an open marketplace or directly to a buyer you know. A broker helps with larger sales, with valuation, and with managing buyers, but you pay a commission for it. If the sale is small and your records are clean, you may not need one.

Do I pay tax when I sell my website?

It depends on where you live and how the business is owned, and the rules differ by country. Speak to a tax adviser before you agree terms, so you know what you owe and when, and so the deal is structured in the best way for you.

Conclusion: value honestly, prepare well, close through escrow

Selling a website or online business comes down to four moves: value it from 12 months of profit with a multiple you can explain, prepare records a buyer can verify, choose a sale route whose fees you understand, and close through escrow with a clear handover. Do not price on a best month, do not hide risks, and never accept payment outside escrow. A clean, honest sale takes longer to prepare, but it closes faster and for a better price.
