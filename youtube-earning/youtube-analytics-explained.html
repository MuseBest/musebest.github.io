YouTube Analytics Explained for Creators in 2026

YouTube Analytics can feel like a wall of numbers. Views, clicks, watch time, traffic, revenue, and audience charts all compete for attention, and it is hard to know which number to fix first. This guide explains YouTube Analytics in plain language, tab by tab, so you can answer two simple questions for every video. Did people click, and did they stay. Once you can answer those two questions, the rest of the dashboard becomes a tool, not a maze.

Step 1: Open Analytics in the right place

Open YouTube Studio on a computer for the fullest view. From the left menu, click Analytics for channel level reports. For one video, go to Content, point to the video, and select Analytics. The YouTube Studio app on a phone gives a shorter view, which is useful for a quick check, but not for a deep review. Use the computer view for monthly reviews and diagnosis work. The official starting point is the YouTube Help page on getting started with Analytics.

Step 2: Tour the channel tabs

Channel Analytics has five main tabs. Overview gives the headline picture: views, watch time, subscribers, and revenue over time. Content shows which videos, Shorts, live streams, and posts performed, plus impressions, clicks, and traffic sources. Audience shows who watches, where they live, when they are on YouTube, and which other channels they watch. Revenue shows earnings for Partner Programme channels, with RPM, CPM, and playback based figures. Trends shows what your audience searches for, breakout videos in your area, and content gaps, which are searches with no good, current, relevant result.

Step 3: Ask the two questions first

Every diagnosis starts with two questions. Did people click, and did they stay. The first question is about packaging: the title, the thumbnail, and the topic. The second question is about content: the opening, the pace, the value, and the ending. If you fix packaging when the problem is content, clicks will rise and exits will rise with them. If you fix content when the problem is packaging, a good video will stay unseen. Keep the two questions separate, and fix the one the data points to.

Step 4: Read click through rate fairly

Click through rate, often shortened to CTR, is clicks divided by impressions, shown as a percentage. It tells you how often people who saw the thumbnail chose to open the video. A high rate means the packaging appealed to the viewers who saw it. A low rate means the packaging did not appeal, the topic did not fit those viewers, or the thumbnail and title did not make the promise clear.

Benchmarks cause confusion, so read them with care. YouTube has reported that half of all channels and videos sit between 2 percent and 10 percent for CTR. Third party tables often label narrower bands as average or exceptional, and those bands conflict with each other. The safe method is to benchmark against your own channel average for the same traffic type, such as search traffic or browse traffic, and for videos of a similar age. A new video judged against a year old video will look unfairly weak, because older videos have had more time to find their audience.

Step 5: Read average view duration and retention

Average view duration, often shortened to AVD, is the average time viewers spend watching a video. It is often read as a share of the video length. As a rough guide from the research, about 40 percent of length counts as strong for long videos, and 50 percent or more counts as excellent, but always compare with your own similar length videos. A 2 minute video and a 20 minute video cannot be judged by the same minute count, because their viewers expect different depths.

The retention graph shows where viewers leave and where they stay. Read it in three parts. First, look at the first 30 seconds. A sharp drop there points to a weak hook or a title that overpromised. Second, look for mid video drops. A drop at one point often means a slow section, a tangent, or a repeated point. Third, look for bumps. A bump means viewers rewatched a section, which often marks a useful demo, a clear example, or a surprising result. Key moments are only shown on videos at least 60 seconds long with at least 100 views, so small videos may not show them yet.

Step 6: Read traffic sources and the healthy mix

Traffic sources tell you where viewers came from. Search traffic comes from viewers who typed a query. Browse traffic comes from the home feed and subscriptions. Suggested traffic comes from recommendations beside or after other videos. External traffic comes from sites outside YouTube. Playlist, notification, and other sources fill out the rest. Each source tells a different story about intent.

Step 7: Read the audience tabs

Audience data tells you who watches and how loyal they are. Monthly audience splits viewers into new, casual, and regular groups. New viewers are watching for the first time in the period. Casual viewers watched in 1 to 5 months of recent viewing. Regular viewers watched in 6 or more months. Returning viewers are viewers who have watched before, as opposed to new viewers in a single video report. A channel with mostly new viewers needs strong hooks and clear topics, because each video must win a fresh audience. A channel with many returning viewers can go deeper, because the audience already trusts the creator.

Step 8: Understand RPM versus CPM

CPM and RPM sound similar, but they measure different things. CPM is the advertiser number. It is the cost per 1,000 ad impressions. RPM is the creator number. It is revenue per 1,000 views, after YouTube takes its share and after counting only monetised playbacks in the right way. RPM is usually lower than CPM, because not every view shows an ad, and YouTube takes a share of ad revenue. For long form Partner Programme videos, YouTube pays 55 percent of net watch page ad revenue to the creator, with the rest going to YouTube. Check current programme terms on the official YouTube Partner Programme eligibility page. That share is a rule to check on official pages at the time you plan, because programme terms change.

Step 9: Read Shorts analytics

Shorts analytics need a different lens from long videos. Filter the Content tab to Shorts to see Shorts only. The key Shorts metric is stayed to watch, which is the Shorts equivalent of click through rate. It shows how many viewers stayed after the Short appeared, rather than swiping away. A low stayed to watch rate points to a weak opening frame or a topic that does not fit the viewers shown the Short. Completion and subscriber conversion per Short complete the picture. Step 11: Diagnose with the quadrant

The diagnosis quadrant crosses two measures: CTR above or below your own average, and retention above or below your own benchmark. It gives four clear outcomes.

CTR Retention Diagnosis Fix first
Above average Above benchmark Winner Repeat the format and topic
Below average Above benchmark Packaging problem Fix title and thumbnail
Above average Below benchmark Content or promise problem Fix opening and pace, or narrow the promise
Below average Below benchmark Both need work Fix packaging first, then retest content

Work one video at a time. Pick a video with enough impressions to judge fairly, note its quadrant, and make one fix. Then wait for new data before making the next fix. Changing title, thumbnail, opening, and topic all at once teaches you nothing, because you cannot tell which change helped. Record the diagnosis, the fix, and the date, so the next review can learn from it.

Step 12: Why some data is missing

A 20 minute monthly review

Use four blocks of 5 minutes. First, check the channel Overview for views, watch time, subscribers, and revenue, and note any sharp rise or fall. Second, check the top and bottom videos by CTR and retention, and note one packaging pattern and one content pattern. Third, check traffic sources and search terms, and note one rising topic and one falling source. Fourth, pick one action for the next month, such as fixing packaging on two videos or testing one new topic from Trends. Write the action down with a date. A review without a written action is just reading numbers.

Benchmark honesty box

Do not trust a single benchmark table as truth. YouTube has reported that half of all channels and videos sit between 2 percent and 10 percent for CTR. Third party tables use narrower bands, such as below 2 percent as weak or above 8 percent as exceptional, without showing the same source. Those tables may describe a specific set of channels, not yours. The honest benchmark is your own channel average for the same traffic type, the same content type, and videos of a similar age. Use outside bands only as a rough sense check, never as a pass or fail grade for one video.

What it costs and what to expect

YouTube Analytics is free in YouTube Studio, and no paid tool is required to use the method in this guide. Third party tools can add convenience, but they cannot add data YouTube does not show, and they should not replace the Studio numbers. The cost is time. Plan 20 minutes for a monthly review, plus 10 minutes per video diagnosis. That time pays back when it stops you from fixing the wrong problem.

Common mistakes

  • Judging a video before its data has settled.
  • Comparing lifetime totals across videos of different ages.
  • Judging CTR against a generic table instead of your own average.
  • Fixing packaging when the problem is retention, or the reverse.
  • Changing title, thumbnail, and opening all at once.
  • Treating a missing data row as zero.
  • Mistaking a measurement change for a performance change.
  • Optimising for views while ignoring watch time and subscriber conversion.

Risks and limitations

Analytics numbers are estimates in some reports, and revenue figures are finalised later, so early revenue numbers can change. Do not make spending decisions on unsettled revenue data. Also note that this guide does not cover the unread tails of two competitor guides used in research, so no claim is made here about their later sections on tools, benchmarks, or routines. Where Partner Programme rules are concerned, check the official Help pages at the time you act, because thresholds and terms change. Results vary with topic, audience, and competition, and no metric target can promise growth.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good click through rate on YouTube

There is no single good rate for every channel. YouTube has reported that half of all channels and videos sit between 2 percent and 10 percent. The fair test is your own channel average for the same traffic type and similar videos. A rate above your own average for that traffic type is good for you, even if it sits below a third party table.

A good duration depends on video length. As a rough guide, about 40 percent of length counts as strong for long videos, and 50 percent or more counts as excellent, but you should compare with your own similar length videos. A short video with a high share and a long video with a lower share can both be healthy, because viewers expect different depths.

How do I read a retention graph

Look at the first 30 seconds for hook strength, look for mid video drops that mark slow sections, and look for bumps where viewers rewatched a useful part. A sharp early drop points to the hook or an overpromised title. A mid drop points to pace or a tangent. Fix the part the graph points to, not the whole video.

Why is some Analytics data missing

Data is hidden when groups are too small to show without risking viewer privacy, and some reports never show certain splits, such as demographics for private videos or geography in real time reports. Totals can exceed the sum of visible rows because hidden rows still count. Do not treat missing rows as zero.

Check real time data lightly after publishing, then do a proper review once the numbers have settled, usually after 24 to 48 hours for most metrics. Do a full 20 minute review once a month, with one written action. Checking every hour adds stress without adding insight, because early numbers move a lot.

Conclusion

YouTube Analytics becomes simple when you ask two questions first. Did people click, and did they stay. Use the tabs to find the answer, use the quadrant to pick the fix, and use a short monthly review to keep learning. Compare each video with your own averages, note measurement changes, and act on one clear fix at a time.
