How to Start a Finance YouTube Channel and Earn Money in 2026

Learning how to start a finance YouTube channel is a good idea if you enjoy explaining money in plain language, but it carries a duty other niches do not. Your viewers will make real money decisions from your videos, so accuracy and honesty matter more than views. This guide shows you how to pick a narrow finance topic, build trust without a finance qualification, set up your channel properly, and earn money through ads, affiliates, sponsorships, and your own products, without promising income you cannot guarantee.

This guide is for beginners who want to teach budgeting, saving, investing basics, credit cards, or debt payoff on YouTube. You will learn how to choose a sub-niche, how to cite sources and date every figure, what disclaimers to use, how the YouTube Partner Program works for finance creators, and what a realistic first year looks like. For the general channel setup steps, read how to start a YouTube channel. This guide covers only what is specific to finance.

What a finance YouTube channel is, and what it is not

A finance YouTube channel teaches people about money through video: budgeting, saving, paying off debt, understanding credit cards, or explaining how investing works for a beginner. A finance channel is not a licence to give personal financial advice, and it is not a place to promise returns. General education and personalised advice are different things. Education explains how a product or idea works in general. Personalised advice tells one person what to do with their money, and that usually needs a licence and a proper understanding of that person's situation. Stay on the education side, and say so clearly in every video.

Finance is one of the highest paying niches on YouTube, because banks, brokers, and financial apps pay a lot to reach people who are about to open an account. That is a real opportunity, and it is also a risk. High paying sponsors attract creators who overstate returns, hide risks, and push products they have not checked. Your job is to be the channel that checks the figures, shows the workings, and says when a product does not suit most people.

Step 1: Pick a narrow finance sub-niche you can sustain

Do not start a channel about finance in general. Finance is too wide, and a general channel competes with large channels that have teams and budgets. Pick one narrow topic you can talk about for a year without running out of ideas, and pick one audience you can serve well.

Sub-niche Who it suits What to teach first
Budgeting for beginners People on a first salary or a tight budget A simple monthly budget, tracking spending, building an emergency fund
Saving and emergency funds People with no savings yet How much to save first, where to keep it, how to automate it
Paying off debt People with card or loan debt Debt payoff order, interest costs, avoiding new debt
Beginner investing People who have savings and want to learn How index funds work, risk, diversification, long term thinking
Credit cards People choosing a first card How interest works, fees, rewards, when a card is a bad idea
Retirement basics People in their 20s and 30s Why starting early matters, employer pensions, simple plans

Test your sub-niche before you commit. Write down 30 video ideas in that topic. If you can only think of 10, the topic is too narrow for you, or you do not know it well enough yet. Then search each idea on YouTube and read the top results. If every idea is already covered by a large channel with a better video, pick a smaller angle, such as budgeting for students, or saving on a low income.

Avoid three topics at the start: crypto trading signals, day trading promises, and any get rich quick topic. These attract the strictest scrutiny from YouTube and regulators, they attract scams, and they are the hardest to cover honestly without a qualification. Start with budgeting, saving, or debt payoff, where the advice is simple, checkable, and genuinely helpful.

Step 2: Build credibility without a finance qualification

You do not need a finance degree or a licence to teach general money education on YouTube. What you do need is a method that lets viewers check your work. First, cite a primary source for every rate, fee, and rule. A primary source is the official page of the bank, broker, or regulator, not another YouTuber's video. Show the source on screen and put the link in the video description. If you cannot find the official source, do not state the figure.

Second, show your workings. If you say a saver earns a certain amount of interest in a year, show the maths on screen: the amount saved, the rate, and the result. Third, date every figure. Rates and fees change, so say the month and year on screen and in the description, for example "checked November 2026". Fourth, say what you do not know. If a viewer asks about tax in their country and you do not know the answer, say so, and point them to their local tax authority or a qualified accountant. Fifth, correct mistakes in public. If you get a figure wrong, pin a comment with the correction, add a note in the description, and mention it in your next video.

Step 3: Set up your channel for a finance audience

Your channel page should tell a new visitor three things in ten seconds: what finance topic you cover, who it is for, and that your videos are education, not personal advice. Write that into your channel name, your About section, and your banner. For example: "Simple budgeting and saving for people on a first salary. Education only, not personal financial advice."

Put a disclaimer in your About section, and repeat a short version in every video description. A plain wording you can use is: "This video is for general education only. It is not personal financial advice. Do your own research, and speak to a qualified adviser before you make a money decision." That wording is a starting point, not legal advice, and rules differ by country, so check the rules where you live. Organise your channel into playlists by sub-niche, such as Budgeting Basics, Saving, and Credit Cards Explained. Plan your first 10 videos before you publish the first one, and make them answer the most searched beginner questions in your sub-niche.

Step 4: Plan your first 30 days of finance videos

Publish two videos a week for your first month, and mix three types of video. Evergreen videos answer questions people will still ask in a year, such as how to build a budget or how an index fund works. Comparison videos help people choose, such as two savings accounts or two budgeting apps side by side. News and update videos react to a rate change or a new rule, and they must be neutral, sourced, and dated, because they go out of date fast.

A simple 30 day plan looks like this: week 1, two evergreen videos; week 2, one evergreen video and one comparison; week 3, one evergreen video and one news or update video; week 4, one comparison and one video that answers the best question from your comments. Keep each video focused on one question, and answer it in the first minute. End with one clear next step, such as a simple action the viewer can take today, and point them to the next video in your playlist.

Step 5: Make finance videos people trust

You can film on camera or make faceless videos with voiceover, stock footage, or whiteboard animation. On camera builds trust faster, because viewers can see the person behind the advice. Faceless is easier to produce, and it suits explainers with charts, but it is harder to build a loyal audience, and YouTube checks reused or low value AI content carefully at monetisation review, so a faceless channel must add real value in the script, not just read text over stock footage.

Use the same script structure either way. Hook the viewer in the first 15 seconds with the problem. State the question the video answers. Give the steps in order. Show one worked example with the maths on screen. Summarise the answer in one sentence at the end, and add the disclaimer. Keep sentences short, explain every money term the first time it appears, and never use jargon to sound clever. A term such as diversification needs one short sentence: it means spreading your money across different investments so that one bad result does not hurt you as much.

How finance channels earn money

Finance channels earn in five ways, and ads are usually the slowest to arrive. First, the YouTube Partner Program, which pays a share of ad revenue and Premium revenue. To join at the full tier, a channel needs 1,000 subscribers plus either 4,000 qualified watch hours in the last 12 months or 10 million qualified Shorts views in the last 90 days, as stated on the YouTube Partner Program overview page checked on 2026-11-11. You also need to follow the channel monetisation policies, live in a country or region where the programme is available, have no active Community Guidelines strikes, have 2-Step Verification turned on, have advanced features access, and have one active AdSense for YouTube account linked. Meeting the threshold does not guarantee acceptance, and the review typically takes about 1 month.

A lower fan funding tier also exists, at 500 subscribers with 3 valid public uploads in the last 90 days and either 3,000 qualified watch hours in 12 months or 3 million qualified Shorts views in 90 days, which unlocks memberships, Super Chat, Super Thanks, and Shopping, but no ad revenue. That tier is reported by guides citing the YouTube Help expanded programme page, and it is not stated on the overview page as opened on 2026-11-11. From February 1, 2027, YouTube announced on 10 August 2026 that new applicants for the ad revenue tier will need 8,000 qualified watch hours in the last 365 days or 20 million qualified Shorts views in the last 90 days, while the fan funding tier stays unchanged. Members must accept the updated terms in YouTube Studio by January 31, 2027. Confirm the current terms in YouTube Studio, because the overview page as opened on 2026-11-11 did not yet state the change.

Second, affiliate marketing. Finance affiliate programmes pay for referrals to bank accounts, cards, brokers, and budgeting apps. The FTC endorsement guides explain when and how to disclose a paid recommendation, and the same principle applies in most countries. Third, sponsorships. Vet the sponsor before you agree, and refuse any deal that asks you to promise a result or to hide a downside. Read how to get YouTube sponsorships before you take your first deal. Fourth, channel memberships and fan funding, which pay small recurring amounts. Fifth, digital products, such as a budgeting template.

Do not quote RPM figures from other guides as facts. Competitor guides for this topic give conflicting figures, from 10 to 60 US dollars per 1,000 views, with no named source. Your real earnings per 1,000 views depend on your viewers' country, the time of year, and advertiser demand. The worked example below uses clearly labelled example numbers only.

Disclaimers, disclosure, and compliance risks

Finance is a regulated area, and the rules differ by country. First, personalised advice in comments and live streams. A viewer will ask "Should I buy this stock?" Do not answer as if you know their situation. Give the general facts, explain the trade-offs, and tell them to speak to a qualified adviser. A live Q and A about investment planning is the riskiest format for a beginner, because you cannot check the facts before you answer, and your words are recorded. Second, promoting a broker, exchange, or crypto product. Check whether the product is legal where your viewers live, check its licence, and say plainly if you have not used it yourself. Never promote a trading signal service, a guaranteed returns scheme, or a product that pressures viewers to act fast. Third, misleading claims. Do not promise returns, do not show a chart without saying what period it covers, and do not call a product the best without evidence. A single misleading video can cost you monetisation or your channel.

Worked example: what a small finance channel could earn

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. They show how the maths works, so you can test your own figures.

Imagine a small finance channel with 5,000 subscribers and 20 long-form videos. It gets 50,000 views a month. Suppose 60 percent of those views, or 30,000 views, are on videos that carry ads, and the channel earns 8 US dollars per 1,000 ad views after YouTube's share. That is 30,000 divided by 1,000, which is 30, times 8, which is 240 US dollars a month from ads. That 8 dollar figure is an example only, not a promise.

Now add affiliates. Suppose 2 percent of the 50,000 viewers click an affiliate link in a description, which is 1,000 clicks. Suppose 1 percent of those clicks becomes a qualifying sign-up, which is 10 sign-ups. At 25 US dollars per sign-up, that is 250 US dollars. Add the two together and the channel earns about 490 US dollars in that month, before tax and before any refunds or cancelled sign-ups. If the channel grows to 200,000 views a month with the same rates, the same maths gives about 1,960 US dollars a month.

Recheck: 50,000 times 60 percent is 30,000. 30,000 divided by 1,000 is 30. 30 times 8 is 240. 50,000 times 2 percent is 1,000. 1,000 times 1 percent is 10. 10 times 25 is 250. 240 plus 250 is 490. 200,000 times 60 percent is 120,000. 120,000 divided by 1,000 is 120. 120 times 8 is 960. 200,000 times 2 percent is 4,000. 4,000 times 1 percent is 40. 40 times 25 is 1,000. 960 plus 1,000 is 1,960. The maths checks out as an illustration, not a promise.

What it costs and what to expect

Starting costs are low. A phone you already own is enough to film, a cheap lavalier microphone costs about 10 to 20 US dollars, and free editing software such as DaVinci Resolve costs nothing. A ring light costs about 20 to 40 US dollars if you need one. Your main cost is time: expect 5 to 10 hours per video when you include research, fact checking, scripting, filming, and editing. Finance videos take longer than most, because every figure must be checked against an official source.

Expect a slow start. A new finance channel usually earns nothing for six to twelve months, because it takes time to reach 1,000 subscribers and 4,000 watch hours, and longer to reach the higher bar that applies from February 1, 2027. Your first income often comes from affiliates, not ads. Results vary with your niche, your viewers' country, content quality, and how well your audience matches the products you discuss. Nothing here is a promise of income.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with a general finance channel instead of one narrow sub-niche you can sustain.
  • Stating a rate or fee without checking the official page, and without dating it.
  • Giving personalised advice in comments or live streams.
  • Hiding affiliate links, or disclosing them only in the description and never in the video.
  • Promoting a broker, exchange, or crypto product you have not checked.
  • Using a clickbait title that promises a return, such as a title that claims a fixed saving with no evidence.
  • Making faceless videos with AI voiceover and stock footage that add no real value, then failing monetisation review.
  • Publishing news videos without a date, so viewers follow old rates months later.
  • Comparing your month 3 to a large channel's year 5, and quitting too early.

Risks and scams to watch for

Watch out for sponsors that offer high payments for promoting a trading app, a crypto exchange, or a guaranteed returns scheme. A high payment is often a sign the product cannot sell on its own merits. Check the product's licence, read independent reviews, and refuse any deal that asks you to hide a risk or promise a result. Watch out for fake finance gurus who sell courses that promise a job, a licence, or income after one course. Check the syllabus, the instructor's background, and whether any certificate is recognised before you recommend it. Watch out for viewers who ask for bank details, or for schemes that ask you to recruit others for a fee. A genuine finance channel never asks viewers for bank details, and never promises income for recruiting. If a viewer reports losing money after following a video, take it seriously, correct the video if it was wrong, and point them to their local consumer protection body. Keep records of the sources you checked for every video, and set aside money for tax on any income you earn.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to be a financial expert to start a finance YouTube channel?

No, but you do need a method for staying accurate. Teach general education, cite primary sources on screen, show your workings, date every figure, and say what you do not know. You do not need a licence to explain how a budget or an index fund works in general, but you must avoid personalised advice, and you should check the rules where you live.

What disclaimer should I put on a finance YouTube channel?

Use a short, plain wording in every video and in the description, such as: "This video is for general education only. It is not personal financial advice. Do your own research, and speak to a qualified adviser before you make a money decision." Put it in your About section too, and disclose affiliate links and sponsorships separately, in the video and before the link.

How much can a finance YouTube channel earn?

It depends on views, your viewers' country, affiliate sign-ups, sponsorships, and the products you discuss. No figure is guaranteed, and competitor guides disagree widely on earnings per 1,000 views, from 10 to 60 US dollars, with no named source. Expect no income for the first six to twelve months.

How long does it take to monetise a finance YouTube channel?

You need 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 qualified watch hours in 12 months or 10 million qualified Shorts views in 90 days for the full ad revenue tier, and the review typically takes about 1 month. From February 1, 2027, new applicants need 8,000 watch hours or 20 million Shorts views instead. A lower fan funding tier at 500 subscribers unlocks memberships and Super Chat sooner, but it pays no ad revenue.

Can I use AI to make finance videos and still monetise them?

YouTube does not ban AI tools, but monetisation review checks whether a video adds real value. A video that only reads text over stock footage, with no original research, no workings, and no sources, is likely to fail review. Use AI to help with scripting and editing, but add your own research, your own examples, and your own sources.

What finance topics should a beginner avoid?

Avoid crypto trading signals, day trading promises, and any get rich quick topic at the start. These attract the strictest scrutiny from YouTube and regulators, they attract scams, and they are the hardest to cover honestly without a qualification. Start with budgeting, saving, or debt payoff, where the facts are simple and checkable.

Do I need to disclose affiliate links on a finance channel?

Yes. Disclose every affiliate link in the video itself and before the link in the description, and disclose sponsorships and free products the same way. A disclaimer that a video is education only does not replace disclosure of a paid relationship. The FTC endorsement guides explain the standard, and most countries have a similar rule.

Should I start faceless or on camera for a finance channel?

On camera builds trust faster, because viewers can see the person behind the advice, and trust matters more in finance than in most niches. Faceless works for explainers with charts, but it is harder to build a loyal audience, and it must add real value to pass monetisation review. If you are comfortable on camera, start there.

Conclusion

To start a finance YouTube channel and earn money, pick one narrow sub-niche you can sustain, build trust by citing sources, showing your workings, and dating every figure, set up your channel with a clear disclaimer and playlists, publish two useful videos a week, and earn through ads, affiliates, sponsorships, memberships, and products, in that order. Do not promise returns, do not give personalised advice, and do not quote earnings figures you cannot verify. Your first income may take a year, but a channel that is accurate and honest can earn for years.
