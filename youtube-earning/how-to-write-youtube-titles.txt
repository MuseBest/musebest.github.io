42:T3e73,<h2 id="how-to-write-youtube-titles-that-get-views-in-2026">How to Write YouTube Titles That Get Views in 2026</h2>
<p>A title is a promise in a few words. If the promise is vague, the right viewer scrolls past. If the promise is too big, the viewer clicks, feels misled, and leaves fast. Learning how to write YouTube titles means learning to make a clear, honest promise that matches the video you actually made. This guide covers intent, keywords, length, formulas, the honesty check, testing, and Shorts titles, so you can write titles that earn clicks from viewers who stay.</p>

<ul>

<li>The honesty check against finished footage</li>
<li>Title and thumbnail pairing</li>
<li>How to test titles fairly</li>
<li>When to change a title</li>
<li>Titles for Shorts</li>

</ul>
<h2 id="step-1-match-the-title-to-the-intent">Step 1: Match the title to the intent</h2>
<p>Start by asking where the viewer will find the video. A search viewer types a question and wants a clear answer. A browse viewer scrolls a feed and needs a reason to stop. Those two viewers need different titles, even for the same video.</p>
<p>Search titles should be clear first. Put the topic and the benefit in plain words, so the viewer can tell at once that the video answers their question. For example, "How to Edit YouTube Shorts on a Phone" works for search because it names the task, the format, and the device. Browse titles can lead with curiosity or a result, because the viewer was not looking for that video. For example, "I Edited 100 Shorts. Here Is What Actually Worked" works for browse because it promises a lesson from real effort. Do not force a browse hook onto a search video, or searchers may skip it as vague. Do not force a flat search title onto a browse video, or feed viewers may scroll past it as dull.</p>
<h2 id="step-2-use-keywords-naturally">Step 2: Use keywords naturally</h2>
<p>A keyword is the phrase a viewer would type or say when looking for your topic. Put the primary keyword near the start of the title when it fits naturally, because early words are seen first and truncated last. Do not force the keyword into a title where it sounds awkward. A forced keyword hurts the click more than it helps search, because viewers judge the title as humans first.</p>
<p>Write the way your viewers talk. If viewers say "fix a cracked screen" rather than "repair a damaged display", use their words. Check search suggestions, comments, and related searches for the phrases real viewers use. Use one primary keyword per title, plus a detail that makes the promise specific, such as a device, a time frame, or an audience. Specificity helps the right viewer choose your video over a generic one.</p>
<p>Avoid stuffing extra keywords into the title. A title such as "How to Edit Shorts, Edit Videos, Video Editing Tips, Shorts Editing Guide" reads as spam and tells the viewer nothing new. One clear phrase, plus one specific detail, beats a list of keywords every time.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/youtube-title-brainstorm.jpg" alt="Whiteboard covered with short title ideas and arrows" /></p>
<h2 id="step-3-get-the-length-rule-right">Step 3: Get the length rule right</h2>
<p>The character limit for a YouTube title is 100 characters, as stated in the <a href="https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/57404?hl=en-GB&amp;co=GENIE.Platform%3DDesktop" rel="noopener" target="_blank">official YouTube Help page on editing video settings</a>. That limit is a hard technical limit, not a target. A useful working guideline is to keep titles at about 60 characters or fewer when you can, because longer titles are often cut off on phones and feeds. The key point is that display varies by surface and device, so there is no universal rule that 60 characters always fits. Put the important words early, so the promise survives even when the end is cut off.</p>
<p>If a title runs long, cut from the middle, not from the start or the end. The start holds the keyword and the hook, and the end often holds the specific detail. The middle often holds filler words that add little. For example, "How to Fix a Cracked Phone Screen at Home in 20 Minutes Without Special Tools" can lose "at Home" or "Special" and stay clear. Read the cut title on a phone preview before you publish. If the cut title still makes sense to a new viewer, it is ready.</p>

<h2 id="step-5-run-the-honesty-check-on-finished-footage">Step 5: Run the honesty check on finished footage</h2>
<p>Write your final title against the finished video, not against your plan. Plans change during filming, and a title written from a plan can promise something the video never shows. Finish this sentence first. This video helps this viewer understand or do this one thing. Then check every title variant against that sentence.</p>
<p>Run this five point check on each title.</p>
<ol>
<li>Can a new viewer tell the subject from the title alone.</li>
<li>Does the video fully deliver the promise, without exaggeration.</li>
<li>Do the important words survive if the title is cut off on a phone.</li>
<li>Do the title and thumbnail tell the same story without repeating all the same words.</li>
<li>Does the opening minute confirm the title, so the viewer feels they are in the right place.</li>
</ol>
<p>Reject any title that fails point 2, even if it sounds clickable. For example, a title that promises to double a harvest overnight must be rejected if the video shows no such result. That kind of title may earn clicks, but it will lose viewers, damage trust, and risk a misleading metadata problem. Honest titles grow slower in the short term and stronger in the long term, because they earn viewers who stay.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-pair-the-title-with-the-thumbnail">Step 6: Pair the title with the thumbnail</h2>
<p>The title and the thumbnail make one promise together. The thumbnail shows the subject, feeling, or result. The title gives the detail in words. Do not repeat the same words in both parts. For example, a thumbnail that shows the words "I quit" works with a title that explains why the creator stopped uploading after years. The two parts together tell a fuller story than either part alone.</p>
<p>Review the pair at phone size before you publish. Look at the thumbnail, read the title, and ask what a new viewer would expect. Then watch the first minute and ask whether that expectation is met. If the pair promises a story but the opening gives a lecture, fix the opening or change the pair. The pair must match the video, not only each other. For thumbnail design in depth, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/youtube-earning/how-to-design-youtube-thumbnails" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to design YouTube thumbnails</a>. The official thumbnail rules are on the <a href="https://support.google.com/youtubecreatorstudio/answer/72431?hl=en-GB&amp;co=GENIE.Platform%3DDesktop" rel="noopener" target="_blank">official YouTube Help page for custom thumbnails</a>.</p>
<h2 id="step-7-test-titles-fairly">Step 7: Test titles fairly</h2>
<p>Testing works when the variants are meaningfully different. Test titles that make different promises, not titles that differ by one word. For example, test a search style title against a browse style title, or test a mistake frame against a how to frame. Near identical titles teach you almost nothing, because the viewer response will be almost the same.</p>
<p>YouTube allows testing of up to three title variants, judged by watch time share over up to about two weeks, not by clicks alone. That rule matters. A title that earns clicks but loses viewers fast is not a winner. Run one test at a time, do not change the thumbnail at the same time, and record the start date and the variants. At the end of the test, keep the variant that brings both clicks and staying viewers. If no variant clearly wins, keep the clearest and most honest title, and test a new promise next time.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/youtube-title-checklist.jpg" alt="Checklist on paper with tick boxes beside a laptop" /></p>
<h2 id="step-8-know-when-to-change-a-title">Step 8: Know when to change a title</h2>
<p>Change a title when the data points to a clear problem, not because you feel bored with it. Change it when impressions are high but clicks are low, which points to a weak promise or a poor title and thumbnail pair. Change it when the traffic source shifts, for example from browse to search, so the old browse hook no longer fits the new search viewers. Change it when the video content changes, so the old title no longer matches the video.</p>
<h2 id="step-9-titles-for-shorts">Step 9: Titles for Shorts</h2>
<p>Shorts titles follow the same honesty rule, but with less room for detail. A Shorts title should name the benefit or the hook in a few words, because the Short itself shows the rest fast. Keep the title short, put the key words first, and avoid long explanations that the Short will show anyway. For example, a Short about a phone fix can use a title such as "Fix a cracked screen in 20 minutes" and let the video show the steps.</p>
<p>Do not reuse a long video title word for word on a Short. The Short needs its own promise, matched to its own length and pace. If the Short points to a longer video, the Short title should still stand alone, so viewers who never open the longer video still get value. For Shorts growth in depth, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/youtube-earning/how-to-grow-youtube-shorts" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to grow YouTube Shorts</a>.</p>
<h2 id="a-short-note-on-descriptions">A short note on descriptions</h2>
<p>Descriptions support titles, but they do not replace them. Put the key point and the main keyword in the first two lines, because those lines are seen before a viewer taps to expand. Add natural secondary keywords, timestamps for longer videos, and a small number of useful links. Keep descriptions in the range of about 200 to 500 words for a longer video, and shorter for a Short. A description that repeats the title word for word wastes space. Use it to add context, chapters, and links that help the viewer take the next step.</p>
<h2 id="what-it-costs-and-what-to-expect">What it costs and what to expect</h2>
<p>Title writing costs time, not money. Plan 15 to 30 minutes per video for title work, including three variants, the honesty check, and the thumbnail pairing review. That time is well spent, because a title change is cheaper than reshooting a video, and a better title can lift a good video that was being skipped.</p>
<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Writing the title from the plan instead of the finished video.</li>
<li>Promising a result the video does not show.</li>
<li>Stuffing several keywords into one title.</li>
<li>Putting the key words at the end, where they get cut off.</li>
<li>Repeating the thumbnail text word for word in the title.</li>
<li>Testing near identical titles and learning nothing.</li>
<li>Changing the title and thumbnail at the same time.</li>
<li>Changing titles every few days without waiting for data.</li>
<li>Using hype words that the video cannot support.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="risks-and-limitations">Risks and limitations</h2>
<p>Misleading titles break trust and can break platform rules on misleading metadata. A title that overpromises may earn a short spike in clicks, but it will raise early exits and teach the feed that your videos disappoint. Click through rate also falls naturally as impressions broaden to less interested viewers, so a falling rate alone does not prove a title failed. Compare a title against your own channel average for the same traffic type, not against a generic benchmark. Results vary with topic, competition, and audience, and no title formula can promise a set number of views.</p>
<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 id="how-long-should-a-youtube-title-be">How long should a YouTube title be</h3>
<p>The technical limit is 100 characters. A useful working guideline is about 60 characters or fewer when you can, because longer titles are often cut off on phones. Display varies by surface and device, so put the important words early, and check the title in a phone preview before you publish.</p>
<h3 id="should-i-put-keywords-in-my-title">Should I put keywords in my title</h3>
<p>Yes, put one primary keyword near the start when it fits naturally, and write in the words your viewers actually use. Do not stuff extra keywords into the title, because a keyword list reads as spam and tells the viewer nothing new. One clear phrase plus one specific detail beats a list of keywords.</p>
<h3 id="when-should-i-change-a-youtube-title">When should I change a YouTube title</h3>
<p>Change a title when impressions are high but clicks are low, when the traffic source shifts from browse to search or the reverse, or when the video content changes. Make one change at a time, record the date, and wait long enough to judge clicks plus watch time. Do not change titles every few days.</p>
<h3 id="can-i-a-b-test-youtube-titles">Can I A/B test YouTube titles</h3>
<p>Yes, YouTube allows testing of up to three title variants, judged by watch time share over up to about two weeks. Test meaningfully different promises, not near identical wording, and do not change the thumbnail at the same time. Keep the variant that brings clicks and staying viewers.</p>
<h3 id="do-shorts-need-different-titles">Do Shorts need different titles</h3>
<p>Yes, Shorts titles should be shorter and should name the benefit or hook in a few words, because the Short shows the rest fast. Do not reuse a long video title word for word on a Short. Write a title that stands alone, even if the Short points to a longer video.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Write titles that make one clear, honest promise and match the finished video. Start with intent, put keywords early without forcing them, keep the title short enough to survive truncation, and run the honesty check before you publish. Then test fairly, change only when data points to a problem, and keep the title that earns clicks from viewers who stay.
