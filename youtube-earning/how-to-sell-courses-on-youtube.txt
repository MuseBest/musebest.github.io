2d:T4c7c,<h2 id="how-to-sell-courses-on-youtube-in-2026">How to Sell Courses on YouTube in 2026</h2>
<p>If you already teach a skill on YouTube, learning how to sell courses on YouTube can turn free viewers into paying students. A course lets you go deeper than a single video. This guide shows you how to use your videos to sell a course, from proving the topic to tracking each sale.</p>

Here you will learn how videos, descriptions, live streams, and your channel page can send viewers to your course.</p>

<h3 id="how-to-sell-courses-on-youtube-and-what-it-is-not">How to sell courses on YouTube, and what it is not</h3>
<p>Selling courses on YouTube means using free videos to prove you can teach, then sending interested viewers to a paid course on another page. YouTube is the shop window. The course platform is the classroom. Free videos answer one question at a time. The paid course puts the full skill in order. That is the core of how to sell courses on YouTube: free teaching first, paid depth second.</p>
<p>You do not need a large channel, and you do not need to join the YouTube Partner Program first. The Partner Program bar of 1,000 subscribers and 4,000 watch hours is for ad revenue, not for linking to a course page. You can put a course link in a description at any channel size. For the full monetisation picture, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/youtube-earning/how-to-monetize-youtube-channel" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to monetise a YouTube channel</a>.</p>
<p>Links must still follow the rules. The <a href="https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9054257" rel="noopener" target="_blank">YouTube external links policy</a> says links must not point to content that breaks the Community Guidelines, and it applies to videos, descriptions, comments including pinned comments, and live streams. A link to a real course sales page is not banned.</p>
<h3 id="step-1-prove-people-want-the-course-before-you-build-it">Step 1: Prove people want the course before you build it</h3>
<p>Do not build a full course before you know anyone will buy it. Building first wastes weeks of work. Prove demand first, with your own channel data.</p>
<p>In YouTube Studio, sort your videos by watch time, not by views. Watch time shows which topics hold attention, and attention is what sells a course. Read the comments for buying signals, such as viewers who ask for the full method. Read <a href="https://earnquill.com/youtube-earning/youtube-analytics-explained" rel="noopener" target="_blank">YouTube Analytics explained</a> to find these numbers.</p>
<p>Then test demand in three steps. First, poll viewers on which course they would want. Second, open a waitlist, a simple page where people leave an email address to hear when the course opens. Third, offer the waitlist a pre-sale, with a clear date for when the course will be ready. A pre-sale means selling before the course is finished, so only offer one if you can meet the date.</p>
<h3 id="step-2-set-up-your-channel-to-sell">Step 2: Set up your channel to sell</h3>
<p>Set up your channel so a new visitor can find the course in one minute. Start with a channel trailer of 60 to 90 seconds that says who you teach and who the course is for. Pin a billboard video at the top of your channel. A billboard video is a trailer that stays featured, and your other videos point to it. For setup, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/youtube-earning/how-to-start-youtube-channel" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to start a YouTube channel</a>.</p>
<p>Organise your channel into playlists that follow a learning path, from beginner to advanced. Put the course link in your About section, with one line that says who the course is for.</p>

<h3 id="step-5-write-the-pitch-into-each-video">Step 5: Write the pitch into each video</h3>
<p>A good pitch is one sentence, and it describes what the course teaches, not what the student will earn. Say: "If you want the full method with exercises and templates, the link is in the description." Keep about 80 percent of each video as free value.</p>
<p>Pitch once per video, and put the pitch after the value, never before it. A launch can help first sales. A launch is a short period, often one week, when you publish several videos about the course topic and open sales to your waitlist first.</p>
<h3 id="step-6-use-descriptions-pinned-comments-and-end-screens">Step 6: Use descriptions, pinned comments, and end screens</h3>
<p>Use the same order in every sales video description. Line 1 states what the video teaches. Line 2 gives the course link and who it is for. Line 3 gives a free resource, such as a checklist. Then add time stamps and any disclosure. Put the course link in the first three lines.</p>
<p>Pin your own comment under each sales video. A pinned comment is a comment you fix to the top of the comments. Write one sentence on who the course is for, then the link. Update it when the price or the link changes. Never pin a comment that promises income.</p>
<p>Use end screens to point viewers to your billboard video or your free lesson, which then carries the course link in its description. An end screen is the panel YouTube shows in the last 20 seconds of a video.</p>
<h3 id="where-free-value-ends-and-the-paid-course-begins">Where free value ends and the paid course begins</h3>
<p>Free videos teach one skill at a time and solve one problem. The paid course gives the full path in order, with practice, templates, feedback, and updates. Give away one full lesson from the course, so buyers can judge the teaching before they pay.</p>
<p>If a viewer says the free videos already cover the topic, explain what the course adds: the lessons in order, the exercises, and your feedback. If you cannot name what the course adds, the course is not ready to sell.</p>
<h3 id="how-to-price-your-course-for-youtube-viewers">How to price your course for YouTube viewers</h3>
<p>Price the course by its depth and by the result it teaches, not by the number of videos in it. Check your platform fees first, because a low price leaves little after fees. Read <a href="https://earnquill.com/digital-products/how-to-price-digital-products" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to price digital products</a> for the full method.</p>
<p>A launch discount can help first sales, but keep it short and dated. Viewers in Pakistan, India, and other lower income markets may not afford a 99 US dollar course, so think about a lower tier, a payment plan, or regional pricing if your platform supports it.</p>
<h3 id="worked-example-what-one-month-of-sales-could-look-like">Worked example: what one month of sales could look like</h3>
<p>Example only: these numbers are not typical results. They show how the maths works.</p>
<p>Imagine a free lesson video that gets 10,000 views in a month. Suppose 2 percent click the course link. That is 200 clicks. Suppose 2 percent of those clicks buy a course priced at 49 US dollars. That is 4 sales, or 196 US dollars in gross sales. Gross sales means the total before fees and tax.</p>
<p>Now take off the fees. On Gumroad, a direct sale costs 10 percent plus 50 US cents, which is 5.40 US dollars on a 49 US dollar sale. Four sales cost 21.60 US dollars. Take 21.60 US dollars from 196 US dollars and you keep 174.40 US dollars, before tax. Recheck: 200 clicks, 4 sales, 196 US dollars gross, 21.60 US dollars in fees, 174.40 US dollars kept.</p>

<h3 id="refunds-and-student-support">Refunds and student support</h3>
<p>Write your refund policy before your first sale, and put it on the sales page. Many creators offer 14 or 30 days. Choose a window you can honour, and keep to it.</p>
<p>Tell students how to reach you, and how fast you will reply. If a lesson is unclear, fix it. If your policy allows a refund, refund quickly.</p>
<p>A chargeback is when a buyer asks their bank to reverse a payment. Too many chargebacks can put your payment account at risk. Refund rules differ by country, so check the rules where you and your buyers live.</p>
<h3 id="how-to-measure-what-works">How to measure what works</h3>
<p>Use UTM tags on your course links. A UTM tag is a short label you add to a link, so you can see where a click came from. For a video description link, use a link like this: <a href="https://example.com/course?utm_source=youtube&amp;utm_medium=video_description&amp;utm_campaign=course_launch" rel="noopener" target="_blank">https://example.com/course?utm_source=youtube&amp;utm_medium=video_description&amp;utm_campaign=course_launch</a>. Use a different campaign name for each video, so you can see which video brings sales. That is how to sell courses on YouTube without guessing: measure each step from views to sales.</p>
<p>Check each week in YouTube Studio and your email tool: views, link clicks, email signups, and sales. If a video gets views but no clicks, the pitch or the description link needs work. If clicks come but no sales follow, the sales page or the price needs work. For the email side, read <a href="https://earnquill.com/blogging/how-to-build-email-list-for-a-blog" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to build an email list for a blog</a>.</p>
<h3 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Building the full course before checking whether viewers want it.</li>
<li>Pitching the course in the first minute, before giving any value.</li>
<li>Making free videos so thin that viewers cannot judge your teaching.</li>
<li>Making free videos so complete that the paid course adds nothing new.</li>
<li>Copying another creator's price without knowing your own fees and costs.</li>
<li>Promising income or results that the course cannot guarantee.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="risks-and-scams-to-watch-for">Risks and scams to watch for</h3>
<p>Watch out for courses that promise a YouTube course system with guaranteed income. A real course teaches a skill, and its sales page names the syllabus, the teacher, and the refund policy. A scam page promises income, hides the syllabus, and pressures you to buy fast.</p>
<p>Do not buy views, subscribers, or comments to push a sales video. YouTube can remove fake engagement. Warn your students to pay only on your real course page, and never through a link in a comment. Keep your email list safe, because it is your backup if a platform changes its fees or closes. Course income is taxable in most countries, so keep records of every sale.</p>
<h3 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Do I need 1,000 subscribers to sell a course on YouTube?</h4>
<p>No. The 1,000 subscriber and 4,000 watch hour bar is for the YouTube Partner Program, which pays ad revenue. It is not a rule for linking to a course page.</p>
<h4>Where should I put the course link?</h4>
<p>Put it in the first three lines of the video description, in a pinned comment, and on your channel About page. Use end screens to point viewers to your course trailer or free lesson video.</p>
<h4>How much should I charge for a course?</h4>
<p>Charge by the depth of the course and the result it teaches, not by the number of videos in it. Check your platform fees first, because a low price leaves little after fees.</p>
<h4>Can I sell a course with Shorts only?</h4>
<p>Shorts can point viewers to a long video, but a Short alone rarely sells a course, because the viewer has not seen enough of your teaching. Build long videos that prove your skill, and use Shorts as teasers.</p>
<h3 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h3>
<p>To sell courses on YouTube, pick a topic your viewers already ask about, prove it with a waitlist, set up your channel, choose a platform whose fees you understand, pitch once per video after the value, and measure which video brings sales. Do not build before you validate, and do not promise income.</p>
