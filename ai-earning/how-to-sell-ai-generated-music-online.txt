3c:T4b65,<h2 id="how-to-sell-ai-generated-music-online-in-2026">How to Sell AI-Generated Music Online in 2026</h2>
<p>Learning how to sell AI-generated music online starts with a question most guides skip: do you actually own what you are selling? AI music tools can produce a finished track in minutes, but the licence, the copyright position, and the platform rules decide whether you can sell it. Get those three checks wrong, and your tracks can be removed or your account can be closed. Get them right, and you have a real product to sell.</p>
<p>This guide is for beginners who do not play an instrument but want to sell music made with AI tools. You will learn the three legal checks, which platforms allow AI music, how to pick a niche, how to make a track worth buying, where to sell, and how to price and licence your work.</p>

<h3 id="the-three-legal-checks-before-you-sell-anything">The three legal checks before you sell anything</h3>
<p>Before you list a single track, run three checks.</p>
<p>First, check the tool licence. Your AI music tool gives you a licence to use what it makes, and that licence decides whether you can sell the track. Free plans often allow personal use only, which means you cannot sell what you make on them. Paid plans often allow commercial use, but only under the terms in force at the time. Read the tool's terms page yourself, on the day you plan to sell, and save a copy of the terms.</p>
<p>Second, check the copyright position. In the United States, copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or to material where there is not enough human control over the expressive parts. That means you may not own the track in the way you own a song you wrote and played yourself. This does not stop you selling, but it changes what you are selling: you are selling a licence to use the track, not a copyright you fully own. Say so plainly in your listing.</p>
<p>Third, check the platform rules. Each place you sell has its own rules about AI music, and they change. Some welcome it, some limit it, and some ban it. Read the rules before you upload, and check them again every few months.</p>

<h3 id="what-the-us-copyright-office-actually-says-about-ai-generated-work">What the US Copyright Office actually says about AI-generated work</h3>
<p>The US Copyright Office published a report in January 2025, called Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability. Its executive summary says that copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or to material where there is not enough human control over the expressive elements, and that prompts alone do not provide enough control.</p>
<p>The report also says two useful things. Copyright protects the original expression in a work created by a human author, even if the work also includes AI-generated material. And human authors are entitled to copyright in the creative selection, coordination or arrangement of material in the outputs, or in creative modifications of the outputs. In plain words, the more real creative work you add, the stronger your claim.</p>
<p>Read the <a href="https://copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf" rel="noopener" target="_blank">US Copyright Office report on AI and copyrightability</a> yourself if you plan to sell in the United States, and check your local rules elsewhere.</p>
<h3 id="platform-rules-bandcamp-and-other-bans-to-know">Platform rules: Bandcamp and other bans to know</h3>
<p>Platform rules are the check most new sellers skip, and it is the one that causes the most lost accounts. Bandcamp announced in January 2026 that music or audio generated wholly or in substantial part by AI is not permitted on the platform, and that AI tools used to impersonate other artists or styles are strictly prohibited. Check Bandcamp's live policy before you upload anything, because platform rules change.</p>
<p>Streaming services and stock music sites each have their own rules too. Some distributors will place AI-generated tracks on streaming services, and some will refuse them. Some stock sites ask you to label AI content, and some do not accept it at all. Always read the upload, labelling, and takedown rules before you commit. If the rules are unclear, ask their support team in writing, and keep the reply.</p>

<h3 id="make-a-track-worth-buying">Make a track worth buying</h3>
<p>A buyer will not pay for a track that sounds unfinished. Use this quality bar before you list anything.</p>
<p>First, listen to the whole track on headphones and on a phone speaker. If the track has odd noises or a section that sounds broken, fix it or cut it. Second, check the start and the end. A track that starts with a burst of noise or ends mid-note sounds unfinished, so add a short fade in and a clean fade out. Third, mix for the buyer's use. Background music should sit quietly under speech, so keep the volume steady. Fourth, give the track a clear name, a tempo, a mood, and a length in the listing.</p>
<p>Do not copy another artist's style on purpose. AI tools can imitate a style closely, and that creates legal and platform risk. Make tracks in a general mood, and never use a real artist's name in your title, tags, or description.</p>

<h3 id="distributors-explained-distrokid-tunecore-content-id-and-royalties">Distributors explained: DistroKid, TuneCore, Content ID and royalties</h3>
<p>A distributor is a service that places your music on streaming services such as Spotify, and collects the royalties for you. DistroKid and TuneCore are the two best known distributors for independent sellers. They charge an annual fee. Check the live pricing page of each distributor before you choose, because fees change.</p>
<p>Content ID is YouTube's system for finding copyrighted music in videos. When you release through a distributor, you may be offered Content ID, which claims videos that use your track and collects money from them. This has a catch for AI music. If your track is similar to another AI track, Content ID can claim the wrong video. Read the distributor's Content ID terms carefully, and check what happens to your releases if your subscription lapses. Some distributors remove your music when you stop paying, and some leave it up.</p>
<p>Keep your expectations honest. One competitor guide reports streaming at 0.003 to 0.005 dollars per stream, and describes streaming income for a new seller as near zero. At those rates, a thousand streams earns only a few dollars.</p>

<p>Write your licence in plain words. It should say what the buyer can do, what they cannot do, and that you do not guarantee copyright ownership, because the track was made with AI.</p>
<h3 id="find-your-first-buyers">Find your first buyers</h3>
<p>Buyers rarely find a new seller on their own, so you need to reach out. Start with the niche you picked, and find ten creators or small businesses in it who use background music.</p>
<p>Then send a short message, by email or by the platform they use. Keep it under 100 words. Say who you are, and offer one free track that fits them. Here is a template you can adapt: "Hello, I make calm background music for YouTube videos. I watched your recent video on morning routines, and I noticed the music stopped halfway through. I made a two-minute track that would fit, and I would like to send it to you free. If you like it, I can make more in the same style."</p>
<p>If they reply, send the track, and ask what they think. If they use it, ask for a short testimonial and offer a paid pack of five more tracks. If they do not reply, move on. Ten thoughtful messages beat a hundred copied messages.</p>
<h3 id="tax-and-record-keeping-basics">Tax and record keeping basics</h3>
<p>Keep records from your first sale. Note the date, the buyer, the track, the licence type, and the amount, in one spreadsheet. Keep copies of every licence, distributor statement, and payment receipt.</p>
<p>Tax rules differ by country, and they change, so this guide gives no tax advice. Check your local tax authority or a qualified accountant before your first tax deadline.</p>

<p>Your costs are modest: an AI music tool subscription, a distributor annual fee, and a simple store page. Check the live price of each before you commit, and do not pay for a tool until you have checked its commercial licence.</p>
<p>Income varies widely, and no honest guide can promise a figure. Competitor guides report wide ranges, and those ranges are their claims, not verified facts. One guide reports track packs at 5 to 50 dollars, custom work for brands at 100 to 2,000 dollars or more, and freelance work at 20 to 300 dollars per gig. Treat these as rough reports, not as promises. Streaming income is near zero for a new seller, so do not build your plan on it.</p>
<p>Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Imagine you sell a track pack of ten tracks for 25 dollars, and you sell four packs a month. That is 100 dollars a month. If each track takes one hour to make and check, ten tracks take ten hours, so your first month earns you about 9 dollars per hour.</p>

<ul>
<li>Selling tracks made on a free plan that allows personal use only, which breaks the tool licence.</li>
<li>Uploading to a platform that bans AI music, then losing the account.</li>
<li>Listing fifty unfinished tracks, instead of ten finished tracks in one niche.</li>
<li>Ignoring Content ID, then finding your tracks claimed or claiming other people's videos.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="risks-and-scams-to-watch-for">Risks and scams to watch for</h3>
<p>Watch for buyers who ask for a full custom track before any payment is agreed, or who ask you to send the finished files before they pay. Agree the price, the licence, and the payment terms in writing first, and ask for a deposit on custom work. Watch for "promoters" who charge a fee to place your music, but who cannot show you any real placements.</p>
<p>Also watch for the risk in your own listings. If you claim full copyright ownership of a purely AI-generated track, a buyer who later learns the truth may ask for a refund. Say plainly in your listing that the track was made with AI tools, and that the buyer is buying a licence to use it, not a full copyright transfer.</p>
<h3 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Can I sell AI-generated music legally?</h4>
<p>Yes, in most cases, but only if the tool licence allows commercial use, the platform allows AI music, and you describe the track honestly. Check the tool terms, the platform rules, and your local copyright law before you sell.</p>
<h4>Do I own AI-generated music?</h4>
<p>You may not own it in the way you own a song you wrote and played. In the United States, copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material. You can still sell a licence to use the track, but you should say so plainly in your listing.</p>
<h4>Can I sell AI music on Spotify?</h4>
<p>Yes, through a distributor such as DistroKid or TuneCore, if the distributor accepts AI-generated tracks. Streaming income for a new seller is very low, so treat it as reach, not as income.</p>
<h4>How much can I make selling AI music?</h4>
<p>There is no reliable typical figure. Some sellers earn nothing in the first months, and some earn a few hundred dollars a month from direct sales and custom work. Your results depend on your niche, your catalogue, your prices, and how many buyers you reach.</p>
<h4>Does Bandcamp allow AI music?</h4>
<p>Bandcamp announced in January 2026 that music generated wholly or in substantial part by AI is not permitted on the platform. Check Bandcamp's live policy before you upload, because platform rules change.</p>
<h4>Do I need to be a musician to sell AI music?</h4>
<p>No. You need a good ear, patience with editing, and a clear niche. Listen to the tracks buyers already use, and check every track for quality before you list it.</p>
<h3 id="conclusion-check-the-licence-first-then-sell">Conclusion: check the licence first, then sell</h3>
<p>Selling AI-generated music works when you treat the legal checks as the first step, not an afterthought. Read your tool licence, understand the copyright position, and check each platform's rules. Then pick one niche, make ten finished tracks, list them clearly, and reach out to real buyers.</p>
