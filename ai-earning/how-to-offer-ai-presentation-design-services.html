How to Offer AI Presentation Design Services in 2026

Learning how to offer AI presentation design services is a practical way to earn from a skill many small businesses need but few enjoy doing. A gym owner needs twelve slides for a bank meeting. A franchise seller needs a deck that proves the model works in one town before it works in ten. A training company needs slides new staff can follow without asking the manager every five minutes. Almost none of these businesses have anyone in-house who likes making slides. AI tools now handle the slow first draft, which means one person can deliver a polished deck in hours instead of days. This guide shows you how to sell that service, and how to make sure the finished deck is worth paying for.

What clients actually buy

Nobody wakes up wanting twelve slides. They want a loan approved, a franchise application that does not get thrown away, a sales team that stops apologising for its own deck, or a training session new staff can follow. The slides are the means, not the end. Your first question is never "what should slide three say?" It is "who reads this, and what do you want them to do afterwards?"

A bank reader wants numbers up front, in order, with nothing hidden. A franchise panel wants proof the model works in one town before it works in ten. A sales team wants one idea per slide and a closing slide they can actually say out loud. If you get the story wrong, no amount of clean design saves the deck, and the client will blame the slides even though the problem was the story. The AI draft is not the deliverable. Your judgement is. The client pays for the cuts you make, the story order you choose, and the fact that the finished file opens cleanly in PowerPoint on their laptop.

Who needs presentation design

Small businesses are your main market. They need decks constantly: for banks, landlords, franchise applications, staff training, sales meetings, and local tenders. They have no design team, and the owner usually makes the deck late at night, which is why so many small business decks look tired.

Startups are a second market. A founder preparing a pitch deck will pay well, because the stakes are high and the deadline is usually tight. Training companies, consultancies, and franchise sellers are a third market, because they present every week and need regular help. These clients are the best source of monthly retainers, because they always have another deck coming.

Step 1: Pick your deck type

Pick one type of deck to start with. Each type has different buyers, different prices, and different skills.

Sales and company decks are the easiest first sale, because the client already has notes, photos, and rough numbers, and they know the deck is weak. Pitch and funding decks pay more, but they need tighter fact-checking and a longer revision cycle. Training decks are steadier work, because the same client needs updates every few months. Deck cleanups are the simplest job of all: the client supplies a finished draft, and you fix the structure, tighten the wording, rebuild the charts, and apply one consistent design.

Pick sales decks first if you want fast sales, or deck cleanups if you want the easiest possible start. Write your choice in one sentence, such as "I turn rough notes into sales decks for small businesses". That sentence goes on your profile and your price list.

Step 3: Build your workflow

A fixed workflow is what stops the AI draft from becoming the deliverable. Follow the same five steps for every deck.

First, get the brief in writing before you open any tool. Ask for four things: who reads the deck, what action they should take, the three numbers that must be in it, and the deadline with a named contact for approvals. If the client cannot answer the first two, that is a paid discovery conversation, not a free chat. Get the source material, notes, spreadsheets, and photos, in one place, and confirm what is confidential. Never put a client's real revenue figures into a sample or portfolio piece.

Second, outline first and design second. Write the slide titles as a story in a plain document: problem, proof, offer, evidence, next step. Send that outline for approval before you design anything. It takes twenty minutes and saves the most expensive kind of revision, the one where the client changes the argument after you have already built the visuals.

Third, draft with AI, then cut hard. Generate a first draft from the approved outline, then remove at least a third of it. AI decks pad: three slides where one will do, five bullets where two carry the point, a chart for every number whether or not the number needs a chart. Your job is to leave the deck shorter than the draft. Clients notice brevity. They rarely complain that a deck was too short.

Fourth, design and check. Apply the client's brand colours and fonts, fix the charts, replace generic stock images with the client's own photos where possible, and check every number against the source material. Then open the file the way the client will: in PowerPoint, on a laptop, projected if possible. Fonts that look fine in a web app can shift in PowerPoint, and a chart that reads well on your monitor can turn to mush on a projector.

Fifth, deliver both formats, with a revision policy in writing. Send the PPTX and the PDF, name the file clearly, and state the terms: two revision rounds included, changes after that billed, and a deadline for feedback. Decks that stay open for "just one more tweak" for three weeks are how a profitable month turns into an unprofitable one.

Step 4: Set your prices

Charge by the package, not by the hour. Hourly billing punishes you for getting faster, and it makes clients nervous, because they cannot tell whether a deck will take three hours or ten. Packages fix both problems.

Package What is included Typical starting range
Deck cleanup Fix structure, wording, charts, one design, 10 slides 150 to 250 dollars
Sales or company deck Story order, AI draft, design, PPTX and PDF, 10 to 12 slides 300 to 600 dollars
Pitch or funding deck Research, fact-checking, design, 12 to 15 slides 600 to 1,200 dollars
Monthly retainer Two decks a month, or one deck plus updates 400 to 800 dollars a month

These are starting ranges from competitor guides, not fixed market rates. Your price depends on the complexity of the brief, the number of slides, and your country. Price the extra round of fact-checking into pitch decks, because a wrong figure in front of a bank is not a typo, it is a credibility problem. Two revision rounds are included in every package. One round is too few for a real client, and unlimited rounds is how a fixed-price package quietly becomes an hourly job at a bad rate.

Step 5: Build sample decks

Portfolio first, advertising second. Small businesses buy from proof, and proof does not have to be a real client. Build three sample decks for fictional businesses, a cafe seeking a second location, a trades firm pitching a council contract, and a gym launching a corporate membership, and label them clearly as samples. That gives a prospect something to judge without you having to hand over a real client's confidential numbers.

Each sample should show the same care as paid work: a clear story order, one idea per slide, short lines, simple charts with the takeaway in the headline, and a closing slide the speaker can say out loud. Keep the samples short, 10 to 12 slides each, and save them as both PPTX and PDF so a prospect can open them easily.

Never use a real client's deck in your portfolio without their written permission, and never include their real figures in a sample. If a client agrees, remove their numbers first and say the deck is adapted from real work.

Step 6: Find your first clients

Your first clients come from local businesses that already need decks. Make a list of 20 small businesses near you that present regularly: gyms, training firms, estate agents, franchise sellers, and consultancies. Send each owner a short message with one sample deck attached and one line about what you do. Offer a deck cleanup as the first sale, because it is the easiest yes: the client already knows the deck is weak.

Freelance marketplaces are a second channel. List a gig on Fiverr or Upwork with your three samples and your package prices. Marketplace work is a good way to build reviews, but the rates are lower, so use it to fill gaps between direct clients. Our guide to how to start freelancing covers profiles and first pitches, and our guide to how to set freelance rates explains how to price your time.

Referrals grow this service fast, because every deck is seen by a room full of people. Ask every happy client if they know another business that presents regularly, and offer a small thank-you, such as a free slide refresh, for a referral that becomes a client.

Worked example: what a sales deck could earn

Example only: these numbers are not typical results, and they are shown only to explain how the maths works.

Imagine a client orders a sales deck of 12 slides at 450 dollars. You spend 30 minutes on the brief and the outline, 1 hour generating and cutting the AI draft, 2 hours on design and charts, 30 minutes checking every number against the source material, and 1 hour on revisions and delivery. That is 5 hours in total. Your tool costs for the month are about 20 dollars, and if you do 4 decks that month, the tool cost per deck is 5 dollars. Your net is 450 minus 5, which is 445 dollars, for 5 hours of work. That is 445 divided by 5, which is 89 dollars an hour.

Now imagine the same deck takes 10 hours because the client changes the story twice after you built the slides. Your rate falls to about 44 dollars an hour, and the month gets harder. That is why the outline approval step matters so much: it protects your time. Results vary with your speed, your prices, and how clear the client's brief is.

How to offer AI presentation design services: what it costs and what to expect

Starting costs are low. A realistic startup cost is 20 to 50 dollars a month for one AI presentation tool, plus a free plan or two for the others. You already own a computer, and you do not need design software beyond the AI tool and a free image editor. A simple one-page site with your samples and prices costs little or nothing to build.

A realistic timeline is 2 to 3 weeks to build three sample decks and list your service, and 1 to 3 months to your first paying client. Deck cleanups sell fastest, because the client already knows the problem. Monthly retainers take longer, because clients usually buy one deck first and only sign a retainer after they trust your work. Plan for slow months at the start, and treat the service as a side income until orders are regular.

Copyright rules matter here. Do not lift images from a web search into a client's commercial deck. Use the client's own photos, properly licensed stock, or images you generated for the job, and keep a note of where each image came from. If a client asks you to copy a competitor's deck design, say no. You can be inspired by a layout, but you cannot copy it.

Common mistakes

  • Delivering the AI draft with a new cover slide, which clients can spot at once.
  • Writing text walls: a slide with eight bullets is a document pretending to be a presentation.
  • Skipping the outline approval, then rebuilding the deck when the client changes the story.
  • Using images from a web search in a commercial deck without a licence.
  • Promising outcomes: a deck does not guarantee funding, sales, or a franchise deal.
  • Including unlimited revisions, which turns a fixed price into an hourly job.
  • Forgetting to open the finished file in PowerPoint, where fonts and charts can shift.
  • Charging by the hour, which punishes you for getting faster.

Risks and scams to watch for

The biggest risk is a wrong number in front of a decision maker. A wrong figure in a bank meeting or a franchise application is not a typo, it is a credibility problem, and the client will blame you. Check every figure, percentage, date, chart, and name against the source document, not against the AI draft. Recalculate any number the AI worked out itself. Keep a written note of what you checked, and send it to the client with the deck.

The second risk is scope creep. A client may keep asking for "just one more slide" or "one more round of changes" for weeks. Stop it with a written revision policy: two rounds included, further changes billed, and a deadline for feedback. If the client misses the deadline, the deck is delivered as it stands.

Watch out for scams aimed at new designers. A common one is a "client" who asks you to pay for a tool, a template pack, or a licence before you can start work. A real client pays you, not the other way round. Another is a listing that asks you to design a deck that misleads investors, such as inventing revenue figures or hiding debts. Decline any work that asks you to deceive anyone, and never put a figure in a deck that you have not checked against a real source.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I charge for a 10-slide deck?

As a starting point, 150 to 250 dollars for a cleanup of an existing draft, and 300 to 600 dollars for a full sales or company deck built from notes. Pitch and funding decks run higher, typically 600 to 1,200 dollars for 12 to 15 slides, because the fact-checking and revision cycle are heavier. Treat these as ranges, not fixed rates, and adjust for complexity and turnaround.

Which AI presentation tool should I start with?

Gamma Plus at 9 dollars a month billed annually is the cheapest honest starting point for fast drafts, and its PPTX export covers most handovers. If your clients insist on native PowerPoint or Google Slides files, start with Plus AI Basic at 10 dollars a month billed annually instead. Add Canva Pro when clients also need matching graphics from the same brand kit. Prices were checked on the official pages on 2026-11-24, except Canva, whose pricing page could not be read that day.

Can I really do this without design experience?

You need judgement about structure, wording, and numbers more than formal design training. The tools handle layout; you handle the story, the cuts, and the accuracy checks. Three labelled sample decks are a better proof of that than a design qualification, and they are what prospects will actually look at.

Do clients care which AI tool I use?

Almost never, and they should not need to. They care about the format they receive, whether it opens cleanly, and whether the story holds up in the room. Mention the tool only when it affects the handover, for example that the deck will arrive as a native PowerPoint file they can edit.

How many revision rounds should I include?

Two rounds, in writing, with further changes billed. One round is too few for a real client, and unlimited rounds is how a fixed-price package quietly becomes an hourly job at a bad rate. Put a feedback deadline in the quote as well, so a deck cannot stay open indefinitely.

Conclusion

Learning how to offer AI presentation design services in 2026 comes down to selling the outcome, not the slides: a loan approved, a franchise won, a sales team that stops apologising for its deck. Pick one deck type, learn one tool well, approve the outline before you design anything, and check every number against the source. Price by the package, include two revision rounds, and build three honest samples that prove you can do the work.
