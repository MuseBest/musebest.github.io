How to Sell AI Voiceover Services in 2026

Clients who buy voiceover do not buy an audio file. They buy a finished video, a course lesson, a phone system, or a training module that sounds clear and professional. If you want to learn how to sell AI voiceover services, start with that fact. Your job is to deliver audio that fits the buyer's project, on time, with the right licence, and without surprises. This guide shows you how to package, price, and sell that service, step by step.

The service lines that sell

Explainer and advert narration. A business needs a voice for a short video that explains a product or service. The buyer is usually a marketing manager or a small business owner. The deliverable is one finished audio track, matched to the video length, with pauses where the visuals need room.

E-learning and training narration. Course builders and employers need clear narration for lessons and staff training. The buyer cares about consistent pronunciation, steady pace, and clean audio across many lessons. This work often repeats, which makes it a good source of steady projects.

YouTube and podcast intros. Creators need a short intro, an outro, and a mid show transition that sound the same every episode. The buyer is a creator or a podcast producer. The deliverable is a small set of short clips, not a long narration.

Phone prompts and IVR. IVR means interactive voice response, which is the recorded menu callers hear when they phone a business, such as "press one for sales". Small businesses need these prompts recorded clearly, and they often need updates when opening hours or services change. This is a repeatable, practical service line that many beginners overlook.

Audiobook and course narration. Long form narration covers books and full courses. The buyer needs a voice that stays consistent for hours of audio, and a file set that is easy to use. This work takes the most review time, so price it with care.

Reusable voice packs. A voice pack is a set of ready made prompts or short clips, such as phone greetings, that a buyer can reuse. This is a product, not a service, because you sell the same pack more than once. It can add income, but it is not a replacement for client work at the start.

Tools and licences first

Before you sell anything, check the licence of the voice tool you plan to use. A commercial licence is permission to use the output in paid work. Many tools offer a free plan for personal testing, but that plan may ban commercial use, add a watermark in audio form, or limit how many characters you can generate. A character quota is the amount of text the tool will turn into speech in a billing period. Paid plans usually raise that quota and allow commercial use, but the details differ by tool, so read the tool's own terms page and note the date you checked it.

Use this licence checklist before every paid project:

  • Does the plan allow commercial use of the output?
  • Does the plan allow the buyer to use the audio after you stop paying for the tool?
  • Can you export clean audio with no watermark and no spoken credit?
  • Does the plan cover voice cloning, which means copying a real person's voice, and if so, what consent is required?
  • What is the character quota, and what happens when you reach it mid project?

If you cannot answer these questions for your chosen tool, do not take the paid job yet. The cheapest plan is often the wrong plan for paid work, because a licence problem can cost you the client and the fee. Also decide early how you will handle pronunciation. Brand names, place names, and technical terms are often mispronounced by AI voices, so keep a pronunciation list for each client and check it before delivery.

Packaging and pricing

Buyers understand packages better than hourly rates. A package names the deliverable, the length, the revision limit, and the turnaround time. A simple three package structure works for most voiceover services:

Basic package. One short narration, up to two minutes of finished audio, one revision round, delivery in three working days. This suits a buyer testing you for the first time.

Standard package. Narration up to ten minutes, two revision rounds, delivery in five working days, with a pronunciation check and clean file naming. This suits course lessons and explainer videos.

Premium package. Long form narration or a full clip set, priority turnaround, and a pronunciation list kept for future projects. This suits a buyer who will order again.

Price by project, by finished minute, or by word count, but never mix the bases in one quote. Per finished minute pricing is easy to compare, but a short intro takes nearly as much setup as a longer track, so a minimum project fee is fair. Per word pricing suits long scripts, because words are easy to count before you start. Competitor guides disagree sharply on voiceover prices, with some quoting per finished minute and others quoting per whole project, so always state your basis in the quote and compare like with like. Always count platform fees before you quote. Marketplaces take a fee from each sale, and that fee can take a large share of a small order.

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Suppose you quote 120 dollars for a five minute training narration. Your tool cost for that project is 15 dollars, and you spend two hours on generation, checking, and mastering, which is the final polish of volume and clarity. If you value your time at 30 dollars per hour, your time cost is 60 dollars. Your total cost is 75 dollars, leaving 45 dollars before platform fees and tax. If a marketplace takes 20 percent, which is 24 dollars, you keep 21 dollars. This is why tracking hours per finished minute matters: a price that looks good can leave very little once fees are counted.

Building an honest portfolio

You need three samples before you pitch. Make one explainer sample, one training sample, and one short intro set. Use fictional brand names, and label every sample clearly as sample work. Never let a buyer believe fictional work was paid client work. That advice matters because some guides suggest letting clients assume fictional samples are real, which is misleading and can destroy trust if the buyer finds out.

Each sample should prove one thing. The explainer sample should prove clear pacing and natural pauses. The training sample should prove consistent pronunciation across a longer script. The intro set should prove a voice that fits a brand and stays consistent across clips. Keep each sample short, name the service line it shows, and state that it is sample work on the page where buyers hear it. Finding first clients

Start with three channels. First, list your packages on a freelance marketplace, where buyers already search for voiceover help. Second, contact creators and course builders directly, and name one specific project of theirs that needs narration, such as a course lesson with no voice yet. Third, contact local businesses that need phone prompts, because many small businesses still use a plain or outdated phone greeting and have never been offered a better one. A short outreach message works best: name the buyer, name the project, link one matching sample, state your price basis, and ask for a short call. Do not send the same message to everyone, and do not promise results you cannot control.

A delivery workflow that avoids rework

A simple workflow prevents most revision problems. First, send the buyer a short intake form asking for the script, the voice style, the pace, the file format, and the deadline. Second, read the script and flag problems before you generate anything, such as unclear sentences, missing pronunciations, or a length that does not fit the video. Third, generate the audio, check every brand name and number by listening, and fix mispronunciations. Fourth, master the audio, which means setting a steady volume, removing long pauses and clicks, and exporting in the format the buyer asked for. Fifth, deliver files with clear names, such as the project name plus the version number, and keep a copy of the final files. Sixth, handle revisions within the limit in your package, and charge for extra rounds. Most rework comes from skipping the script check, so never skip it to save time.

Rules and risks

Tell the buyer that the voice is AI generated. Some buyers will not mind, and some will prefer a human voice, but hiding the fact risks a refund, a bad review, or a lost client. Never clone a real person's voice without that person's clear permission, and never use a cloned voice to mislead listeners. Copyright rules for AI output differ by country and are still developing, so keep your claims general and point buyers to the tool's terms for the licence. Some jobs are a poor fit for AI voice, such as emotional adverts, sensitive announcements, or content where listeners expect a real person. Saying no to those jobs is better than delivering audio that harms the buyer's reputation. Tax rules also differ by country, so check your local tax authority or a qualified accountant before you treat voiceover income as a business. Frequently asked questions

Do clients care that the voice is AI generated?

Some do and some do not. Many buyers care about clarity, pace, and price, not about how the voice was made. Others want a human voice for emotional or sensitive content. Being open about AI use lets the buyer choose, and it protects you from disputes later.

Do you need a paid plan to sell AI voiceovers?

In most cases, yes. Free plans often ban commercial use or limit output, so a paid plan with a commercial licence is the safer choice for paid work. Check the tool's own terms page before you sell, and keep a note of what the plan allows.

How much should a first gig cost?

Price it to cover tool costs, your time, and platform fees, with a small margin left over. A small fixed project price is often fairer than an hourly rate for a first gig, because the buyer knows the total in advance. State the revision limit and the file format in the quote.

How long does a ten minute narration take?

Generation itself is fast, but checking, fixing pronunciations, and mastering take most of the time. A careful ten minute narration can take two to four hours of work in total, depending on the script quality and the number of corrections. Do not promise a one hour turnaround for a ten minute script.

Can you sell the same audio twice?

Only if the buyer agreed to it and the licence allows it. Most client work is made for one buyer and should not be resold. Reusable voice packs are the exception, because the buyer knows they are buying a shared product, not exclusive audio.

Conclusion

Pick one service line, publish three labelled samples, and track two numbers from the start: hours per finished minute, and revenue per finished minute after fees. Those two numbers tell you whether a price is working. Then add a second service line only when the first one runs smoothly.
