How to Make Money with AI Videos in 2026

You can make a video with an AI tool in an afternoon, but that alone does not earn money. Buyers pay for a finished video that helps them sell, teach, or explain something. This guide shows you how to make money with AI videos by selling videos to businesses first, and by publishing videos for platform payouts second. It is written for a beginner who can write a clear script and learn one editing tool.

The two ways AI videos earn money

AI videos earn money in two ways. The first way is client work. A business pays you to make a video for them. The second way is publishing. You post videos on a platform, and the platform pays you from ads, a creator programme, or affiliate sales. Client work usually pays first, because one buyer can pay for one video. Publishing usually pays later, because you need an audience and you must meet the platform rules for monetisation before payouts start. Most beginners should start with client work, then add publishing once they have samples and a steady process.

A "finished minute" means one minute of video that a buyer can use. It is not the same as one minute of raw AI output. A finished minute includes the script, the generated clips, the voice, the captions, the music, and the editing. Keep that difference in mind when you price work, because clients buy finished minutes, not raw clips.

Which route fits you

Ask yourself three questions before you pick a route. First, do you need income soon? If yes, choose client work, because a single sale can pay within days or weeks. Second, do you already have an audience? If yes, publishing may work, because you can put videos in front of people at once. Third, do you enjoy working with clients? If you like briefs, feedback, and deadlines, client work suits you. If you prefer working alone and building a channel slowly, publishing suits you better. There is no wrong answer, but mixing both routes at the start usually means neither gets enough attention. Pick one route, get it working, then add the second.

Five routes that actually pay

1. Client video services. You make explainer videos, training videos, or product videos for a business. The buyer gets a video they can put on their website, in an advert, or in staff training. This route needs a clear offer and a small portfolio. It is the most direct route to a first payment.

2. UGC adverts. UGC means user generated content, which is content that looks like it was made by a real customer rather than a polished advert. Brands pay creators to make short adverts for their products. With AI video, you can make the visuals and the voice, but you must be honest about what the product is and what it does. Never fake a testimonial, and never claim you used a product when you did not. Brands that want honest adverts will still pay well for good work.

3. Product videos for online sellers. Online sellers need short videos that show a product clearly. A seller might want three short vertical videos for one product, one showing the product, one showing how it is used, and one answering a common question. Accuracy matters here. The video must show the real product, its real colour, and its real size. Do not let an AI tool invent features the product does not have.

4. Social video retainers. A retainer is a fixed monthly fee for ongoing work. A business pays you each month for a set number of videos. This route gives steadier income than one off projects, but it also needs a monthly system, with approval steps and a delivery schedule. For the full content system behind a retainer, including calendars and reporting, see our guide on how to offer AI social media content services.

5. Publishing for platform payouts. You publish videos on a channel or account and earn from ads, a creator programme, or affiliate links. This route takes the longest. You must meet each platform's rules for monetisation, and those rules change. Treat platform payouts as a second income stream, not your first plan. For more on building a channel, see how to monetise a YouTube channel.

Tools compared on cost per finished minute

AI video tools fall into three groups. Avatar tools create a presenter who speaks your script on screen. They suit training videos and explainers. Cinematic text to video tools create moving scenes from a written prompt. They suit adverts and product shots. Repurposing tools turn a long video or a piece of text into short clips. They suit social content.

Do not compare tools by their monthly price alone. Compare them by cost per finished minute. A cheap tool that needs many retries can cost more than a dearer tool that gets the shot right first time. To work this out, track three numbers for your first ten videos: the tool cost for the month, the number of finished minutes you delivered, and the hours you spent editing. Divide the tool cost by the finished minutes for the tool cost per finished minute. Then add your time at a fair hourly value. This simple check stops you from choosing a tool on marketing claims.

Also check the commercial licence before you sell anything. A licence is the permission to use a tool's output for paid work. Some free plans allow personal use only, and some plans add a watermark, which is a logo or text laid over the video. Never deliver a watermarked video to a paying client. Check the tool's own terms page before you buy, and check again before you take on paid work, because terms change.

Pricing honestly, with a worked example

There are three honest ways to price video work. You can price per video, per finished minute, or as a monthly retainer. Per video pricing suits one off projects. Per finished minute pricing suits training and explainer work, where length is easy to measure. A retainer suits a client who needs videos every month. Whichever basis you choose, state it in your quote, and state what it includes: the number of revision rounds, the delivery format, and the usage rights. Usage rights mean where and for how long the buyer may use the video. A video used in one paid advert for one month is worth less than the same video used in adverts everywhere for a year, so charge more for wider use.

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Suppose you quote 150 dollars for a one minute product video. Your tool cost for that video is 20 dollars, and you spend two hours making it. If you value your time at 30 dollars per hour, your time cost is 60 dollars. Your total cost is 80 dollars. That leaves 70 dollars before platform fees and tax. If a marketplace takes a 20 percent fee, which is 30 dollars, you keep 40 dollars. Now you can see why a low price can look fine until fees are counted. Work this maths out before you publish a price list, not after your first sale.

A first week plan

Day one and day two: pick one niche, such as local estate agents, fitness coaches, or online sellers in one product type. A niche is a narrow group of buyers with a shared need. Day three and day four: make three sample videos for fictional businesses in that niche, and label each one clearly as sample work. Samples must never pretend to be real client work. Day five: write a one page offer that names the video, the price basis, the turnaround time, and the revision limit. Day six and day seven: send the offer to ten real businesses in your niche, each with one sample that fits their business. Ten honest, personal messages beat one hundred copied messages. If nobody replies, change the niche or the sample, not the whole plan.

Rules that protect your income

Label AI generated video where the platform asks you to. Platforms have added AI labelling rules, and they change, so check the rules of each platform you publish on before you post. Never publish a fake testimonial. In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission banned fake reviews and testimonials in a rule dated August 2024, and other countries have similar consumer protection rules. Never claim a product result that you cannot prove. Check music and footage rights before you use them. A track or clip that is free for personal use may not be free for a paid advert. When a client supplies photos, ask them to confirm they own the rights to those photos. Put these points in writing in your quote, so a dispute later is about the written agreement, not a memory.

Common mistakes

  • Making dozens of generic clips with no buyer in mind, then wondering why nobody pays for them.
  • Promising sales results for a video, when you control the video, not the buyer's product, price, or advert spend.
  • Skipping AI disclosure, then losing an account or a client when the platform or buyer finds out.
  • Underpricing the first gig so far that fees and revisions leave almost nothing, which teaches clients to expect that price.
  • Selling a tool instead of a result, by talking about which AI model you used rather than what the video does for the buyer.
  • Delivering a video with invented product details, which puts the seller at risk and harms your reputation.
  • Taking on every route at once, instead of proving one route with three samples and ten pitches.

Frequently asked questions

How much can I earn in my first month with AI videos?

There is no typical figure, because income depends on your niche, your samples, your prices, and how many buyers you contact. A realistic first month goal is one paid project and three good samples, not a full income. Client work pays sooner than platform payouts, but even client work needs outreach time.

Can you monetise AI videos on YouTube?

You can, but only if you meet the platform's monetisation rules and its AI content rules, and if the videos offer real value beyond mass produced clips. Check the current rules on the platform's own help pages before you build a channel around AI videos, because thresholds and labelling rules change.

Do TikTok and Facebook pay for AI videos?

Both platforms have creator programmes and rules about original content and AI labelling, and eligibility depends on your account, your region, and your content. Some AI heavy content is treated as low quality, especially where it replaces a real product demo or repeats other people's work. Read the current programme rules before you promise yourself payout income.

Do you need to show your face or use your own voice?

No. Avatar tools and AI voices can carry a video, and many product and training videos work without a human presenter. But the script, the accuracy, and the editing still decide whether a buyer pays, so a faceless video is not a shortcut around good work. If you sell narration as a service, see our guide on how to sell AI voiceover services.

What should I charge for a first gig?

Charge enough to cover tool costs, your time, and platform fees, with a small margin left over. For a first gig, a small fixed project price is often fairer than an hourly rate, because the buyer knows the total cost in advance. State the revision limit and the usage rights in the quote, and do not offer unlimited revisions.

Conclusion

Start with one route and one niche. Make three labelled samples, write a one page offer, and pitch ten real businesses. Price by finished minute or by project, count fees before you quote, and label AI content where the platform requires it. Once the first route pays, add a second route.
