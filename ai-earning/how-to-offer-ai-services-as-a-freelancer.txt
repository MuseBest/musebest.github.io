35:T3313,<p>Offering AI services as a freelancer works when you sell one clear result, not when you list every AI tool you know. A client does not buy "AI". They buy blog posts that publish on time, social content that stays consistent, a chatbot that answers common questions, or a workflow that saves their team hours each week. This guide shows you how to turn that result into a package, price it honestly, write a proposal buyers trust, and deliver it without cutting corners on quality or disclosure.</p>
<h2 id="start-with-one-sellable-result-not-one-ai-tool">Start with one sellable result, not one AI tool</h2>
<p>Test your service with one sentence: can a client understand the result, and who it is for, without asking a follow-up question? "Blog posts for small fitness studios, researched, written, and edited, delivered weekly" passes that test. "AI services" does not.</p>
<p>Three service types package well for beginners. Content systems, such as weekly blog posts or social content, suit writers and organisers. Chatbot setup suits sellers who can build a simple, tested bot for one niche, and our guide to <a href="/ai-earning/how-to-sell-ai-chatbots-to-small-businesses" rel="noopener" target="_self">selling AI chatbots to small businesses</a> covers that route in detail. Workflow automation suits sellers who can connect tools a small business already uses. Pick a niche and a buyer before you pick more tools, because the same service sells very differently to a restaurant, a coach, and a software company.</p>
<p>If you are new to freelancing itself, start with our guide to <a href="/freelancing/how-to-start-freelancing" rel="noopener" target="_self">starting freelancing</a>, then come back here to package the AI part of your offer.</p>
<h2 id="turn-your-service-into-three-clear-packages">Turn your service into three clear packages</h2>
<p>A package states what is included, what is excluded, how many revisions are allowed, how long delivery takes, and how long support lasts. Three tiers work well: a starter package for a small first job, a standard package that most clients should choose, and a premium package that adds strategy, setup, or support.</p>

<p>Leave out of every package the things that cause scope creep: unlimited revisions, extra formats you did not quote for, and ongoing maintenance unless it is priced as a separate retainer. Scope limits protect you and the client, because both sides know exactly what "done" means. State exclusions in plain language in the offer, not in small print discovered later.</p>
<h2 id="price-the-package-then-check-what-you-keep">Price the package, then check what you keep</h2>
<p>Price from scope and value, not from hours saved by AI. A client pays for a finished, checked result, and for the fact that you take responsibility for it. Add tool costs, platform fees, revision time, and unpaid admin to your price before you quote, or you will discover after delivery that the job paid less than you thought. Check the live fee page of any marketplace before you quote, because platform fees change, and a fee figure copied from an old guide can make your quote wrong.</p>
<p>A worked example, using illustrative figures only, shows why the check matters. Suppose a standard package sells for 400 units of your local currency. After a marketplace fee, payment processing, and a paid tool subscription shared across several jobs, the amount you keep is noticeably less than 400. Work that subtraction out for your own prices and your own platform before you publish a package, and adjust the package price or the scope until the amount you keep is worth the work.</p>
<p>Use project pricing for defined deliverables, a retainer for regular monthly work with clear limits, and a small paid trial when a client is unsure. Avoid hourly-only pricing for AI assisted work, because it punishes you for working faster and invites arguments about how long a job "should" take.</p>
<h2 id="choose-where-to-sell-it-marketplace-direct-outreach-or-both">Choose where to sell it: marketplace, direct outreach, or both</h2>
<p>Fixed package marketplaces suit services with a clear deliverable and a clear price, because buyers compare offers quickly. Proposal platforms suit custom work, where you lead with the client's problem, not with the tool. Direct outreach and LinkedIn suit local and niche buyers with a visible problem, such as a business with no recent content or unanswered customer questions.</p>
<p>Fiverr's <a href="https://www.fiverr.com/categories/ai-services" rel="noopener" target="_blank">AI services category</a> shows how buyers browse AI offers, including chatbot setup, AI content editing, and AI consulting, which is useful research for naming and positioning your own package. Whichever route you choose, work one channel properly for a few weeks before adding another, and keep your offer identical across channels so buyers do not get confused by different prices for the same work.</p>
<h2 id="write-a-gig-and-proposal-that-a-buyer-can-trust">Write a gig and proposal that a buyer can trust</h2>
<p>A gig title should name the result and the buyer, not the tool. An opening line should name the client's problem in their words. A short proposal structure works well: the problem as you understand it, your approach in two or three lines, proof from labelled samples or a demo, the deliverables and the price, and a clear next step.</p>
<p>Portfolio proof for AI services must be honest. Label samples as samples, show a demo for technical services such as chatbots or automation, and add a short walkthrough that explains what the demo does. A screenshot alone proves little. A sample, a demo, and a short explanation prove that you can deliver and that you understand the client's world.</p>
<h2 id="qualify-the-client-before-you-start">Qualify the client before you start</h2>
<p>Ask intake questions before you accept work. For content, ask about the audience, the tone, and who approves the work. For chatbot and automation offers, ask what systems the client uses, who owns the accounts, and what happens when the bot does not know an answer. Red flags include unclear ownership, no access to the systems you need, rushed deadlines with no brief, and requests to use private or confidential data in a public AI tool. When you see those flags, pause, suggest a smaller first project, or decline. A bad first client costs more time than no client at all.</p>
<h2 id="deliver-it-well-a-repeatable-workflow">Deliver it well: a repeatable workflow</h2>
<ol>
<li>Confirm the brief in writing, including deliverables, revisions, and the deadline.</li>
<li>Draft with AI, then edit, check facts, and match the client's brand voice. Never deliver raw AI output.</li>
<li>Test automation and chatbot flows before handover, using real example questions, not only the easy ones.</li>
<li>Deliver files, documentation, and a short handover note that says what was built, how to use it, and what to do when something goes wrong.</li>
</ol>
<p>Run a quality checklist before you send anything: facts, numbers, names, and links checked; originality checked; brand voice matched; formatting consistent; and files open correctly. That checklist is what separates a professional AI service from a quick generation that a client will regret buying.</p>
<h2 id="be-clear-about-ai-use-and-client-data">Be clear about AI use and client data</h2>
<p>Use simple disclosure wording in your proposal, such as: "AI tools assist with drafting and research. I review, edit, and check every deliverable before delivery, and I remain responsible for the final work." Do not paste client confidential data, customer lists, or unpublished business information into a public AI tool unless the client has agreed and the tool's terms allow it. Explain to clients, in plain terms, the limits of AI output: it can be outdated, it can invent details, and AI generated images and text have licensing limits that vary by tool and by country. Check the commercial use terms of each tool you use before you sell work made with it.</p>
<h2 id="handle-common-objections-without-discounting-on-the-spot">Handle common objections without discounting on the spot</h2>
<p>When a client says "AI is cheap, so why should I pay you?", answer with the result and the responsibility: they pay for a checked, delivered result, for revisions, and for someone who fixes problems. When a client asks "Did you just generate this with AI?", be honest about your process and show the editing and checking steps. When a client asks "Can you do it faster and cheaper?", do not discount on the spot. Offer a smaller scope at a lower price, or a paid trial, so the price stays tied to the work.</p>
<h2 id="turn-one-project-into-repeat-work">Turn one project into repeat work</h2>
<p>Offer maintenance and optimisation for automation and chatbot clients as a separate, priced retainer with clear limits, and offer a monthly content or support retainer for content clients. At the end of a good project, ask for a review and for permission to share anonymised results in your portfolio. One happy client who returns monthly is worth more than five one-off jobs that each need new outreach.</p>

<h2 id="a-realistic-first-30-days">A realistic first 30 days</h2>
<p>Week 1: choose one service, one buyer, and one package. Week 2: build two strong samples and one demo. Week 3: publish the offer and send targeted outreach, following up once. Week 4: deliver your first job, learn from replies, and refine one thing at a time. Results vary with experience, location, demand, time invested, competition, expenses, and platform fees, and income is never guaranteed. If you also want a broader starting point, read our beginner guide to <a href="/ai-earning/how-to-make-money-with-ai" rel="noopener" target="_self">making money with AI</a>, and for client finding in general, read our guide to <a href="/freelancing/how-to-find-freelance-clients" rel="noopener" target="_self">finding freelance clients</a>. You can browse all of our <a href="/ai-earning" rel="noopener" target="_self">AI earning guides</a> for the full collection.</p>
<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 id="what-ai-services-can-i-offer-without-coding">What AI services can I offer without coding?</h3>
<p>Content writing and editing, research summaries, social content systems, review summaries, and simple no-code automation. Chatbot setup is also possible with no-code tools, though you should test every flow before handover.</p>
<h3 id="do-i-need-to-tell-clients-that-i-use-ai">Do I need to tell clients that I use AI?</h3>
<p>Yes, in plain terms, in your proposal. Disclosure builds trust, and it protects you if a client later questions how the work was made. State that you review, edit, and check every deliverable.</p>
<h3 id="how-should-i-price-my-first-ai-service-package">How should I price my first AI service package?</h3>
<p>Price from scope and value, add tool costs and platform fees, and check the live fee page before quoting. Start with a flat fee for a clearly defined deliverable, and adjust after your first few jobs.</p>
<h3 id="what-should-be-included-in-an-ai-service-package">What should be included in an AI service package?</h3>
<p>Deliverables, exclusions, revision rounds, turnaround time, and a support period, all stated plainly. A package without exclusions invites scope creep.</p>
<h3 id="how-do-i-find-clients-for-ai-services">How do I find clients for AI services?</h3>
<p>Start with one channel: a marketplace, proposal platform, or direct outreach to buyers with a visible problem. Show a labelled sample or a demo, follow up once, and refine one thing at a time when replies are low.</p>

<p>You are responsible for the final work, which is why every deliverable needs a human check for facts, numbers, names, and links before delivery. If an error reaches a client, fix it promptly and explain what changed.</p>
