<h2 id="what-house-sitting-is-and-what-it-is-not">What house sitting is, and what it is not</h2>
<p>House sitting means staying in someone's home while they are away, and looking after the home, and often the pets, the plants, and the garden. The homeowner may be on holiday or travelling for work. In return you get a free place to stay, and sometimes cash pay as well.</p>
<p>House sitting is not free travel with no work. You have real duties, and the homeowner expects them done well. You cannot leave the home empty for days or bring extra guests without asking. It is also not a full time income. Sits are irregular, homeowners can cancel, and the best sits go to sitters with reviews.</p>

<h2 id="what-house-sitters-actually-do">What house sitters actually do</h2>
<p>The duties change with every home, but the core list is the same. You keep the home secure by locking doors and windows, turning lights on and off, and collecting the mail. You keep it clean, water the plants, and take the bins out. If there are pets, you feed them, walk them, clean up after them, and watch their health. Report problems at once, such as a leak or a sick pet.</p>
<h2 id="types-of-house-sits">Types of house sits</h2>
<p>Sits come in several shapes. A live in sit means you stay in the home day and night. A live out sit means you visit daily but sleep at your own home. A pet sit is a live in sit where animal care is the main job. Local sits are near your home and suit beginners, because you can meet the homeowner first and you have no travel cost. Travel sits take you to another city or country, and they suit sitters who work online or who have free time. Start with local sits. They are easier to win, and they build the reviews you need for travel sits.</p>
<h2 id="step-1-get-experience-with-no-experience">Step 1: Get experience with no experience</h2>
<p>Every sitter starts with no reviews. The way past that is to get experience close to home. Offer to sit for friends, family, and neighbours, even for a weekend, and ask them to write you a short reference. Offer to look after a home while the owners are away, and treat it like a paid sit. Volunteer for a local animal shelter or a pet sitting job first, because pet care experience is what most homeowners ask about. Our guide on <a href="https://earnquill.com/side-hustles/how-to-start-a-pet-sitting-side-hustle" rel="noopener" target="_blank">how to start a pet sitting side hustle</a> explains how to build that experience, and it transfers directly to house sitting.</p>
<p>Two or three good references from people you know are enough to start applying on a platform.</p>
<h2 id="step-2-build-a-profile-that-wins-trust">Step 2: Build a profile that wins trust</h2>
<p>Your profile is your shop window. Homeowners choose a stranger to trust with their home and their pets, so your profile must answer their worries before they ask. Use a clear, friendly photo of yourself. Write a short bio in plain words: who you are, why you house sit, what experience you have, and what kind of sits you want. Mention practical skills: pet care, gardening, basic repairs, and a clean driving licence. Add your references, and complete the identity and background checks the platform offers. </p>

<h2 id="step-4-apply-fast-and-interview-well">Step 4: Apply fast and interview well</h2>
<p>Good sits attract many applications, so speed matters. Check new listings two or three times a day, and apply within hours of a listing going up. Read the whole listing, and write a short message that answers it directly. Say why you fit this sit, mention the pets by name, and note your dates and your experience. Do not send the same message to every listing, because homeowners can tell.</p>
<p>If the homeowner replies, expect a video call. Ask about the trip dates, the pets, the daily routine, the home rules, and what happens if plans change. Ask to see the home on video, and ask for the vet details and the emergency contacts. If a homeowner will not answer questions or pressures you to confirm at once, walk away.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-agree-everything-in-writing">Step 5: Agree everything in writing</h2>
<p>Before you confirm a sit, agree the details in writing, in a message or a short document. This protects you and the homeowner. Agree the exact dates and arrival and departure times, the duties, the pet care routine, the pay if any, who pays for pet food and cleaning supplies, what happens if the homeowner returns early, and what happens if you must cancel. Agree the emergency plan: the vet, the plumber, a trusted neighbour, and how to reach the homeowner. Agree the house rules on guests, smoking, and use of the car. Keep a copy of the agreement and the listing.</p>

<p>Paid sits usually pay by the night. Those figures are from 2024, so treat them as a guide and check current local listings before you set your rate. Most sits are paid by the day, not by the hour, so a nightly rate is the better way to judge a sit.</p>

<h2 id="is-house-sitting-worth-it">Is house sitting worth it</h2>
<p>House sitting is worth it if you are flexible, you like animals and quiet homes, and you can work online or take time off. It is a poor choice if you need steady cash every week, if you cannot handle a cancellation, or if you dislike being alone in a strange home. Before you accept a sit, add up what you receive, in cash or in saved rent, and compare it with what is asked. If the work is heavy and the reward is small, say no. <h2 id="staying-safe-in-someone-else-39-s-home">Staying safe in someone else's home</h2>
<p>Staying in a stranger's home carries real risks, so take them seriously. Always meet the homeowner on a video call before you confirm, and check the listing for photos, reviews, and clear details. Tell someone you trust where you are going, the address, and the dates, and check in with them when you arrive and when you leave. On arrival, check that the home matches the listing and that you have the emergency numbers. If you feel unsafe, leave and contact the platform. Never share your bank details or passwords with a homeowner, and never pay money to secure a sit. If a listing asks for either, report it to the platform and check the <a href="https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/job-scams" rel="noopener" target="_blank">US Federal Trade Commission guide to job scams</a>.</p>
<h2 id="risks-and-scams-to-watch-for">Risks and scams to watch for</h2>
<p>The main scam in house sitting is a fake listing that asks you for money. A real homeowner never asks a sitter to pay to secure a sit, to send a deposit, or to share bank details. A real platform never asks you to pay a listing fee for the homeowner. Check every listing for photos, reviews, and clear details, and meet the homeowner on a video call before you confirm. If a listing has no photos, no reviews, and a host who will not talk to you, walk away and report it to the platform.</p>
<h2 id="when-things-go-wrong">When things go wrong</h2>
<p>Things go wrong on sits, and a good sitter is ready for them. If a pet falls ill, call the vet the homeowner named, and tell the homeowner at once. If a pipe bursts or an appliance breaks, turn off the water or the power if it is safe to do so, take photos, and call the homeowner and the emergency contact. If the alarm goes off, follow the instructions the homeowner gave you, and call the alarm company if you cannot stop it. If the homeowner cannot be reached, use the emergency contact they named. If something breaks, tell the homeowner at once, take photos, and agree how to fix it.</p>

<h3 id="what-does-a-house-sitter-do">What does a house sitter do?</h3>
<p>A house sitter stays in someone's home while they are away, and looks after the home, the pets, the plants, and the garden. Duties include locking up, collecting mail, keeping the home clean, feeding and walking pets, watering plants, and reporting problems at once. </p>
<h3 id="do-house-sitters-get-paid">Do house sitters get paid?</h3>
<p>Some do and some do not. Paid sits usually pay a nightly rate, often about 25 to 55 dollars a night according to Rover data reported in 2024, with higher rates in big cities. Most sits on the big platforms are exchange sits, where you stay for free and receive no cash.</p>
<h3 id="how-much-can-you-make-house-sitting">How much can you make house sitting?</h3>
<p>It depends on whether you take paid sits or exchange sits, and on your city. Paid sits of 25 to 55 dollars a night, as reported in 2024, can add up over a long sit, but sits are irregular and homeowners can cancel. Exchange sits save you rent, which is real value but not cash. </p>
<h3 id="do-i-need-experience-to-house-sit">Do I need experience to house sit?</h3>
<p>No, but you do need references. Start with sits for friends, family, and neighbours, even unpaid ones, and ask them to write you a short reference. Pet sitting experience helps a lot, because most sits involve pets. Two or three good references are enough to start applying on a platform.</p>
<h3 id="is-house-sitting-safe">Is house sitting safe?</h3>
<p>It can be, if you take care. Always meet the homeowner on a video call first, check the listing for photos and reviews, and tell someone you trust where you are going and when. Check that the home matches the listing when you arrive, and leave if you feel unsafe. Never pay money to secure a sit, and never share your bank details with a homeowner.</p>

Nomador, MindMyHouse, and HouseCarers are smaller and cheaper, but their fees were last reported in 2024. </p>
