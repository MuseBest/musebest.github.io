<p>A pet sitting side hustle pays you to look after other people's animals while they are at work or on holiday. If you love animals and you want flexible work around a job or study, this is one of the lowest-cost side hustles you can start.</p>

<h2 id="what-a-pet-sitting-side-hustle-is-and-how-it-differs-from-dog-walking">What a pet sitting side hustle is, and how it differs from dog walking</h2>
<p>Pet sitting means looking after other people's animals for money. The main services are drop-in visits, doggy day care, overnight sitting, house sitting, and boarding in your own home. You feed the pet, refresh water, give medication, play, and send the owner photo updates.</p>
<p>Pet sitting is distinct from dog walking, and the difference matters for your prices and your day. Dog walking is a walk, usually 20 to 30 minutes, near your home. Pet sitting is overnight or in-home care, which means you stay with the pet for hours or days, often in the owner's home, and you take responsibility for the animal around the clock. Overnight sitting and boarding pay more than walks, because the owner is trusting you with their home and their pet while they are away. Our guide to <a href="https://earnquill.com/side-hustles/how-to-make-money-with-a-dog-walking-side-hustle" rel="noopener" target="_blank">making money with a dog walking side hustle</a> covers the walking side of pet care if you want to compare the two.</p>
<p>The work suits you if you are comfortable with animals, including accidents and anxious pets, and you can be flexible on weekends and holidays, which is exactly when owners travel. It does not suit you if you need money within days, because platform payouts take days, or if you cannot handle a pet getting sick or hurt in your care. It also does not suit you if you rent a home where pets are not allowed and you plan to host animals there.</p>
<h2 id="step-1-pick-your-services">Step 1: Pick your services</h2>
<p>You do not need to offer everything on day one. Pick one or two services you can deliver well, then expand. Here is the menu, roughly from easiest to start to most involved:</p>
<ul>
<li>Drop-in visits. A short stop to feed, refresh water, scoop a litter box, let a dog out, and give some company. Drop-in visits are gold for cat owners and for dogs who need a midday check while their owners travel.</li>
<li>Dog walking. Usually 20, 30, or 60-minute walks, often at midday for working owners. This is the recurring engine of a pet care business, because a weekday noon walk repeats for months.</li>
<li>Doggy day care. Watching a dog at your home during work hours.</li>
<li>Overnight sitting or house sitting. You stay at the client's home overnight. This is the highest-paying service, it books out fast around holidays, and pets stay comfortable in their own space.</li>
<li>Boarding in your home. Hosting pets at your place. This pays well, but check local rules and your lease first, and start here only once you are established.</li>
<li>Pet taxi. Driving pets to the vet, groomer, or day care. A simple add-on that busy owners happily pay for.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="step-2-check-the-basic-requirements">Step 2: Check the basic requirements</h2>
<p>The barrier to entry is low, but there are rules. On Rover, you must be at least 18 to sit or walk, and you must pass a background check before your profile goes live. Rover runs background checks through a third-party provider. Other platforms have similar age and check rules.</p>
<p>Licensing depends on where you live. Rover tells sitters to follow local laws and regulations on licensing, and to check with their local government. Some cities and counties require a business licence for pet care, and some have rules about how many animals you can board in a home. Search your city or county for pet care licensing before you advertise. If you board animals in your home, check your lease or your homeowners association rules too, because some forbid it.</p>
<p>You do not need formal credentials, but owners book sitters who can show they have handled animals before, even if that is your own pets, family dogs, or volunteering at a shelter. Be honest in your meet and greets about your experience, and start with easygoing pets until you have built confidence. Confidence with animals is built one calm, well-handled visit at a time.</p>
<h2 id="step-3-get-certified-and-insured">Step 3: Get certified and insured</h2>
<p>A pet first aid certificate is the cheapest trust signal you can buy. The American Red Cross runs a 35-minute online cat and dog first aid course that covers vital signs, breathing and cardiac emergencies, wounds, and seizures. The course costs $25, and you can start it at once. Add "Pet CPR and First Aid certified" to your profile the day you complete it, because it answers the first question in every owner's head before they even ask it. You can find the course on the <a href="https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/classes/cat-and-dog-first-aid-online/a6R0V0000015EUf.html?cgid=online-safety-classes&amp;isCourse=true&amp;storedistance=undefined&amp;bvstate=pg:2/ct:r" rel="noopener" target="_blank">American Red Cross website</a>.</p>
<p>Insurance is the step new sitters skip and established sitters insist on. If a dog in your care gets injured, or injures someone else, you are personally liable. Specialist pet sitter insurance through Pet Care Insurance starts at about $29.43 a month, or $332.20 a year. Basic cover from other providers can run as low as about $13 a month for a solo sitter just starting out. Industry experts recommend at least $25,000 in care, custody, and control cover, and full policies typically cost $300 to $1,000 or more a year.</p>
<p>Platform protection is not the same as your own insurance. Rover's guarantee covers certain costs from certain injuries to the pet in your care, plus damage to your property, when the service was booked and paid through Rover. It excludes injury to you or the owner, bookings made outside Rover, preventive care, illness and pre-existing conditions, and preventable issues such as fleas and ticks. If you take private clients outside a platform, you need your own policy, because the platform guarantee does not cover those bookings.</p>
<h2 id="step-4-set-rates-that-survive-the-platform-fee">Step 4: Set rates that survive the platform fee</h2>

Pet owners pay a separate booking fee of 11 percent. Rover also charges a profile review fee of $49 to $79 depending on location. Some US sitters may be in a pilot program with different fees, and fees in California differ for legal reasons, so check the fee shown on your own account. You can read the details on <a href="https://support.rover.com/hc/en-us/articles/203062400-Is-Rover-free" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Rover's help page about fees</a>.</p>

<p>Wag, another pet care marketplace, takes roughly 40 percent, which is far more than Rover's 20 percent. That is a real difference in what you keep, so compare platforms before you commit to one.</p>
<p>Price per unit of service: per walk, per drop-in visit, per night for overnights. Then build packages on top, because packages are where recurring revenue lives. A discounted weekly bundle, such as five midday walks, locks in a client and smooths your income. The discount is small, and the loyalty is large. Add holiday premiums for peak dates such as Thanksgiving, the December holidays, and summer travel, and set that policy upfront so it is never awkward. Raise prices as you fill up. When your calendar is tight, that is the market telling you to charge more. New clients pay the new rate.</p>
<p>Do not race to the bottom. The average sitter near you might charge $15 to $20 per visit, but established sitters report charging $40 or more per walk or drop-in and $50 to $70 for boarding. New sitters undercut to win the first few reviews, then raise rates fast. Owners trust their pet to the person who feels like a professional, not the cheapest option.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-build-a-profile-owners-trust">Step 5: Build a profile owners trust</h2>
<p>Your profile is your shopfront. The sitters who book consistently do these things. Lead with a great photo, you with a dog, smiling, outdoors, not a selfie from the couch. Write a bio about the dog, not about you. "Your pup will get a 30-minute sniff walk and a photo update after every visit" beats "I have loved animals my whole life." List your certification and your experience in the first two sentences. Be specific about your ideal client. Niching down, such as anxious rescue dogs or senior dogs, is a proven way to stand out. Say yes to small jobs first. A midday Tuesday walk earns the review that unlocks the week-long boarding job.</p>
<p>Target your ideal client. Busy professionals who work long hours and frequent travellers are the two biggest groups. Partner locally with vet offices, groomers, and dog trainers, because they are asked for referrals constantly. A friendly introduction and a stack of cards turns them into a steady client pipeline. Ask for a review after every single booking. The first five reviews are the hardest. After that, the algorithm starts working for you.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-run-the-meet-and-greet">Step 6: Run the meet and greet</h2>
<p>Never take a booking without meeting the pet first. A meet and greet protects you and shows the owner how you handle their pet. It is also the moment when trust is built, so treat it like part of the product.</p>
<p><img src="/covers/side-hustles-pet-sitting-meet-greet.jpg" alt="Pet sitter meeting a dog owner at the front door of a house while a small dog greets them" /></p>
<p>Before the first paid service, collect a real intake form for every client:</p>
<ul>
<li>Pet name, breed, age, and temperament, and the honest details: does she pull, is he reactive to other dogs, any bite history?</li>
<li>Feeding and medication, with amounts, times, where it is stored, and exactly how medicines are given.</li>
<li>Vet details and emergency contacts, with the clinic name, phone, and a backup person who can authorise care.</li>
<li>Home details, alarm codes, where the leash and waste bags live, quirks of the door and the key, and rooms you should not enter.</li>
<li>Permissions, including vet authorisation up to a set amount, and written agreement to handle emergencies if the owner cannot be reached.</li>
</ul>
<p>Decide how you store and label keys. Never label a key with the client's name or address. Use a code only you can read, store keys securely, confirm the key works during the meet and greet, and return keys promptly when a client stops using you. Good key handling is a visible trust signal.</p>
<p>During the visit, over-communicate. Send photo updates after every visit. It costs nothing and it is why clients rebook. Tell the owner what you did, how the pet ate, and anything unusual you noticed. If a pet gets sick or hurt, call the owner and the vet at once, follow the instructions on the intake form, and write down what happened.</p>
<h2 id="step-7-win-repeat-clients-and-grow">Step 7: Win repeat clients and grow</h2>
<p>Pet sitting is relationship-based. Once owners trust you with their pets, they tend to rebook and refer friends, so a few reliable regular clients matter more than a long list of one-off bookings. Start a referral program early. Most of an established sitter's business comes from client referrals.</p>

<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Skipping insurance and relying on a platform guarantee that does not cover private clients.</li>
<li>Pricing before the fee, then finding the 20 percent cut leaves you with too little.</li>
<li>Skipping the meet and greet, then meeting a reactive dog for the first time on a paid visit.</li>
<li>Taking bookings off the platform to dodge the fee, which voids the platform guarantee.</li>
<li>Forgetting taxes. Net earnings of $400 trigger self-employment tax, so keep every record.</li>
<li>Saying yes to every animal, including ones you are not ready to handle.</li>
<li>Labelling keys with the client's name and address.</li>
<li>Waiting for clients to find you, instead of asking every happy customer for a review and a referral.</li>
<li>Keeping all your bookings on one platform, which owns the client relationship and can change its terms.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="risks-and-scams-to-watch-for">Risks and scams to watch for</h2>
<p>The main risks with a pet sitting side hustle are an injured animal, a missed local rule, and a price that ignores fees. A pet can get sick or hurt in your care, and a bad week with one animal can cost more than a good week earns. Meet every pet first, ask about medical needs, and decide in advance what you would refuse. If a pet has a bite history or severe anxiety that you cannot manage safely, say no. That protects the pet, the owner, and you.</p>
<p>Watch for scams aimed at new sitters. Be wary of any client who asks you to pay for supplies upfront and promises to reimburse you, or who asks for your bank details before a booking is confirmed. Real clients book through the platform or pay you directly after the work is done. Never share your bank login or a verification code with anyone. If a job offer asks you to buy a starter kit or pay a fee to join, walk away. Legitimate platforms charge a profile review fee, which is listed on their own help pages, and nothing else upfront.</p>
<p>Taxes apply from your first booking. In the United States, the <a href="https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employment-tax-social-security-and-medicare-taxes" rel="noopener" target="_blank">IRS self-employment tax page</a> explains that you generally owe self-employment tax once your net earnings reach $400 in a year. The rate is 15.3 percent, which covers Social Security and Medicare, and the IRS expects quarterly estimated payments. Keep a simple log of every booking, with the date, the client, the service, and what you were paid. Rules differ by country, so check your local tax authority if you are outside the United States.</p>
<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 id="how-much-do-pet-sitters-actually-make">How much do pet sitters actually make?</h3>
<p>It depends on services and market. Current 2026 figures are about $17 an hour equivalent for dog walking on Rover, $35 to $75 a night for overnight boarding, and $16 to $23 an hour in typical job postings. The BLS lists a median of $17.08 an hour for animal care and service workers in May 2025, but that covers employed workers, not marketplace sitters.</p>
<h3 id="do-i-need-a-licence-or-certification-to-pet-sit">Do I need a licence or certification to pet sit?</h3>
<p>No licence is required to start in most places, and platforms only require you to be 18 and pass a background check. Local rules differ, so check with your city or county government. A pet first aid and CPR certificate, about $25 and 35 minutes online with the Red Cross, is the highest-value credential you can add.</p>
<h3 id="rover-or-wag-which-pays-sitters-better">Rover or Wag, which pays sitters better?</h3>
<p>Rover generally keeps more in your pocket. It takes a 20 percent service fee per booking, so you keep 80 percent. Wag takes roughly 40 percent. Rates also vary by city, so check what local sitters charge before you set your prices.</p>
<h3 id="do-i-need-insurance-as-a-pet-sitter">Do I need insurance as a pet sitter?</h3>
<p>Yes, especially for private clients outside platforms. Specialist cover starts at about $29 a month, and experts recommend at least $25,000 in care, custody, and control cover. Platform guarantees only cover bookings made through the platform, so private clients need your own policy.</p>
<h3 id="how-do-i-get-my-first-pet-sitting-clients-with-no-experience">How do I get my first pet sitting clients with no experience?</h3>
<p>Start on a platform to collect reviews, say yes to small walks and drop-in visits, and lean on your real-life network, such as friends' dogs, neighbours, and coworkers. Offer a meet and greet, ask every happy customer for a referral, and introduce yourself to local vets and groomers.</p>
<h3 id="is-pet-sitting-different-from-dog-walking">Is pet sitting different from dog walking?</h3>
<p>Yes. Dog walking is a walk, usually 20 to 30 minutes. Pet sitting is overnight or in-home care, which means you stay with the pet for hours or days, often in the owner's home, and you take responsibility for feeding, medication, and the animal's safety around the clock. Overnight sitting pays more than walks for that reason.</p>
