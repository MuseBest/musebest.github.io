How to Start a Babysitting Side Hustle in 2026

Parents do not hire the cheapest babysitter. They hire the one they trust with their children. That is good news for a beginner, because trust is built with training, clear communication, and reliable work, not with years of experience. This guide shows you how to start, what to charge, how to find your first families, and how to win regular bookings.

What a babysitting side hustle is

A babysitting side hustle means caring for children in their own home while their parents are out, usually for a few hours in the evening or at the weekend. You play with the children, serve a meal the parents have left ready, help with bedtime, and stay until the parents return. Most sittings last three to five hours.

A babysitter is not the same as a nanny. A nanny usually works regular full time hours for one family and often does laundry, school runs, and housework. A babysitter works occasional hours for several families and focuses on the children. A mother's helper works while a parent is at home, often with a baby. For a side hustle, babysitting is the right model, because the hours are flexible and you can build a roster of several families.

Who can start

You do not need a degree or a childcare qualification to start babysitting, but you do need to be reliable, calm, and good with children. Most families look for a sitter who is at least 16, and many prefer 18 or older for evening work and for driving children to activities. Younger sitters can still start with families they know, with daytime sits, and with a parent nearby at first. The American Red Cross runs a Babysitting Basics online course for ages 11 and older, which shows how young a sitter can start training. Age rules for paid work differ by country, so check local rules before you take paid work as a teenager. What matters most is your safety training, your references, and how you handle the first meeting.

Step 1: Get safety training first

Safety training is the single best investment a new sitter can make. It is also the first thing parents ask about. Book a first aid and CPR course before you look for work, and add a babysitting course if one is available near you. The American Red Cross Babysitting Basics online course takes about 4 hours, is designed for ages 11 and older, and costs 45 dollars, according to the Red Cross babysitting course page. It covers care for infants and children, what to do in an emergency, age appropriate activities, and the basics of starting a babysitting business. The certificate of completion is valid for two years. Ask about a background check. In the United States, parents often ask for one before they leave a sitter alone with their children.

Step 2: Choose your services and ages

Do not offer everything to everyone. Pick the ages and the services you are comfortable with, and say so clearly in your profile. The table below shows the usual service types and what each one involves.

Service Typical hours What it involves
Date night sitting Evenings, 3 to 5 hours Meal, play, bedtime routine
After school care Weekday afternoons School pickup, snack, homework help
Infant care Any time, shorter sits Feeding, nappy changes, naps
Overnight sitting Evening to morning Bedtime, night waking, breakfast
Event sitting Weekends Several children at a wedding or party

Infant care pays more than sitting for older children, because it needs more skill and more attention. Overnight and holiday sits pay more too. Special needs care pays more again, but only take it on if you have the training and the confidence. Start with the ages you know best, and add infant care after training and practice.

Step 3: Set your rate

Your rate should reflect your local market, your experience, and the number of children. Check what sitters near you charge, and do not copy a rate from another city, because rates vary a lot by place. In the United States, sitters on Care.com posted an average of 19.81 dollars an hour for one child in February 2026, according to Care.com data as reported by MaidStaff. Care.com suggests adding 0.50 to 1 dollar an hour for each extra child, and 1 to 2 dollars an hour for an infant. City rates ranged from 17.17 dollars an hour in San Antonio to 25.23 dollars in Seattle. These are posted asking rates, and they are US figures only.

Here is an example rate sheet for one sitter. Example only: these are example rates, not a recommendation for your area.

Sitting Example rate
One child, daytime or evening 19 dollars an hour
Two children 20 dollars an hour
Infant under one year 21 dollars an hour
After midnight 22 dollars an hour
Holiday or short notice 24 dollars an hour

Start near the local average, not below it. A rate that is too low is hard to raise later. When you have regular families and good references, raise your rate by 1 to 2 dollars an hour and tell families a month in advance.

Step 4: Find your first families

Your first two or three families matter more than any advert. Start with people who already know you. Tell friends, family, neighbours, and parents at your school or church that you are looking for babysitting work. Ask them to pass your name to families they trust. A personal recommendation beats any platform profile. Join local parent groups on Facebook and Nextdoor. Reply with a short message: your name, your training, your ages, your rate, and your availability. Babysitting platforms such as Care.com can help, but they take time to set up and they charge families or sitters for full access. Use them alongside personal referrals, not instead of them. Keep your profile short and honest: a clear photo, your training, the ages you sit for, your rate, your free evenings, and one reference.

Step 5: Your first sitting, step by step

A good first sitting is planned, not improvised. Ask the parents these questions before the booking: the children's ages, allergies, and medicines; the bedtime routine; what food is ready and what the children may eat; the emergency contacts and the nearest hospital; and what time the parents expect to be home. Arrive ten minutes early. Walk through the home with the parents, check where the first aid kit and the children's bedrooms are, and agree what to do if a child is sick or you cannot reach the parents.

During the sitting, follow the routine the parents gave you. Serve the meal they left ready, play the games they allow, and start bedtime on time. Keep your phone charged and nearby, but do not spend the evening on it. When they return, give a short report on food, sleep, and anything unusual. Then ask for a reference and for the next booking. That last step is how one sitting becomes a regular one.

What to bring: a simple babysitting kit

A small kit makes you look prepared and it saves the evening when a child is bored or scraped a knee. Keep it in a bag you can carry to any home. You do not need to spend much. Most of these items cost under 30 dollars in total: a small first aid kit with plasters and antiseptic wipes, two picture books, a colouring book and pencils, a simple card game, a phone charger, and a notebook and pen for emergency numbers and notes. Check your kit after every sitting and replace what you used.

Bookings, payments, and simple agreements

Keep bookings simple, but write the important parts down. A short message before each sitting should confirm the date, the start and finish time, the rate, the number of children, and how you will be paid. Most families pay by cash, bank transfer, or a payment app at the end of the sitting. Agree the method before the sitting, not after it. Set a cancellation rule and tell families about it. A fair rule is that a cancellation with less than 24 hours notice still pays for one hour, and a cancellation with less than 2 hours notice pays for two hours. If parents are more than 30 minutes late without calling, charge your hourly rate for the extra time. Keep a record of every sitting: the date, the family, the hours, the rate, and the amount paid.

Building a roster of repeat families

One off gigs pay for one evening. A roster of repeat families pays every week. Aim for three to five regular families who book you once or twice a week each. The way to build a roster is simple. Do good work, ask for the next booking before you leave, and follow up. After a sitting, message the family the next day to ask if they would like you again next week. Ask happy families to recommend you to their friends. Do not offer free sits to win referrals. Keep a note of each family's routine and the children's likes. That is what turns a good sitter into the family's first call.

Staying safe

You will be alone in a stranger's home, so take safety seriously. Meet a new family in person first, and bring a friend or a parent of your own if you are under 18. Tell someone you trust where you are going, the address, and what time you expect to be home. Check in with them when you arrive and when you leave. Do not accept a booking from a family you have not met. If something feels wrong, leave and call your trusted person. Never give a family your bank details, your password, or a copy of your ID in the first message. A real family will never ask for those.

Risks and scams to watch for

Fake families are the main scam in babysitting. A real family meets you in person, or on a video call, before the first sitting. A fake one asks for your bank details, your password, or a copy of your ID in the first message, or offers far more than the local rate for little work. Never send money to secure a sitting, and never pay for a list of families. The US Federal Trade Commission guide to job scams explains these tricks and says that honest employers never ask you to pay to get a job. If a booking feels wrong, say no and tell someone you trust.

What it costs and what to expect

Startup costs for babysitting are low. The table below shows the usual costs in US dollars. Prices vary by country and by what you already own.

First aid and CPR course 0 to 100 dollars Prices vary; some community courses are free
Babysitting Basics online course 45 dollars Red Cross, about 4 hours, valid 2 years
Background check 0 to 50 dollars Rules differ by country and state
Kit bag and supplies 20 to 30 dollars Books, first aid kit, colouring supplies

3 sittings times 4 hours equals 12 hours a week. 12 hours times 19 dollars equals 228 dollars a week. 228 dollars times 48 weeks equals 10,944 dollars a year, before travel, kit costs, and tax. Treat it as a ceiling, not a promise. Expect a slow start.

Taxes in plain terms

Babysitting income is taxable in most countries, even if you are paid in cash. Keep a record of every payment and set money aside for tax. Put aside 15 to 20 percent of your pay for tax. The exact rate depends on your country and your total income. In the United States, the tax rules depend on whether you are a household employee or an independent contractor. The IRS explains the rules in Publication 926, the Household Employer's Tax Guide, and it sets a cash wage threshold above which the family must handle payroll taxes. Check the current threshold on the IRS page, because it changes. Rules differ by country, so treat this section as general guidance only.

Common mistakes

  • Do not set your rate too low. A low rate is hard to raise later and it can worry parents.
  • Do not take a booking without asking about allergies, medicines, and emergency contacts.
  • Do not spend the sitting on your phone. Parents notice, and they will not rebook you.
  • Do not skip the first aid course. It is the one thing parents ask about most.
  • Do not work without a simple agreement on rate, hours, and cancellations.
  • Do not forget to ask for the next booking before you leave.

Frequently asked questions

How much do babysitters charge per hour?

It depends on your city, your experience, and the number of children. In the United States, sitters on Care.com posted an average of 19.81 dollars an hour for one child in February 2026, with city rates from 17.17 dollars in San Antonio to 25.23 dollars in Seattle. Set your rate near the local average.

How do I start babysitting with no experience?

Start with safety training, then sit for families you know. Take the Red Cross Babysitting Basics course, which takes about 4 hours and is designed for ages 11 and older. Sit for a friend or a neighbour at your normal rate, ask for a reference, and use it to win your next family.

Do I need a licence to babysit?

No licence is usually needed for occasional babysitting in a family's home. Some places have rules for regular childcare or for caring for children in your own home. Check your local rules before you take regular work or care for children in your own home.

What age can you start babysitting?

Most families look for a sitter who is at least 16, and many prefer 18 or older for evening work. Younger sitters can start with daytime sits for families they know, and with training such as the Red Cross Babysitting Basics course, which is designed for ages 11 and older. Age rules for paid work differ by country, so check your local rules.

How do babysitters get paid?

Most families pay by cash, bank transfer, or a payment app at the end of the sitting. Agree the method and the rate before the sitting, not after it. Keep a record of every payment, because babysitting income is taxable in most countries.

Is babysitting a good side hustle?

Yes, if you like children and you want flexible evening and weekend work with low startup costs. It suits students and anyone who wants steady work from a small roster of families. It does not suit you if you need a guaranteed income, because hours depend on bookings.

Conclusion

To start a babysitting side hustle, get your safety training first, choose the ages and services you are comfortable with, set your rate near the local average, and win your first families through personal recommendations. Do one good sitting at a time, and build a roster of three to five regular families.
