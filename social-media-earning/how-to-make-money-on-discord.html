How to Make Money on Discord in 2026

Learning how to make money on Discord starts with one honest fact. Discord does not pay you for views. There is no ad revenue share for posting in a server, and no creator fund that pays per message. The money comes from the people in your server, not from Discord itself. You earn when members pay for access, buy something you sell, hire you, or when a brand pays to reach your community. That is a slower start than a platform with ads, but a member who chose to join your server is easier to reach than a follower who may never see your next post.

Can you make money on Discord?

Yes, but only if you build something people value enough to pay for. Discord is a chat app built around servers, which are private spaces where people talk by text, voice and video. A server can be free, paid, or a mix of both. The free part attracts members and builds trust, and the paid part offers something extra.

The size that matters is engagement, not headcount. A server with 300 active members is worth more than a server with 5,000 silent ones, because sponsors pay for attention and trust. Before you plan income, ask a plain question. Would a stranger pay each month for what happens inside your server? If the answer is no, build the value first.

Discord is not a discovery platform. People do not stumble across your server the way they find a video on YouTube. You have to bring members in from somewhere else, such as a YouTube channel, an Instagram account or a blog. Plan for that from the start.

The 7 ways to earn on Discord

Most earning servers combine two or three of these streams. Start with one, prove it works, then add another.

Income stream How it pays Best for Time to first income
Server Subscriptions Members pay monthly inside Discord Creators with an engaged server Weeks after setup
External memberships Members pay through Patreon, Whop or Ko-fi Creators who want more control Weeks after setup
Digital products One-off sales of guides, templates or presets Designers, writers, musicians, coaches Days after launch
Coaching and services One-to-one sessions or fixed-price work People with a skill others want to learn Days after first booking
Paid events Entry fees or sponsor-funded prize pools Gaming, hobby and learning servers Weeks after first event
Sponsorships Brands pay for a channel, event or announcement Servers with 2,000 or more engaged members Months
Affiliate links Commission when members buy a tool you recommend Tool-heavy niches such as design, gaming or music Weeks after first sale

Subscriptions produce the steadiest income, because they repeat each month. Digital products and services produce the fastest first income, because you can sell one item without waiting for a large audience.

Step 1: Pick a niche and one clear offer

A niche is the specific topic your server covers. "Gaming" is not a niche. "Competitive Valorant coaching for teenagers" is a niche. A narrow topic helps people decide in seconds whether the server is for them, and it helps you know exactly what to post.

Pick a topic you can talk about for a year without getting bored, and one where people already spend money. Good signs are that people pay for courses, tools or coaching in the topic, and that they ask the same questions again and again.

Then define one clear offer. An offer is what a paying member gets, stated in one sentence. Examples include "weekly live coaching and a private channel where I review your games" or "daily study rooms with a tutor on call each evening". If you cannot explain the offer in one sentence, it is not clear enough to sell. Test the offer on ten people who fit your audience before you build anything around it. If they would not pay, change the offer, not the price.

Step 2: Set up a server that sells the offer

Set up the server so a new member understands the free part and the paid part within a minute of joining.

  1. Create the server and switch it to a Community server. Community servers unlock the analytics and moderation tools you need, and they are a requirement for Discord's own monetisation features. You can change this in Server Settings.
  2. Turn on two-factor authentication. Discord requires it for monetisation, and it protects your account. You will also need to require it for moderators.
  3. Build two channel groups. A free group with a welcome channel, general chat, a resources channel and announcements. A paid group with the private channels your offer promises.
  4. Set up roles. A role controls what a member can see and do. Create a free member role and a paid member role, then set the paid channels so only the paid role can see them.
  5. Add a welcome message and rules. Say what the server is for, what members get free, what the paid tier adds, and how to upgrade. Keep the rules short.
  6. Add a bot for welcome messages and role assignment. A bot such as MEE6 or Carl-bot can greet new members and hand out roles automatically. You do not need to code anything to use it.

Keep the free part genuinely useful. If the free channels are empty, nobody will trust you enough to pay. Post something useful every day for at least two weeks before you launch a paid tier, so new members see a live community, not a sales page.

Step 3: Turn on Server Subscriptions

Server Subscriptions are Discord's built-in paid membership feature. Members subscribe inside Discord and get the role and channels you set. Discord handles the billing, and you keep 90% of the revenue while Discord keeps 10%. That 90/10 split is the headline figure, but it is not the whole story.

Eligibility matters more than most guides admit. As of November 2026, Discord's own rules say the server owner must be based in the United States, with US bank details for Stripe, Discord's payment provider. If you live outside the US, you cannot turn on Server Subscriptions. The owner must also be 18 or older, have two-factor authentication on, and run a server in good standing.

Payouts also have thresholds. Your first payout is released only after you have earned at least $100. After that, each later payout needs at least $25 earned. Payouts are reviewed at the end of each month. Subscriptions started inside the iOS app carry an extra fee of about 30% that Apple takes. Check the current rules on Discord's Creator Portal and in the Discord Help Centre before you build your pricing around any of these figures.

If you are outside the US, use an external membership platform instead. Patreon, Whop and Ko-fi all work with Discord through role sync, which means a paying member automatically gets the paid role in your server. Compare the fees before you choose. Whop handles checkout, role assignment, refunds and tax paperwork in one place. Patreon suits creators who already have patrons elsewhere.

Set one to three tiers, not five: a low tier for supporters, a middle tier for the full offer, and a high tier for direct access to you, such as a monthly one-to-one call. Price the middle tier first, because most members will choose it. A common starting point is $5 to $10 a month, with the low tier at $3 and the high tier at $25 or more. Raise prices only after members stay and renew.

Step 4: Add a second income stream

Once the membership is running, add one more stream that fits your niche. Pick the one that matches what your members already ask you for.

Sell digital products. If your server is about design, sell presets, templates or icon packs. If it is about music, sell sample packs. If it is about study, sell revision guides. Sell through a simple store such as Gumroad, and announce new products in the server first. Only sell products you made and would use yourself.

Offer coaching or services. If members ask you to review their work, that is a service waiting to be priced. Offer one-to-one sessions, portfolio reviews, or fixed-price help, and keep a booking link and a clear price in a dedicated channel. Take sponsorships. Once your server passes about 2,000 engaged members, brands may pay for a sponsored channel, an announcement, or a branded event. Price on engagement, not member count, and be honest with members about what is paid.

Use affiliate links carefully. Recommend tools you genuinely use, and say clearly when a link earns you a commission. Members will ask follow-up questions no landing page can answer, so only recommend products you know well.

Step 5: Grow to 200 to 500 free members first

Do not launch a paid tier into an empty server. Aim for 200 to 500 free members before charging. At that size the server feels alive, and a small share of members will convert to paying members.

Bring members in from outside Discord, because Discord itself will not send them to you. Post useful content on YouTube, Instagram, TikTok or a blog, and link to the server at the end. List the server on community listing sites for your niche, and partner with a creator in a nearby niche for a joint event. Ask happy members to invite friends, and reward them with a role, not with cash.

Do not spam invite links in other people's servers. It destroys trust and can get your account banned. Join a few communities in your niche, be helpful there, and let people find your server through your profile.

A realistic conversion rate is 2 to 5% of free members in the first months. That means 500 free members may bring 10 to 25 paying members, or about $100 a month at $5 each before fees. That is a real start, and it grows as the server grows.

What it costs to run a monetised server

The honest costs are time and tools, not Discord itself, because creating a server is free.

Cost Typical amount Notes
Your time 5 to 15 hours a week Posting, answering questions, running events
Moderator time Unpaid at first, then $50 to $300 a month each Volunteers burn out past a few thousand members
Bots $5 to $30 a month each Moderation, ticketing and role bots add up
Membership platform fees 5 to 10% plus payment processing Only if you use Patreon, Whop or Ko-fi
Discord's cut 10% of Server Subscriptions Plus about 30% extra on iOS purchases, taken by Apple
Design and tools $0 to $50 a month Canva, a domain, or a simple website

Do the maths before you celebrate. A server earning $300 a month, paying two moderators and three bot subscriptions, is running at a loss. Either raise revenue per member or keep the operation small enough to run yourself.

Worked example: what a small server could earn

Example only: these numbers are not typical results, and they are shown only to explain how the maths works.

Imagine a study server for GCSE maths with 600 free members after eight months. About 4% of them pay, which is 24 paying members at $6 a month, so the server takes in $144 a month. Discord keeps 10%, which is $14.40, and payment processing takes a small cut on top, so the owner keeps roughly $125 a month from subscriptions.

The owner also sells a revision guide for $12, about 5 copies a month, which adds $60. One parent books a one-to-one session each month at $40. The total is about $225 a month, minus $15 for two bots, so the net is about $210 a month for roughly 8 hours of work a week. That is a realistic shape for a small server: modest membership income plus smaller streams that add up.

Common mistakes

  • Launching a paid tier into an empty server, so nobody is there to buy it.
  • Putting old free channels behind a paywall, which members read as a betrayal. Add new paid value instead.
  • Setting five tiers with confusing perks, when three clear tiers would sell better.
  • Posting nothing useful for free, so new members see a sales page and leave.

Risks and scams to watch for

The most common scam is the fake Nitro giveaway, where a bot or a direct message promises free Nitro if you click a link and log in. That link steals your account. Never click a Nitro link from a stranger, and never enter your Discord login on a site that is not discord.com.

Fake sponsorships are common too. A brand account messages you offering a generous deal, then asks you to pay for the product first. A real sponsor pays you or sends the product free. Never pay to join a sponsorship, a network, or a "creator programme" that charges a fee.

Grey-market game keys are another trap. Selling keys is legitimate when they come from developers or authorised resellers. Cheap keys from unofficial sources lead to chargebacks, revoked games and angry members. If a deal looks too cheap to be real, it is.

Your server is not yours. Discord can suspend it or change the monetisation terms, and you have little appeal. The fix is to build a list you own outside Discord, such as an email list, so the community also exists somewhere you control. Tax rules apply to Discord income too, and they differ by country. Frequently asked questions

Can you make money on Discord?

Yes, but Discord does not pay you for views or messages. You earn from your members through paid subscriptions, digital products, coaching, events, sponsorships and affiliate links. Build engagement first and add payment later.

How do Discord Server Subscriptions work?

Server Subscriptions are Discord's built-in paid memberships. You create one to three monthly tiers, and members who subscribe get a role that unlocks private channels and perks. Discord handles billing and keeps 10% of the revenue. The owner must be based in the United States with US bank details, be 18 or older, and have two-factor authentication on.

How many members do you need to monetise Discord?

There is no single official number, but most working servers launch paid tiers after 200 to 500 free members. Smaller servers can still earn from coaching or digital products, which need only a few buyers.

Is it free to start a Discord server?

Yes. Creating a server costs nothing. The costs come later, from your time, bots, moderators and payment fees. Start with no spending and add paid tools only after the server starts to earn.

Can you make money on Discord without a large server?

Yes, if your niche is valuable. A server with 100 members in a high-value niche, such as B2B software or finance, can earn more than a server with 5,000 casual members. Coaching and digital products work with small audiences, while sponsorships need a larger one.

Conclusion

Learning how to make money on Discord in 2026 comes down to building a community people value, then charging for the extra value, not for what was already free. Pick one clear niche, set up a server with a useful free side and a paid side, and turn on Server Subscriptions if you are eligible, or use an external platform if you are not. Grow to a few hundred free members first, and build an email list so the community exists somewhere you own too.
