How to Get Brand Deals as a Small Creator in 2026

Small creators win brand deals on fit and trust, not follower count. This guide walks the process: media kit, target list, pitch, pricing, and repeat deals.

Brands do not buy follower counts. They buy fit, trust, proof, and reliability. A small creator whose audience matches a brand's customers, whose content looks professional, and who delivers on time is often a better deal partner than a large creator with a mismatched audience. That is why creators with a few thousand engaged followers land paid deals every week, while larger accounts wait for inbound offers that never come.

This article teaches the deal process only, across platforms, for sponsored posts to your own audience. It assumes you have already chosen sponsored posts as your model. If you have not, read the cross-platform guide to turning a small following into income first. It does not re-explain Instagram thresholds or general income models; the Instagram specific guide covers those in the companion article on making money on Instagram with a small account.

One boundary must be clear from the start. This article is about selling a sponsored post to your own audience. The companion guide to making money as a UGC creator is about selling content files the brand publishes, where no audience is sold and often nothing is posted by the creator. The two jobs are different products with different prices. If a brand asks for both, quote them as two separate lines. Never let a UGC fee quietly become a sponsored post, or a sponsored post fee quietly include unlimited content use.

Results vary with niche, audience quality, outreach volume, time, and market demand. Rates in this guide are reported ranges from creator guides, not guarantees, and they vary by deliverables, usage, exclusivity, and audience. Treat every figure as a planning aid, and price from your own proof.

What brands actually buy from a small creator

Brands buy four things. Fit means your niche, values, and audience match their customers. Trust means your followers believe your recommendations, because you promote selectively and disclose honestly. Proof means past content, engagement quality, and any prior results you can show honestly. Reliability means you reply promptly, meet deadlines, follow the brief, and report results without being chased.

Audit yourself against the brand scorecard

Before you pitch, score yourself honestly on the seven things brands check: niche fit, engagement quality, audience match, content quality, consistency, reliability, and proof. For each one, write one sentence of evidence. Niche fit: one sentence describing your niche and ideal follower. Engagement quality: your average saves and comments on recent posts, not just likes. Audience match: location and age ranges from analytics, where available. Content quality: your three best posts, pinned and ready. Consistency: how often you post and for how long. Reliability: any past deadlines met and testimonials you can share with permission. Proof: any past brand work, affiliate results, or product sales you can report honestly.

If any score is weak, fix it before a big pitch push. Weak content quality means two weeks of focused improvement, not more pitches. Weak proof means a small gifted or low fee job done excellently, or strong UGC work you can reference, with permission. Weak consistency means a realistic posting rhythm you can sustain. Brands forgive small numbers. They rarely forgive sloppy profiles, silent comments, or inconsistent posting, because those predict a difficult partnership.

Make your profile brand ready

Your profile is your shop window. Your bio should say who you help, what you create, and how brands can contact you, in two short lines. Add a business or creator contact email, not only a direct message link, because brand managers often work from email. Pin three posts: your best tutorial or value post, your best performing post in your niche, and a post that shows your personality or process. A brand should understand your niche within ten seconds of opening your profile.

Build a one page media kit

A media kit is a one page summary a brand can forward to a decision maker. Keep it to one page, update it monthly, and date it. Include: your name and niche in one line, follower counts by platform, engagement rates and average views for recent content, audience location and age ranges where available, past brand work or relevant proof (with permission), content formats you offer, starting prices or a price range, and contact details. Add two or three small content thumbnails that show your style.

Build a target list of 30 brands that can say yes

Do not pitch the biggest brands first. Pitch brands that can say yes to a small creator: direct to consumer brands, local businesses, startups, and brands that already sponsor creators your size. Find them by watching who sponsors similar sized creators in your niche, by searching platform ads in your niche, and by browsing brand ambassador and creator pages. Note each brand's products, recent creator content, and whether its audience matches yours.

Build a list of 30 brands with four columns: brand, product fit in one line, contact route, and status. Contact routes include a partnerships email, a creator application page, or a platform marketplace such as a creator marketplace or a collaboration platform. Avoid scraping private emails or messaging personal accounts; use public business contacts and official application routes. If you cannot find a legitimate contact route, skip the brand and note why. Respectable outreach starts with respecting how a brand asks to be contacted.

Write and send the pitch

A good pitch is under 150 words, personal, and easy to answer. Structure it in four parts: one line proving you know the brand, one line about you and your audience fit, one concrete idea, and one simple next step. Attach or link your media kit, and link one or two relevant posts, not your whole feed. Personalisation means naming a real product and a real observation, not writing "I love your brand" to fifty brands.

Worked template: "Hello, I am [name], a [niche] creator with [follower count] engaged followers, mostly [audience note]. I noticed your [product] and how your recent creator videos focus on [observation]. I would love to create a [format] showing [concrete idea] for your [audience benefit]. My media kit is linked here, with recent work in this niche. Would you be open to a short brief and rate card?" Adapt every bracket. A template with brackets left in is worse than no pitch at all.

Follow up and track every pitch

Follow up once, five to seven days after the first pitch, with a short note and one new angle, such as a fresh post link or a seasonal idea. If there is still no reply, move on and mark the brand for a later round in a few months, with new proof. Do not follow up three or four times. Persistence is a virtue in outreach; pestering is not, and brand managers talk to each other.

Track every pitch in a simple pipeline sheet: brand, contact, date sent, follow up date, reply, brief received, rate quoted, deal status, delivery date, and payment status. Review the sheet weekly. Patterns appear quickly: one niche replies more, one pitch opening works better, one price point stalls deals. Adjust from the sheet, not from feelings. A pipeline also prevents the most embarrassing small creator mistake: pitching the same brand twice with different prices, or forgetting a brand that replied yes.

Price the deal properly

Price from deliverables first, then add usage, exclusivity, and revisions. Deliverables are what you create: one Reel, a Reel plus Story set, a carousel, a video, or a series. Usage is where and how long the brand can use your content beyond your own post, such as in its ads or on its website. Exclusivity is a promise not to work with competitors for a period. Revisions are changes after delivery. Each element has a price, and bundling them into one vague fee usually undercharges you.

Element Price it separately
Deliverables Per post or set
Usage By channel and months
Exclusivity By competitor scope and months
Revisions Included up to 2 rounds, then per round

Negotiation replies to keep ready: for a low offer, thank the brand, restate the scope the fee covers, and offer a reduced scope at that fee. For extra usage, quote the usage fee separately and state the duration and channels. For exclusivity, quote it separately by competitor scope and months, and never agree to open ended exclusivity. For a rushed deadline, quote a rush fee or a realistic later date. For payment terms, ask for payment within 14 to 30 days of delivery, and consider a deposit for larger deals or new clients. Get all of it in writing: brief, deliverables, timeline, revisions, usage, exclusivity, payment amount and date, and disclosure requirements. Deliver, disclose, and report results to win the second deal

Deliver on time, follow the brief, and communicate early if anything slips. Send files or publish at the agreed time, use the paid partnership label and any disclosure wording agreed with the brand, and keep copies of the published post and its early performance. If a post underperforms, say so honestly and note what you learned. Brands trust creators who report truthfully far more than creators who only share wins.

After the post has run its agreed period, send a short report: reach, engagement, saves, link clicks or code uses where available, audience comments themes, and one suggestion for next time. Keep it to one page. This report is the bridge to repeat work: it proves reliability, gives the brand numbers to justify a second booking, and opens a natural conversation about an ambassador program or a series. Propose the next step explicitly, such as a second post next month or a three post series, rather than waiting and hoping the brand returns.

When to accept gifted product, and when to decline

Gifted product can be worth accepting when you genuinely want the product, the brand fits your niche, and the work builds proof you lack, such as your first deal in a new niche. It is not worth accepting when the brand demands full deliverables, paid advertising usage, and exclusivity for product alone, or when the product has no value to you. A fair middle ground is gifted product plus a reduced fee for a first collaboration, with full rates from the second job.

Frequently asked questions

How many followers do you need for a brand deal?

There is no single threshold. Small brands regularly work with creators under 5000 followers when fit and engagement are strong. Focus on audience match, content quality, and proof. A targeted pitch to the right brand beats waiting for a magic follower number.

What should a brand pitch include?

One line proving you know the brand, one line about your audience fit, one concrete content idea, a media kit link, and one simple next step, all under 150 words. Personalise every pitch. Generic pitches are easy to spot and easy to ignore.

How much should a small creator charge?

Price from deliverables, then add usage, exclusivity, revisions, and rush fees separately. Creator guides report broad ranges, but no range is a guarantee. Price from your engagement, niche, and proof, offer a smaller scope for a lower budget, and get terms in writing before filming.

When should I accept free product?

Accept gifted product when you want the product, the brand fits, and the work builds proof you lack. Decline when the brand demands full scope, advertising usage, or exclusivity for product alone, or when the fit or claims are wrong. Offer a reduced fee plus product for a first job, with full rates after.

How do I turn one deal into repeat work?

Deliver on time, disclose properly, and send a short results report with an honest summary and a proposal for next time. A one page report plus a concrete next offer, such as a series or ambassador term, turns a one off post into a relationship.

Conclusion

Brand deals for small creators are won by process, not by waiting. Audit yourself honestly, build a one page media kit with dated numbers, list 30 brands that can say yes, pitch with a concrete idea, price usage and exclusivity separately, and report results that earn the second deal. Your next step is the target list: write down 30 fitting brands today, pick the best ten, and send your first five pitches this week. One well delivered small deal, reported honestly, is the proof that unlocks the next ten.
