How to Make Money on Threads in 2026

Learning how to make money on Threads starts with an honest fact. Threads has no payout button for most creators. You can post every day, grow a following, and still earn nothing from the app itself. The money comes from what your audience does after they read your posts, not from the posts themselves.

This guide is for creators and small business owners who want a realistic way to earn from Threads. You will learn the four routes that earn money, how brand deals work, and how to test one route for 30 days.

Does Threads pay creators directly?

The short answer is no, not for most people. Threads has no general creator fund, no ad revenue share for creators, no tipping feature, and no open payout programme as of November 2026. Meta did test a bonus programme with a small group of selected creators, but that programme was invite only and it wound down around April 2025. You cannot apply to join it now.

Threads ads went global in January 2026, but those ads pay Meta, not the people who post, and text posts carry no pre-roll or mid-roll ads.

How money actually works on Threads

Money on Threads comes through four routes. Each one suits a different kind of creator, and each one has a different speed and a different risk. The table below compares them, so you can see the trade-offs before you pick one.

Route What you earn from Speed to first money Control Main risk
Affiliate links Commission when a reader buys through your link Days to weeks Low, the product and the rate belong to someone else Links can hurt reach if you use them in every post
Your own products Sales of a guide, template, or course you made Weeks to months High, you set the price and keep the sale You must make something people want to buy
Email list Sales and bookings from readers who join your list Months High, the list is yours even if your account is lost Slow to build, and it needs regular useful emails
Services and client work Fees for work a reader hires you to do Days to weeks Medium, each client sets the scope Your income stops when you stop working

Route 1: Affiliate links

Affiliate marketing means you share a link to a product, and you earn a commission when someone buys through it. An affiliate commission is a small percentage of the sale, paid by the seller to you. On Threads, this works best when the product solves a problem your posts already talk about.

Do not put a link in every post. Threads readers scroll fast, and a feed full of links looks like a shop, not a conversation. A better pattern is to post useful advice most of the time, and put the link in a reply to your own post, or in your bio, when it fits. Pick products you have used yourself, or can check properly.

You must also disclose the link. If you earn a commission, say so in plain words in the post itself, such as "This is an affiliate link, so I may earn a small commission if you buy." The Federal Trade Commission explains this rule in its Endorsement Guides FAQ: endorsements must be honest and not misleading, and any material connection must be disclosed clearly. The Guides were revised in 2023. For the full method, read how to start affiliate marketing.

Route 2: Your own products

Selling your own product gives you the most control, because you set the price and keep the sale. The product does not need to be big. A short guide, a set of templates, or a simple checklist can be enough, if it solves one clear problem for your readers.

Start with the problem your posts already answer. If people often ask how you plan your week, a weekly planning template is a natural first product. Price it low at first, so the first buyers can try it easily, and ask them what was missing, so the next version is better. Before you build a product, read how to create your first digital product.

Example only: these numbers are not typical results. Imagine a creator with 2,000 followers who sells a 12 pound planning template pack. In one week, 60 people click the link in her bio, and 3 percent of them buy. That is 60 times 0.03, which is about 2 sales, and 2 times 12 pounds, which is about 24 pounds for the week. The point of the example is not the amount. It is that a small audience can produce a small number of sales, and the maths is simple enough to check. Your own numbers will differ, and they may be zero at first.

Route 3: Email list growth

An email list is a list of readers who gave you their email address and agreed to hear from you. It is the most durable route on Threads, because the list is yours. If your Threads account is suspended, or the algorithm stops showing your posts, the list still works. No other route on this page gives you that safety.

Grow the list with a free offer, such as a short guide, a checklist, or a template. Put the link in your bio, and mention the offer in your posts when it fits the topic. A post that solves a small problem, then says "I made a free checklist for this, and the link is in my bio", gives readers a clear reason to sign up.

Route 4: Services and client work

Services mean you sell your time and skill directly, such as writing, design, coaching, or social media help. Threads suits this route well, because short posts let you think out loud about your work. A writer who posts short lessons about clear writing will attract readers who need a writer.

The trade-off is time. Each hour you sell is an hour you cannot sell again, and your income stops when you stop working. That is normal for services, and it is why many creators use services to earn first, then build a product later, when they have steady income. Post about problems you can solve, and end with a clear next step, such as "Send me a message if you want help with this."

Brand deals on Threads

A brand deal is an agreement where a brand pays you to post about its product. On Threads, brand deals are usually bundled into a wider Instagram deal, with a Threads post added on, rather than a standalone Threads campaign. That is because most brands plan Instagram first, and Threads is still a smaller part of their budget.

One guide reports that a Threads post is often priced as a 10 to 20 percent add-on to the Instagram fee, not as a doubling of the fee. Treat that as a starting point for your own pricing, not as a market rate, because rates differ by niche, audience size, and the brand. Brands that find you on Instagram may add Threads posts to the same deal, so keep your Instagram profile active and consistent with your Threads posts.

You should also know about the paid partnership label. This is a label that marks a post as a paid partnership with a brand. Meta's own Threads help pages have given conflicting answers about whether the tool exists on Threads, so check the current help pages before you rely on it. Whatever the tool does, the safe rule is simple: disclose the partnership in the post text itself, in plain words, such as "Paid partnership with [brand name]." For the video side of Instagram, read how to use Instagram Reels to earn money.

Which route to pick first

Pick the route that fits what you already have. Answer these four questions honestly, and the choice will be clear.

  • Do you already have a skill clients pay for, such as writing or design? Pick services first, because it can pay fastest.
  • Do you have a product, or can you make a small one in a week? Pick your own products.
  • Do you have an audience that trusts you, but nothing to sell yet? Pick affiliate links, with one product you use yourself.
  • Do you have time, but no audience and no product yet? Pick the email list, and build it slowly while you post useful content.

What does not pay

Some tactics look like shortcuts, but they waste time and can hurt your account. Follow-for-follow means you follow people only so they follow you back. It fills your feed with people you do not care about, and most of them leave. Engagement pods are groups where members agree to like and reply to each other's posts. They can inflate your numbers for a while, but the replies are not from real readers, and brands can spot the pattern. Bought followers are worse: they are not real people, they never buy anything, and they make your engagement rate look weak to brands.

A 30-day test plan for one route

Pick one route, then test it for 30 days. The plan below keeps the test small and gives you a clear decision at the end.

Week Action What to keep as evidence
Week 1 Pick your route and set up the next step, such as a bio link or a simple product page A working link a reader can click
Week 2 Post five useful posts about the problem your route solves, and mention the next step twice Post views, replies, and link clicks
Week 3 Post five more useful posts, and answer every reply that asks a buying question The questions readers ask, in their own words
Week 4 Review the numbers and decide: keep the route, change the offer, or try a different route A short note on what worked and what did not

Use this rule of thumb to read the results. If you get fewer than 20 link clicks in a week, your posts are probably not reaching the right people yet, so work on the topic and the wording. If you get 100 clicks and no sales or sign-ups, the offer is probably wrong, so change the product or the free offer, not the posts.

What it costs and what to expect

Threads itself is free to use. The costs come from the tools around your route. An email tool may be free for a small list and charge as the list grows. A product needs a payment page, which may take a small fee per sale. Affiliate links cost nothing, but they pay only when someone buys. Expect a slow start. Most creators earn nothing in the first month, so allow 30 days to test one route and 3 to 6 months to judge the result.

Common mistakes

  • Putting a link in every post, so readers stop reading and the feed looks like a shop.
  • Having no clear next step, so a reader who likes your post has nowhere to go.
  • Switching routes every week, so no route gets a fair test.
  • Hiding an affiliate link, instead of disclosing it in plain words.
  • Measuring only likes, and ignoring clicks, sign-ups, and sales.
  • Copying another creator's posts, instead of writing from your own work and knowledge.

Risks and scams to watch for

Be careful with anyone who offers to grow your Threads account for a fee, or promises a payout programme you can join for payment. There is no public Threads payout programme to join, and no one can sell you a place in one. A genuine brand deal will come with a clear brief, a named brand, and a contract, not a message that asks you to pay first.

Frequently asked questions

Does Threads pay creators?

No, not as a general rule. Threads has no creator fund, no ad revenue share, and no open payout programme as of November 2026. Creators earn indirectly, through brand deals, affiliate links, products, and services.

How many followers do you need to make money on Threads?

There is no official follower threshold, because Threads does not pay creators directly. Some guides mention 1,000 followers as a common benchmark, but that is not an official rule. What matters is whether followers trust you and click your links.

Can you do affiliate marketing on Threads?

Yes. Share affiliate links in your bio and in replies to your own posts. Disclose the link in plain words, only promote products you have used or checked, and keep links rare.

How long does it take to make money on Threads?

There is no fixed timeline, and most creators earn nothing in the first month. A fair test is 30 days of steady posting on one route, and 3 to 6 months before you judge whether Threads can bring regular income.

Is Threads worth it for a small business?

It can be, if your customers are on Threads and you can post useful content regularly. Use it to answer questions, share short lessons, and send readers to your shop or booking page.

Do I need an Instagram account to use Threads?

Yes, Threads is built on Instagram, and you sign in with an Instagram account. Brand deals are often bundled across Instagram and Threads, so a brand can check both profiles before it hires you.

Conclusion: build trust first, then add one route

Threads does not pay most creators directly, so do not wait for a payout programme. Build trust with useful posts, pick one route that fits what you already have, and test it for 30 days. Track clicks, sign-ups, and sales, not likes, and disclose every affiliate link and paid partnership in plain words. Google's guide on creating helpful, reliable, people-first content makes the same point: show first-hand expertise and real depth, because trust matters most.
